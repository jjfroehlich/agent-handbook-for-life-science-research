Lab manuals and shared expectations

A lab manual helps members find the group’s shared practices without having to learn every convention by watching others. Explain who is responsible for routine work and whom to ask when an arrangement does not fit.

The manual need not contain every protocol, personal agreement or university rule. Operational instructions and checklists can sit in the same document when a small group can keep it navigable; a larger group may link separate protocol, safety and data records. Choose the arrangement that lets a member find the current instruction and its owner [5].

Decide what needs documenting

List the recurring questions first. Ask current members what they had to learn by watching others, which questions receive different answers and which unstated expectations have delayed work or made them reluctant to ask for help. Review onboarding questions, repeated booking or handover problems and decisions that depend on seniority. Include technicians, managers, students, postdocs and research staff so that the manual covers the responsibilities and support each needs, including the work of maintaining shared resources.

Write down a practice when it recurs across members and knowing it in advance changes what someone does. Common candidates include communication and response times, meetings, shared equipment, routine responsibilities, time away, data and code, mistakes, feedback, joining and leaving. Choose among these topics according to the group’s work: field seasons, clinical data and shared instruments raise different questions from those of a small theoretical group. Published manuals illustrate those choices; they do not establish a universal policy.

Keep shared practices in the lab manual: routine workflow, reciprocal expectations, responsible roles and routes for questions or concerns. Keep an individual’s goals, supervision, feedback and development in a personal mentoring agreement; see mentoring. Keep employment conditions, safety rules, ethics approvals, data-management plans, access controls and collaboration-specific decisions in the responsible institutional or project record. The lab manual can link to those records and explain what members need to do.

Keeping these records separate prevents a local preference from appearing to be an institutional requirement and keeps private matters out of a group document. Mentor–mentee expectations should be tailored and revisited as the relationship changes [4].

Decide which material can be public, which belongs on an internal group site and which should remain in an access-controlled project or institutional system. Prospective members can benefit from seeing genuine expectations. Keep sensitive contact details, restricted data procedures and individual accommodations in the appropriate protected record.

Writing the policies

A value such as “respect people’s time” can introduce a section, but it does not settle a booking clash. State when the policy applies, what people should normally do and who is responsible. Then explain what changes in a foreseeable exception and where someone can take a question or concern. Name who keeps the policy current. These points can fit into a short paragraph; they do not each need a separate heading.

Use reciprocal language where responsibility is reciprocal. If members should flag a delayed analysis, state when and to whom; if the project lead promises a response or decision, state that too. Include a route that does not depend solely on the person whose conduct might be questioned. A manual can name an institutional ombud, graduate programme, staff representative or reporting office using the organization’s current terminology. It should not promise confidentiality, remedies or authority that the group cannot provide.

The SAFE Labs authors similarly turn broad aspirations into actions that can be documented while leaving groups to choose policies suited to their setting [3].

Example: a shared-equipment policy

Northbank Imaging Group manual — shared microscope bookings, version 1.1

Finish at the booked end time whenever possible. If an acquisition is likely to run over, the current user updates the calendar and contacts the next user as soon as they know. The next user decides whether moving their start is workable; silence does not mean agreement. If the users cannot agree, each tells the lab manager how time-critical the session is, what preparation is already committed and whether the work can be repeated. The manager decides, records the reason and arranges a replacement slot when one is needed. If the manager is involved in the clash, the facility director makes the booking decision. Concerns about unfair treatment can also go to the institution’s staff-advice office.

Live-cell acquisitions may need a longer window because the useful endpoint depends on cell behaviour. Label this when booking and include a realistic buffer. This exception does not change institutional access or lone-working rules.

The current user remains responsible for the acquisition log, shutdown or handover and cleaning. The lab manager owns the booking policy. Repeated overruns or an exception that the policy does not handle trigger an earlier review; otherwise the next review is 1 March 2027.

This is one fictional group’s arrangement. Another facility could reserve fixed changeover periods, assign priority by study stage or prohibit overruns.

Draft the manual with the people affected

The person accountable for the group should first state the constraints and promises they can actually uphold. Then collect cases from the people affected. A meeting can expose differing interpretations; a short form or private route lets members raise concerns they may not voice in front of a supervisor. Asking members to contribute does not transfer the leader’s responsibility for employment, safety or fair treatment.

Scale the drafting process to the group. A small lab may revise a short draft together. A larger group can assign topic teams, exchange drafts and use several meetings to resolve inconsistent language. The WIN Physics group used volunteer teams, cross-review and advice from departmental and human-resources specialists for its multi-PI group of about 30 people [6].

For each section, compare an ordinary case with an awkward one. Check whether the responsible person has the authority and capacity to act and whether the exception is clear. Ask the appropriate institutional office to review policy-sensitive sections. The responsible leader then confirms what the group can adopt. Record unresolved concerns and keep a route outside the direct supervisor for a member who cannot safely resolve a matter within the group.

Keep the first version short enough to finish. Mehr selected topics around his group’s asynchronous collaborations, left specialist platform detail elsewhere and observed which sections members ignored or could not understand [5].

Introduce, use and revise it

Publish a version, effective date and named owner. Walk through the document with current members, then discuss the relevant sections with a prospective or new member rather than sending a link without context. An acknowledgement can record that the person received the current version and had a chance to ask questions. It should not be presented as blanket consent, a waiver of rights or proof that every expectation is fair.

Refer to the manual when the described situation occurs. If practice differs from the text, either correct the practice or revise the document. Mariam Aly describes using her manual during onboarding, holding herself to its promises and sharing maintenance of a separate practical wiki [1].

Review the manual on a set date, and revise it sooner after a new instrument, changed institutional rule, repeated exception or unclear responsibility. Communicate material changes directly and keep a short change log so that members can tell which expectation applied when. A review of how the group works can reveal when the practice itself needs to change before its description is updated.

Example: adopting and revising the booking policy

The Northbank lab manager begins with two recurring cases: unannounced overruns and unrealistically short bookings made to secure scarce time. Current users discuss both at a lab meeting and can add examples through an anonymous form. The manager drafts two options, checks the facility’s access rules and asks the affected users to test each option against a normal fixed-sample session and a variable live-cell acquisition.

The group leader confirms which arrangement the lab can support. The manager publishes version 1.0, explains it at a meeting and includes the booking section in onboarding. Members are asked to report unclear cases to the manager; a person concerned about unfair treatment can also use the institution’s staff or student route.

During the six-week trial, members interpret “a short shift” differently. Version 1.1 removes that phrase: affected users must agree directly, and the lab manager decides when they cannot. The change log records the reason. The next review is scheduled for six months later, with an earlier review if the same exception recurs.

Data and AI practices

The data section should help a member find an approved place to store and work with their data and the person who can arrange access. Explain which kinds of data each environment can hold and how to record versions or changes during active work. Point to the project record used when someone joins or leaves. Keep dataset-specific retention, consent, transfer and access conditions in the data-management or ethics record that governs the project. Link to that record and explain what members need to do, rather than creating a second copy of rules that may change.

For generative AI, check the current policies that apply to the institution, funder, project partners, data and intended publication. Record the version consulted, approved tools or environments, excluded inputs, uses that require a project record or disclosure, and who verifies the output. A trainee may be able to check generated syntax while still needing to develop the judgment required to choose an analysis or interpret a result. Discuss concrete tasks before writing the rule, and connect permitted use to what the member can explain and verify [2]. Detailed questions about contribution and authorship belong in collaboration, authorship and credit.

Check whether the manual works

Give one section to a member who did not draft it. Ask them what they would do in the ordinary case, what would change in the stated exception and whom they would contact if the responsible person were involved in the concern. If those answers require unwritten knowledge, revise the section.

Check that links open, named roles still exist, public and internal material are separated, and each promised response has an owner with capacity to provide it. Compare the written rule with recent practice. Where they differ, establish whether the practice needs correcting or the manual needs updating before revising the text.
