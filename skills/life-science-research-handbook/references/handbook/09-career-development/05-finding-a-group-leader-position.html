Finding a group leader position

Leading a group changes the work as well as the job title. You would set research priorities, support people and obtain resources alongside doing science. Decide whether you want that combination, then look for positions whose research, teaching and mentoring responsibilities fit the group you intend to build. [3].

Define an independent program

Build the program around questions you intend to lead. Explain how your previous work prepares you to address them, which projects you would begin, and how their results would guide later work. Independence can come from a distinct question or approach within the same field. Discuss with your current supervisor which questions each group will pursue, what materials or data can be transferred, and where collaboration will continue. Agree who will lead the overlapping work; continued collaboration need not prevent you from developing a distinct program. [2].

Prepare a short program summary before searching widely. Include the people, facilities and funding needed to start; these needs will help you assess calls and later offers. If an essential assay can only be performed in your current lab, agree how that work could continue or develop an alternative before making it central to the proposal.

Finding suitable positions

Compare each call with your program summary. A research institute, a university department and a teaching-focused position may offer very different time, facilities and student populations. Check whether your projects can work in that setting, who you could collaborate with and how the group would be staffed. Use recent faculty appointments and conversations with colleagues to understand the local expectations. [2].

For each role, keep the advertised criteria and requested materials alongside the parts of your program that fit and the questions you still need to ask. Confirm facilities, appointment terms and local expectations with the host. A department’s scientific interests may match yours even when its available space or staffing cannot support your first projects. [2].

Prepare the faculty application

Adapt the materials to the call, using CVs, resumes, letters and references. The research statement should connect your important contributions to the program you intend to build. Write for scientists outside your specialty: explain why the question matters, how your prior work prepares you to address it, and what you will lead. Give enough experimental detail for them to assess whether the first projects are feasible. The exact balance between past and future work depends on the call. [2]; [4].

Use the letter to explain your interest in the position and the contribution you would make. In a teaching or mentoring statement, connect experience to specific plans for this setting: what you have taught, how you helped someone learn, and how you would develop that work with the students or researchers there. [2].

Test readiness with the research statement

Drafting the statement can help you judge readiness. You need both an account of completed work and credible future projects. Organize the draft around four parts:

Part What a reader should be able to find
Research trajectory How your prior contribution prepares you to address the new question, and which work belongs to the former and proposed groups.
Program question A question broad enough to matter to adjacent scientists, stated without assuming that the proposed answer is already known.
First projects What each project compares, which possible explanations it can distinguish, how the findings would guide the next experiment, and whether it can run alongside another project.
Feasibility and growth The people, facilities and access required to begin; how the first projects could be funded and how their answers create the next tractable question.

The following planning sheet uses the hypothetical epithelial-injury program introduced in Chalk talks.

Example: turn a program into a research-statement plan

Opening question and significance
How does resolved inflammation change the next epithelial injury response? Understanding whether cells retain an injury history could explain why the same tissue responds differently to a later insult.

Preparation and independent boundary
Prior work on acute inflammation supplies the biological starting point and culture experience. The former lab continues acute inflammation-signalling studies. The new group leads the epithelial re-injury comparisons and functional assays; any shared protocol or collaboration will be agreed explicitly.

First project and decision — Year 1
After removing the inflammatory cue, compare exposed and control airway organoids. Measure cell composition, expression and accessibility to assess whether a retained difference occurs within the same cell types or reflects a changed mixture of cells. These comparisons guide which explanation to investigate further; the re-injury project tests whether exposure history changes function.

Parallel project and dependency — Months 9–24
Re-injure matched organoids and measure barrier repair and inflammatory output. This can begin alongside the first project. A mechanistic project starts only after a reproducible functional difference is established.

Conditions for starting
Confirm access to primary airway organoid cultures, a cue-clearance assay, sufficient single-cell facility capacity and a first researcher able to run the functional assay. State an alternative analysis or facility route if the preferred access is unavailable.

Give the draft to readers with different roles: one who knows the field, one who can assess a related scientific question, and one who understands the prospective setting. Ask them to mark the first point where they lose the question, cannot distinguish the projects, or cannot see how the work starts. Revise the plan behind the prose when their answer exposes a missing comparison, dependency or boundary.

Share the program summary and application materials with your referees, including the agreed boundary with your current lab. See reference requests for coordinating their submissions.

Prepare for the interview

Before a visit or remote interview, ask for the itinerary, audiences and presentation formats. Read recent work by the people you will meet. Prepare concise explanations of your previous contributions, initial projects, independence, possible funding routes and teaching experience. Discuss genuine scientific connections with prospective colleagues, and use meetings with recent hires to ask how the department supports a new group. [2]; [4].

Match questions to the person who can answer them. A facility director can explain capacity, fees and sample preparation; a department chair can clarify teaching, review and space commitments; a recent hire can describe what support was available in practice; trainees can describe supervision and local research life. Record the answer, the person who gave it and what still needs written confirmation. For a resource your first project depends on, ask when you could use it and what would have to be arranged first.

For the job talk, use Explaining research to an audience, Designing a presentation and Delivering a talk and answering questions to develop and rehearse a scientific explanation. For a chalk talk, use the board-map method: show the program overview, a comparison that distinguishes your proposed explanations, and how different findings would change the next experiment. Rehearse interruptions and returning to the map.

Comparing and negotiating offers

Once an offer exists, cost the first projects and compare their needs with the proposed support. Include staffing and running costs as well as equipment and space. Check when the resources become available and what restrictions apply: do salary amounts include benefits, for example, and must startup funds be spent by a particular date? Also discuss the teaching and service expected while you establish the group. Use these details to explain each request. [1]; [4].

For the proposed epithelial program, access to an established single-cell facility could reduce the need to purchase equipment. Check its capacity, charges and sample-preparation support before removing those items from the budget. Airway-organoid culture, cue-clearance measurements, staffing and facility access must also be available when each project is due to begin.

Use the same categories to compare offers, including the start date, teaching load, appointment or review timetable and personal constraints. Prioritize the requests that determine whether you can establish the program. Discuss alternatives when an item is unavailable—for example, funded facility access instead of equipment—and ask for the agreed resources and conditions in writing. Seek advice from recent hires and an experienced colleague who understands the local appointment system. [1].

For the hypothetical epithelial program, a request sheet could look like this:

Program need What to establish from the offer Acceptable alternative or follow-up
Airway-organoid culture Suitable space, access date and responsibility for any preparation Temporary shared space with a written date for the permanent arrangement
Single-cell analysis Facility capacity, access charges, sample-preparation support and booking lead time Another facility only if its cost and turnaround fit the first-project schedule
First researcher Amount, duration, eligible role and whether salary costs include benefits A different staffing mix that still supplies the required assay expertise
Time to establish the group First-year teaching and service assignments A specified lighter schedule or delayed course responsibility, where the setting permits
Startup funds Included cost categories, purchasing process and any spending deadline Written extension or staged funds when procurement or hiring will outlast the initial period

The written follow-up should lead with appreciation, then separate essential clarifications from ranked requests. Each request should name the work it enables. Avoid copying another applicant’s list: equipment access, teaching arrangements, appointment terms and negotiable items vary by institution and jurisdiction.
