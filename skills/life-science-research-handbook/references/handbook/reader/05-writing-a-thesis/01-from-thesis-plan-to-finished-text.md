# From thesis plan to finished text

A thesis explains the contribution of your research. Published papers may supply much of the material; the thesis also needs to explain how the projects relate and what their findings establish together.

<a id="thesis-scope"></a>

## Choose the thesis scope and structure

<a id="thesis-local-expectations"></a>**Agree the local expectations.**

Thesis format and length vary between countries, universities and programmes, and with supervisors’ expectations. Check the current requirements and look at recent accepted theses. Before substantial drafting, agree a provisional contents page and length with supervisors, including how much background and general discussion they expect. If you plan to include papers, check the conditions for doing so. [[8]](#ref-thesis-uclthesisformat).

<a id="thesis-length"></a>**Plan the length as well as the contents.**

Check which components count toward the word limit. [[7]](#ref-thesis-cambridgethesislimits). Agree provisional lengths for the chapters, allowing more space for those that need fuller explanation. Page counts are harder to compare because figures, spacing and appended papers affect them. Revisit the estimates after drafting a representative chapter.

<a id="thesis-question"></a>**State the thesis question and contribution.**

Write a short statement of what the thesis investigates and what the completed work adds. If the projects do not answer one question, explain the topic or approach that connects them.

Identify how the projects relate. For example, one may test an explanation suggested by another, or provide a method used in a later study. Projects can also examine different aspects of the same problem. An unfinished project can belong if its available findings contribute to the thesis; explain what those findings establish and what remains unresolved.

Develop the agreed contents into headings, figure and table titles, with notes about what each chapter will explain. [[1, chs. 19–20, pp. 143–159]](#ref-thesis-goslingnoordam2022thesis).

<a id="thesis-format"></a>**Select a format that the programme permits.**

Three useful starting patterns are:

**A continuous monograph.** An introduction establishes the problem, a literature review develops the background, and methods, results and discussion build one sustained account. The review may sit within the introduction, and results and discussion may be combined. This arrangement works when the studies are best understood as parts of one argument. [[5]](#ref-thesis-monashthesisstructure).

**A monograph organized around studies or themes.** A general introduction and, where useful, shared methods precede several research chapters. Each chapter introduces its own question, explains its methods, presents findings and discusses them. A general discussion then brings the studies together. This is a useful option for several related projects, including work that has not become a paper. Whether that work is sufficient for the degree remains a local academic judgment.

**An article-based, compilation or cumulative thesis.** Papers form part of a larger account that explains their shared background, aims and combined contribution. Connecting chapters may surround the articles, or an integrative account may precede the collected papers. The arrangement and terminology vary, but the intellectual task is similar: explain how the studies fit together and what they establish beyond their individual conclusions. The integrating text needs its own critical argument, even when the papers are already published. [[9]](#ref-thesis-kithesiscompilation).

Front matter usually includes the title, declarations, abstract and contents; references and appendices complete the document. Their required order, additional summaries and any separate impact statement are local choices or requirements. A hybrid structure can be sensible where permitted, but agree its logic before drafting.

Check which parts of published papers you may edit for the thesis. Where changes are permitted, adapt the opening to explain the paper’s place in the thesis, update references and remove repeated overview figures that add no new information. Shared methods can reduce repetition, but each chapter still needs enough context to understand its experiments and assess its findings. [[1, ch. 20, pp. 155–157]](#ref-thesis-goslingnoordam2022thesis).

<a id="thesis-map"></a>

## Map the completed projects

<a id="thesis-map-artifact"></a>**Map the chapters.**

Before drafting the general introduction, list each research chapter’s question, main finding and contribution to the thesis. Add the shared background and final synthesis. Check whether readers will understand why one project follows another and which findings the synthesis needs to compare.

<a id="thesis-local-context"></a>**Give chapters enough local context.**

Put broad background and shared methods in a common chapter where the format permits. Give each research chapter a short introduction to its question and enough methodological context to interpret its results. Cross-reference the common account for detail. [[1, ch. 20, pp. 155–156]](#ref-thesis-goslingnoordam2022thesis).

A schematic may need to appear again if readers use it to understand a new comparison. A general overview figure usually belongs in the introduction. Revisit the literature for paper-derived chapters: work published since submission may change how you interpret their findings.

<a id="thesis-integration"></a>

## Develop the introduction and background

<a id="thesis-general-introduction"></a>**Write an introduction for the whole thesis.**

Introduce the research area, explain the unresolved problem and state the thesis contribution early. Use the chapter map to select the background readers need before the first research chapter. Explain the scope of the work and finish with a brief account of how the chapters develop the answer. [[2]](#ref-thesis-jansen2025thesisintroduction); [[3]](#ref-thesis-lemprierethesisintroduction).

Draft a short introduction early to test the proposed structure. Keep collecting relevant literature and notes, then develop the full account once the research chapters are stable. [[1, ch. 20, p. 157]](#ref-thesis-goslingnoordam2022thesis). For project-level openings and literature transitions, see [Titles, abstracts and introductions](../04-writing-a-paper/02-titles-abstracts-and-introductions.md#opening-chapter).

<a id="thesis-opening-functions"></a>**Introduction, background, motivation and significance**

These are functions, not mandatory headings. Their order and emphasis depend on the work and the agreed format. [[6]](#ref-thesis-monashthesisintro).

**Introduction: orient the reader.** Establish the topic, question and route through the thesis. The opening may lead to a separate background chapter or incorporate the fuller literature review.

**Background or literature review: establish the basis for the question.** Explain the necessary concepts and critically compare what is known, disputed or missing. Include the breadth needed to understand the studies, while connecting each topic to a later question, design choice or interpretation.

**Motivation or rationale: explain why the investigation matters.** Explain why the question is worth answering and how your approach could help. Saying that little is known leaves the reader to work out why knowing more would be useful.

**Aims, questions or hypotheses: specify the task.** State what the thesis investigates and how the studies contribute. If a study tests a hypothesis, explain the prediction used to test it. Do not invent prior hypotheses for exploratory or resource-building work.

**Contribution and significance: preview what the thesis adds.** Name the new evidence, method, resource or understanding and why it matters. Keep this preview proportionate to the completed work; develop the interpretation in the discussion.

**Impact: explain possible or demonstrated use.** Identify who could use the work and how. Benefits may lie within research itself. Distinguish prospective uses from demonstrated effects, and place this account where it fits the thesis rather than assuming a separate statement is needed. [[10]](#ref-thesis-uclthesisimpact).

<a id="thesis-background-plan"></a>**Build the background around the thesis questions.**

Work backward from the chapter map to select the background and arrange it so that the aims follow naturally. [Comparing and synthesizing papers](../02-reading-the-literature/03-comparing-and-synthesizing-papers.md#synthesis-chapter) explains how to compare evidence and evaluate conflicting accounts.

For the fictional seedling thesis, introduce heat exposure and root development, then explain why lateral-root emergence and primary-root length are different outcomes. Review what is known about their dependence on developmental timing. Because conditions other than heat differed in the greenhouse, the second project tests heat exposure under controlled conditions. The third asks whether the response differs with the seedlings’ age. Together, the studies help identify which effect needs a mechanistic explanation. They may guide later experiments, but do not demonstrate improved crop performance.

<a id="thesis-methods-results"></a>

## Write the methods and results chapters

The principles in [Methods, results and figure legends](../04-writing-a-paper/03-methods-results-and-figure-legends.md#reporting-chapter) apply to theses too: describe what was done reproducibly, organize findings around questions and comparisons, report uncertainty, and keep prose, figures and legends consistent. A thesis gives these principles more room and may distribute them across several chapters.

**Methods explain both how and why.** Describe the procedures and analysis, including why you chose them and which assumptions or limitations affect the conclusions. Include validation or method development needed to show that a measurement can answer the research question. Common procedures can appear in a shared account; explain each study’s design and modifications in its own chapter. [[11]](#ref-thesis-monashthesismethods).

**Results establish what the evidence shows.** Introduce each question or comparison, present the finding and direct readers to its support. Include negative or inconclusive findings when they affect the answer. A thesis can accommodate fuller controls, validation and exploratory work than a short article, but each addition should serve the argument. Keep essential evidence in the main account and use appendices for supporting detail. Whether interpretation accompanies each result or follows in a separate discussion, make the distinction between observation and explanation clear.

<a id="thesis-synthesis"></a>

## Bring the findings together

Begin the final synthesis with what the thesis establishes. Use the findings from different chapters to explain that answer: one study may test an explanation suggested by another, or show that a result depends on particular conditions. Discuss disagreements and explain which conclusions remain uncertain. A reader should finish with an understanding of the combined contribution, rather than another summary of each chapter.

<a id="thesis-discussion-functions"></a>**Discussion, synthesis and implications**

A research chapter’s discussion interprets that study; the general discussion asks what the whole thesis establishes. The functions below may form sections or recur within a discussion organized by theme. Use the detailed advice in [Discussion and conclusions](../04-writing-a-paper/04-discussion-and-conclusions.md#discussion-chapter) at both scales.

**Overall answer: return to the thesis question.** State what can now be concluded, including a partial answer where necessary. Use a brief summary of the decisive findings to support it.

**Interpretation and synthesis: explain the relationships.** Show whether studies reinforce, qualify or challenge one another. Differences between systems, measurements or conditions may explain an apparent conflict. A methodological chapter may enable a later result rather than provide another test of the same claim.

**Comparison with the literature: locate the contribution.** Explain how the combined findings change, extend or support existing understanding. Discuss relevant evidence that disagrees, and distinguish what your work establishes from explanations proposed by others. [[12]](#ref-thesis-monashthesisdiscussion).

**Limitations and alternatives: explain what remains uncertain.** Identify conclusions that depend on an untested assumption or a measurement limitation, and discuss other explanations that still fit the findings. Consider weaknesses shared by the studies: repeating a result does not remove a bias present in every experiment.

**Significance and implications: explain what follows.** Develop the contribution previewed in the introduction. Separate a change in scientific understanding from a potential application, and state what further evidence the application would require.

**Future work: choose the questions that follow.** Select a few unresolved questions raised by the findings. For each, explain what the proposed experiment would help you learn.

For the fictional thesis, the central synthesis paragraph could read:

<a id="thesis-example-synthesis"></a>**Example: a general-synthesis paragraph**

Brief heat pulses reduced lateral-root emergence in sterile culture, with a stronger response to exposure early in seedling development. Primary-root length remained unchanged, suggesting that the treatment affected root branching rather than uniformly slowing root growth. These controlled experiments provide a possible explanation for the reduced emergence observed under the greenhouse heat regime, where other environmental conditions also varied. The timing result now directs attention to the developmental processes that make early seedlings more sensitive to heat exposure.

The synthesis starts with the controlled result, then uses it to interpret the greenhouse pattern. Its order differs from the chapter map because the paragraph is organized around the answer.

For a complex synthesis, use a table to compare what different projects establish about the same question, including conditions under which their findings differ.

<a id="thesis-conclusion"></a>**The conclusion**

A separate conclusion is useful after a substantial general discussion. State the overall contribution, its scope and the next question that follows from it. Develop detailed interpretations and present essential evidence in the preceding chapters. [[4]](#ref-thesis-georgemccombes2024thesisconclusion).

<a id="thesis-completion"></a>

## Plan the route to submission

<a id="thesis-backward-plan"></a>**Work backward from the submission date.**

List what already exists and what each unfinished chapter still needs. Working backward from submission, allow time for final checks and approvals, review of the complete draft, chapter revisions and any unfinished analysis or figures. Include permissions and declarations. Assign each task to a person and agree when it will be ready, leaving time for anyone whose work depends on it.

For work that may not finish, agree when to decide whether to continue and what you need to know to make that decision. Also agree what the thesis will contain if the work stops. You can then complete the document without leaving its scope dependent on an open-ended experiment.

Discuss what you would do if a critical step is delayed. You may need to narrow a claim, change the chapter order or revise the submission date. Agree the response with supervisors and update the affected chapters and schedule. [[1, ch. 19, pp. 145–150]](#ref-thesis-goslingnoordam2022thesis).

<a id="thesis-last-project"></a>**Decide what to do with the last project.**

When the last project has not yet become a paper, decide which deadline matters more. Write the paper first if competition, priority or a coauthor commitment requires rapid submission. Write the thesis chapter first if the thesis needs the fuller account and publication timing allows it. Reconsider the choice when the data, competition or graduation deadline changes. [[1, ch. 20, pp. 157–159]](#ref-thesis-goslingnoordam2022thesis).

If the selected thesis format requires accepted publications, include that dependency in the submission plan. Finishing the prose cannot substitute for a publication requirement; discuss permitted alternatives early if acceptance is uncertain.

<a id="thesis-check"></a>

## Check the complete document

Read the general introduction, research-chapter openings and final synthesis together. They should name the same question or framework, assign each chapter a distinct role and state compatible conclusions. Then inspect the rendered thesis against the current programme requirements: chapter order, cross-references, references, figure and table numbering, declarations, permissions, acknowledgements and deposit files. Return to the map if this check exposes a chapter that promises or concludes something different.

<a id="thesis-checklist"></a>**Before submitting the thesis**

- Can a reader state the thesis question or framework, its contributions and their scope from the general introduction and final synthesis?

- Have supervisors agreed the structure, background depth and provisional length within the current programme rules?

- Does the background prepare the reader for the research questions, and are motivation, significance and potential impact explained without inflated promises?

- Does every research chapter contribute a distinct finding, test or boundary to that answer?

- Do article-derived chapters give readers the local context and method detail needed to assess them?

- Does the final synthesis compare findings across projects instead of repeating chapter summaries?

- Does the completion plan include analysis, review, administrative dependencies and a decision for each critical uncertainty?

- Has the rendered document been checked against the current local submission requirements?
