# Keeping up with the literature

Choose a few ways to find papers on your current questions and wider interests. If screening new papers takes all your reading time, reduce the number of alerts or narrow their searches.

<a id="literature-scope"></a>

## Choosing what to follow

Write down the question, method or research area you currently need to follow. “Single-cell biology” is a broad interest. A more focused question is how to distinguish a change in the abundance of a cell type from a change in gene expression within that type. You can search for methods that make this distinction while still reading more widely about single-cell biology.

How closely you read depends on what you need from the paper. In a new field, an article may help you learn the vocabulary and find earlier work. If you plan to adopt its method, examine the procedure, the assumptions it relies on and the evidence that it works under conditions like yours [[1, Rule 1 and Table 1]](#ref-literature-carey2020reading).

Alerts help you notice new work. Before designing an experiment or making a claim in a manuscript, also search for earlier studies that address the question. Reviews can help you find them; read the original studies when you need to assess their methods or rely on their findings [[2, pp. 93–95]](#ref-literature-goslingnoordam2022thesis).

<a id="literature-discovery"></a>

## Finding relevant papers

A topic search can find papers across journals, but it depends on the terms you know to search for. Following a few journals or research groups may reveal work you would otherwise miss. Suggestions from colleagues and seminars can also take you beyond your usual searches [[3]](#ref-literature-ulitsky2018literature).

Start with a query that describes the biological question or method, then inspect what it returns. Try alternative terms used by authors, including names for the system, intervention or measurement. Check whether it finds a few papers you already know are relevant. If an important paper is absent, ask why: missing terminology, a narrow condition in the query or a database that does not cover the material. An experienced colleague or research librarian can help with terminology and search strategy [[2, pp. 94–95]](#ref-literature-goslingnoordam2022thesis).

Use alerts or feeds for searches and publications you want to follow, or revisit their pages regularly. Check what each subscription adds: perhaps one finds new applications of your assay while another follows a neighboring field. When several alerts return the same paper, keep one record.

When you find an important paper elsewhere, check why your searches missed it. A different term or an additional source may help. Even several well-chosen searches can miss relevant work; adding subscriptions is useful only if they help fill a gap you have identified.

<a id="literature-triage"></a>

## Choosing what to read

Use a paper’s title and abstract to decide whether it addresses a question that interests you. Look at a relevant figure or method passage if the connection is unclear. Assessing its conclusions will take closer reading.

Decide what to do with the paper:

- **Read for a current question.** Name what you need to learn. A result that could change your experimental plan, a method you may use or a paper you must explain to others warrants a focused reading slot.

- **Keep for a named later use.** Save the reason to return: background for an introduction, a method for a possible extension or a study to revisit when a particular result appears. Keep these records separate from the active reading queue.

- **Leave it.** If you cannot name a reason to return to it, you need not save it. This is a decision about your reading priorities, not a judgment of the paper’s quality.

Reading a selected paper may reveal a method or concept you need to learn first. Allow time for that background reading, and keep your note labeled as a screening note until you have examined the evidence [[1, Rule 1 and Table 1]](#ref-literature-carey2020reading). [Understanding a research paper](02-understanding-a-research-paper.md#paper-reading-chapter) explains how to follow the connection between a paper’s experiments and its conclusions.

<a id="literature-retrieval"></a>

## Saving papers and notes

Keep papers and their notes together in a searchable reference manager or document collection. Include the authors, title, year and a stable link or identifier so you can reopen the source [[2, pp. 95–97]](#ref-literature-goslingnoordam2022thesis).

For a screened paper, note why you saved it and what you have read. After closer reading, add the finding you expect to use and where its supporting evidence appears. Keep your interpretation and unanswered questions distinct from what the authors report. If you highlight a passage, add a note when you might otherwise forget why it mattered.

Use labels you are likely to remember when the paper becomes relevant again: a project question, assay, organism or unresolved problem. Before relying on the record in a new plan or manuscript, reopen the source and check the relevant passage. Check which version you retained and whether a later version or correction changes the point. When several records bear on the same question, move from individual notes to [comparing and synthesizing papers](03-comparing-and-synthesizing-papers.md#synthesis-chapter).

<a id="literature-routine"></a>

## Making time to read

Reserve recurring time for discovery and selected reading. Weekly screening is a useful starting point, with frequency adjusted to the pace of the field and the decision ahead. Ulitsky emphasizes regular screening and warns that too many subscriptions can make researchers abandon the routine altogether [[3]](#ref-literature-ulitsky2018literature).

If your reading queue keeps growing, check what is filling it. Revise searches that mostly return irrelevant papers and remove overlapping subscriptions. If the papers are relevant but you cannot read them all, choose which questions need attention now or set aside more reading time. During a busy experimental period, you may need to pause broad alerts while following work needed for an imminent decision. Return to wider interests when time allows.

<a id="literature-routine-example"></a>**Example: a routine for a method decision**

In this fictional example, a researcher is deciding whether to pilot a new cell-segmentation method on crowded fluorescence images. During assay setup, they can give literature work 90 minutes each week. They initially reserve 20 minutes for screening and 70 for selected reading and notes; these times describe their available week, not a recommended reading rate.

**Discovery:** a focused method-topic search and a broader imaging-journal stream, with occasional suggestions from colleagues.

**Current reading:** paper A describes a method intended for crowded fluorescence images. Its abstract suggests a possible fit. The researcher assigns it the reading question “Does the evaluation include image conditions similar to ours, and how were errors assessed?”

**Orientation:** review B explains segmentation-evaluation metrics. The researcher keeps it for the pilot plan, beginning with the section needed to understand paper A rather than treating the whole review as an immediate assignment.

**Later interest:** paper C concerns a different tissue. It goes into a background collection to revisit if the project expands to that tissue, outside the active method-reading queue.

**No further reading:** paper D addresses microscope calibration. It is related to imaging, but the researcher has no current calibration question and the paper does not address segmentation. They leave it outside the reading collection.

The initial note attached to A reads:

**Purpose:** decide whether this method merits a pilot on our images.

**Reading status:** abstract and one figure inspected; methods and evaluation not yet assessed.

**Next check:** read the evaluation Methods and the crowded-image example in Figure 3. Identify the reference annotation, image conditions and types of error counted. The abstract’s claimed relevance is still unverified.

The incoming alerts double during a busy assay week. The researcher removes duplicate notices and pauses the broad journal stream. They keep the method-topic search and use the reading slot to continue A, postponing optional background reading. Before committing to the pilot, they also search for earlier evaluations of the method. If understanding A requires more background reading than fits this week, they postpone the decision.

Review the routine after using it for a few weeks. Can you find a saved paper when you need it? Which alerts led to useful reading, and which mostly added notices? Adjust the searches and reading time accordingly.
