# Elevator pitches and chalk talks

A short pitch introduces your research and invites a conversation. In a chalk talk, you explain a proposed research program while the audience questions its plans and experiments.

<a id="pitch-conversation"></a>

## Elevator pitches

Think about what you would like to discuss with this person. If you want advice on a difficult experiment, introduce the problem and explain where you are stuck. If you hope to collaborate, explain how your work connects with theirs. [[2]](#ref-pitch-morganwright2021).

Start with the scientific question or finding. Introduce your role first if it helps the listener understand the connection. Give enough evidence to make the point credible, then leave room for a reply. You can explain the methods or discuss further results if they ask.

Follow the listener’s questions: they may want more technical detail or need some background first. When rehearsing, ask your partner to explain what they understood and what they thought you were asking for. Revise where their account differs from yours. [[2]](#ref-pitch-morganwright2021).

The fictional Brake study is inspired by Tang and colleagues (2024) [[5]](#ref-pitch-tang2024).

<a id="pitch-brake-example"></a>**Example: a pitch that opens a specialist conversation**

“You work on bacterial defence, so can I run a strange result past you? Brake has no conventional effector gene. Instead, an RNA template supplies DNA that encodes an antiviral protein. When we block translation, the DNA remains but phage restriction disappears; supplying the protein separately restores it. Have you seen infection-linked DNA intermediates play a similar role in other systems?”

<a id="pitch-chalk-talk"></a>

## Chalk talks

A chalk talk usually presents a proposed research program through an interactive discussion of its questions and experiments. Ask the host about the audience, duration, use of boards or slides, and expected level of detail. Prepare to explain both the first experiments and what you would do with different outcomes. [[1]](#ref-pitch-martin2017); [[3]](#ref-pitch-strassmann2016).

Begin with the overall question in terms a scientist outside your field can understand. Sketch the projects before questions take the discussion into detail, then start with the most important one. Explain who would begin the work and when, including which projects can run in parallel. [[4]](#ref-pitch-vosshall2015); [[3]](#ref-pitch-strassmann2016).

For each project, explain the question and the experiment that could answer it. Discuss what different results would lead you to do next and what resources you need to begin. Show how the program develops your own questions, identifying which work you will lead and which depends on continuing collaborations. [[1]](#ref-pitch-martin2017).

Keep an outline of the program in view as questions arrive. Write the project questions and their connections where everyone can refer to them; explain the details aloud. If a question exposes a flaw, address it and revise the plan when warranted. If discussion moves away from the program, offer to return to that question later and continue with the next project. [[3]](#ref-pitch-strassmann2016); [[4]](#ref-pitch-vosshall2015).

**↓ Continue only if the first projects establish a reproducible functional difference**

**Project 3 — What sustains that difference? Months 18–36, if warranted.** Nominate candidates from Projects 1 and 2, perturb a candidate and repeat the injury-response test.

**Board margin:** residual cue? transferable factor? changed cell composition? Write these confounds where the room can return to them. Keep access to primary cultures, the cue-clearance assay and single-cell measurements beside the project that needs each resource.

Keep the top question and the two parallel projects visible while sketching detailed comparisons below them. Discuss staffing and access alongside the experiment that depends on each resource. The conditional third project should be physically downstream, so the board does not imply that a mechanism will be pursued regardless of the first results. A clear overview matters because questions may interrupt before every experiment has been discussed. [[4]](#ref-pitch-vosshall2015); [[3]](#ref-pitch-strassmann2016).

<a id="pitch-chalk-response"></a>**Example: respond by sharpening the program**

**Question:** “How will you tell a cell-state change from residual cue or a different mixture of epithelial cells?”

**Response:** “I would first measure the residual stimulus with an assay sensitive below the concentration that affects these cells. Transferring conditioned medium to untreated cultures would test for a soluble factor, whether residual stimulus or a new secretion. We would also compare the same cell types using lineage and single-cell measurements. A difference that remains would justify testing for a persistent cell state; the re-injury experiments would establish whether the exposure history changes function.”

<a id="pitch-chalk-rehearse"></a>

### Rehearse with interruptions

Run a mock session with close scientific colleagues and people less familiar with the topic. Ask them to interrupt and challenge your reasoning, including whether the work is feasible and distinct from your former supervisor’s program. Check that they can read the board from their seats. Afterward, identify where they lost track of the program. Revise experiments that could not distinguish the proposed explanations, and be ready to explain how you would obtain the equipment or expertise they need. [[1]](#ref-pitch-martin2017); [[4]](#ref-pitch-vosshall2015).

Prepare more experimental detail than you expect to present. In a quiet session, use it to develop a comparison or discuss alternatives. In a lively session, return to the overview between exchanges so that questions about one project do not consume the whole talk.
