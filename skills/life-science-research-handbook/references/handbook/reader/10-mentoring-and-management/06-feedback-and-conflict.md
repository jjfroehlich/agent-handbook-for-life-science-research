# Feedback and conflict

A disagreement about a figure needs a different response from a dispute about working conditions. Before arranging a conversation, identify what needs to change and who can help. If speaking directly could expose someone to retaliation, seek independent advice first.

<a id="feedback-conflict-route"></a>

## Identify the issue and the right route

Separate what happened from your interpretation of why it happened. “The sample sheet arrived on Wednesday after the Tuesday deadline” identifies an event. “They do not care about the project” supplies a motive you have not established. Ask what expectation existed, who knew it, what resources were available and who had authority to change the plan. Conflict can come from overlapping responsibilities, incompatible demands or unclear decisions as well as interactions between people [[2, pp. 100–104]](#ref-feedback-conflict-bennett2018).

The following questions help choose a starting point. More than one may apply.

An unsuccessful experiment is not by itself evidence of poor performance. Discuss the quality of the reasoning, conduct, records and follow-up rather than demanding a preferred result. Conversely, a productive researcher still needs to meet standards of conduct. A promising dataset does not resolve a concern about how a colleague is treated.

For ordinary misunderstandings, allow for incomplete information without deciding that either person’s account is wrong. An explanation of intent may change your understanding, but it does not remove the impact or the need for a different arrangement. Repeated harmful conduct and immediate safety or integrity concerns require a route beyond this initial conversation [[7, pp. 27–29]](#ref-feedback-conflict-rabinak2026).

<a id="feedback-conflict-feedback"></a>

## Giving feedback

Prepare a small number of relevant examples. Name the work or behavior you observed, its consequence and the expectation or requested change. Include specific positive feedback when it helps someone recognize what to continue. “Your summary separated the observed result from the proposed explanation, so we could decide which control was missing” is more useful than “Good job.” Avoid making a personality judgment stand in for an explanation [[2, pp. 22–23]](#ref-feedback-conflict-bennett2018).

Discuss an individual’s performance privately, at a time when both people can give the issue attention. Explain the concern directly, then invite their account. Check whether they had the training, time, access and guidance the task required. If you are the supervisor, examine your own commitments as part of that comparison. Missing a deadline does not establish lack of effort when the person was assigned incompatible priorities or could not obtain necessary access [[3, pp. 60–62]](#ref-feedback-conflict-rightmoves2006); [[7, pp. 55–64]](#ref-feedback-conflict-rabinak2026).

Agree a feasible next attempt, the support each person will provide and when you will review it. Check that both people understand the same task; invite corrections to the written summary. For recurring problems, review whether the agreed support was delivered and whether the expectation remains realistic. Formal performance management, disciplinary action or a change of role needs the institution’s current process for that person’s status. An informal agreement does not replace it [[7, pp. 58–64]](#ref-feedback-conflict-rabinak2026).

**An original example: a missed submission deadline**

In this fictional laboratory, a research assistant prepares sample sheets for weekly sequencing submissions. Two sheets due Tuesday at noon arrived Wednesday morning; one lacked the required batch field. The analyst could not begin the agreed check on Tuesday. The supervisor schedules a private discussion with the assistant.

**Supervisor:** The last two sheets arrived Wednesday rather than Tuesday noon, and one was missing the batch field. That delayed the analyst’s check. I want to understand what happened and agree how we will handle the next submission.

**Assistant:** I thought the new template was optional because the old one still opens from the shared folder. I have not been shown how the batch field should be filled. I was also preparing for the imaging work you assigned for Wednesday through Friday, and I did not know which task came first.

**Supervisor:** I assigned both without clarifying the priority, and I did not arrange training on the new template. I need to fix that. For next week, the sample sheet is the Tuesday priority; the imaging preparation can follow. What would you need to complete it by noon?

**Assistant:** A short walkthrough with the analyst and a completed sheet to check against. If I cannot resolve a missing field, I can flag it before the deadline rather than hold the whole sheet back.

**Supervisor:** I will arrange the walkthrough for Monday and confirm that the current template is the one linked from the submission instructions. Please prepare the next sheet by Tuesday noon and tell the analyst and me if a field or another assignment blocks you. Let us review the next two submissions together.

**Assistant:** That works. I will send the sheet Tuesday noon, flag anything unresolved and check the fields with the analyst. Could we put the priority change in the summary so the imaging team knows?

**Supervisor:** Yes. I will confirm the change with them today.

**Written summary agreed after the meeting**

For the next two submissions, the assistant will prepare the sample sheet by Tuesday noon using the current template and flag unresolved fields or competing assignments before the deadline. The supervisor will arrange Monday’s walkthrough, correct the template link and confirm the revised priority with the imaging team. The analyst will check the required fields. The assistant and supervisor will review both submissions in two weeks.

**At the review**

Both sheets arrived by the agreed time with the required fields completed. An equipment delay affecting one sample was flagged in advance, allowing the analyst and supervisor to decide how to handle it. They retain the template and priority arrangement, with a further discussion if the workload changes.

If the sheets remain late, check whether the training occurred and whether competing assignments still prevent timely preparation. Review the agreed work and communication; the outcome of the experiment is a separate question.

<a id="feedback-conflict-science"></a>

## Scientific disagreement

When receiving criticism, ask which result, method or inference the person is questioning and what evidence supports their concern. Take time to examine it before answering if needed. Feeling upset does not establish that the criticism is wrong, and a criticism’s scientific value does not excuse personal humiliation. Lakens’s account of reconsidering his own published work illustrates that distinction; it is a personal reflection, not a prescription to make criticism hurt [[6]](#ref-feedback-conflict-lakens2016criticism).

State the competing interpretations and the assumptions behind them. Ask what observation would distinguish them. Sometimes the missing step is an analysis or a control; sometimes the available evidence cannot decide. Agree what can be concluded now and whether further work is justified by the importance of the question and the resources it would require. Establish who can authorize that work. Senior expertise deserves consideration, but seniority cannot settle an empirical claim [[1, pp. 150–151]](#ref-feedback-conflict-ayres2022).

**An original example: a challenge during lab meeting**

**Researcher:** The figure shows a lower signal after treatment, so I think the treatment reduces the process we are studying.

**Colleague:** Could lower cell numbers explain that signal? I cannot see a cell-number control in this figure.

**Researcher:** This figure alone does not distinguish those explanations. Let us check whether we have an appropriate control before interpreting the decrease. If we do not, we need to decide whether to obtain one or limit the claim.

Group members should be able to question an interpretation during a scientific discussion without first obtaining the project leader’s private approval. The person chairing a meeting can keep criticism focused on the claim, let the presenter finish an answer and acknowledge a useful challenge, including one that changes their own view.

As a senior researcher receiving feedback about your decisions or behavior, listen before rebutting. Ask for clarification, reflect on the examples and return with the action you will take or the reason a proposed change is not feasible. An invitation to disagree carries little weight if disagreement is followed by ridicule, exclusion or loss of opportunity [[2, p. 22]](#ref-feedback-conflict-bennett2018).

<a id="feedback-conflict-arrangements"></a>

## Resolve competing needs and unclear responsibilities

For an ordinary work conflict, ask each person what they need and what is preventing it. Two colleagues disputing a microscope booking may both need access, but one needs a particular time because a live experiment has a fixed window while the other’s samples can wait. Learning that difference creates options that a repeated demand for the same slot cannot reveal. Check the booking rules, actual capacity and alternatives before deciding.

Explore feasible options and judge them against agreed criteria: experimental timing, safety, available staffing, costs and existing commitments. Establish who has authority to resolve the remaining trade-off. A temporary compromise may be adequate for a low-cost scheduling choice; a recurring resource shortage may need a different allocation or additional capacity. Joint problem-solving is useful when people can participate safely and the decision is negotiable. It is not a reason to compromise a safety or integrity requirement [[2, pp. 101–108]](#ref-feedback-conflict-bennett2018).

End with a record of the arrangement, owners, unresolved points and review date. If the same problem returns, inspect the system as well as the last conversation. Did two supervisors promise the same person’s time? Was project authority ambiguous? Did the arrangement depend on a resource that was never supplied? Use the [group’s shared expectations](02-lab-manuals-and-shared-expectations.md#manuals-chapter) to clarify recurring practices and the [collaboration agreement](04-collaboration-authorship-and-credit.md#collaboration-chapter) for project responsibilities and credit.

<a id="feedback-conflict-support"></a>

## Seeking independent support

Direct discussion is not always a safe or effective first step. Threats about graduation, employment, references, funding or residence-related arrangements can make an apparently simple conversation consequential. Repeated humiliation, coercion, harassment, discrimination or retaliation warrants support beyond an ordinary feedback exchange. You do not need to establish a legal label or confront the person before asking for advice [[1, pp. 146–148]](#ref-feedback-conflict-ayres2022).

Identify an adviser whose role fits the concern and whose position is sufficiently independent of the person involved. Possible routes include a programme or committee contact, another manager, an ombud service, an employee or student representative, an equality or harassment adviser, a research-integrity office or a safety contact. Availability and authority vary. A trusted mentor can help you think through a decision, but may not be able to initiate protective or formal action [[4]](#ref-feedback-conflict-chopra2016).

Before sharing identifying details, ask what the adviser can do, what they must report, who may receive information and what confidentiality means in that service. An informal conversation is not automatically off the record. An anonymous channel may also reveal a person’s identity through the details of a small group. Clear routes help, but a lab policy or feedback channel cannot itself remove the barriers to challenging a powerful person [[1, p. 148]](#ref-feedback-conflict-ayres2022); [[5, p. 8]](#ref-feedback-conflict-dona2025).

**An original example: first contact for advice**

I would like advice about a supervision concern. Before I share names or details, could you explain your role, what you would have to report and whether information could reach my supervisor? I would also like to understand what support or formal routes are available, because my completion timetable and reference depend on this relationship.

Keep a factual chronology where it is appropriate and safe: dates, observed words or actions, decisions, relevant messages, witnesses, effects on work and previous requests for help. Distinguish your account from facts you learned from others. Preserve records through permitted channels; ask how to retain relevant evidence without taking restricted research data or exposing someone else’s personal information. A written summary sent to the other person may clarify an ordinary work agreement, as in the example above. If contacting them may trigger retaliation, seek advice about the record and communication first [[1, pp. 147–148]](#ref-feedback-conflict-ayres2022).

If a colleague approaches you for help, listen without demanding that they confront the person or immediately join a meeting with them. Explain the limits of your role and any information-sharing obligations; do not promise confidentiality you cannot provide. Ask what support they are seeking and help them identify a suitable route. A supervisor implicated in the concern cannot serve as its independent facilitator. If there is an immediate danger, prioritize safety and the relevant emergency or institutional response rather than completing a conversation plan.

Discuss with the adviser how each option could affect your work and position. Changing supervisors or leaving the group may affect funding, degree progress or access to records. Papers and references can leave you dependent on the former supervisor after a move. Where relevant, ask about consequences for affiliation or residence arrangements. Consider supported discussion, independent facilitation and formal reporting alongside changes of supervision, assignment or group. Seek current advice before relying on an assumed outcome.

<a id="feedback-conflict-checklist"></a>**Check the next step**

- The concern names an observed event, claim or pattern, with uncertainty kept explicit.

- The proposed route fits the scientific, performance, resource or treatment issue.

- A work agreement includes support, owners, a feasible task and a review point.

- A person with less power can seek advice without first confronting the other party.

- Information-sharing limits and the adviser’s authority are understood before detailed disclosure.

- Follow-up checks what actually changed, including whether promised support was delivered.
