# Mentoring

Discuss what the researcher wants to learn and what support the mentor can provide. A short mentoring agreement can record those expectations and give both people something to revisit as the work and the researcher’s needs change [[6]](#ref-mentoring-masterskreeger2017).

<a id="mentoring-agreement"></a>

## Agreeing expectations

Prepare separately before the first substantial conversation or when revisiting the arrangement. The researcher identifies current work, decisions they want help with, capabilities they want to develop and circumstances that affect what they can undertake. The mentor identifies what support they can reliably provide, where another person is better placed to help and which constraints are fixed. Begin with the researcher’s account, then compare it with the mentor’s expectations. Preparing independently gives both people a chance to identify priorities before either adopts the other’s view [[7]](#ref-mentoring-vincent2015).

Record the decisions in language that both people can use. A concise agreement might cover:

- **Purpose:** Which research decisions and development goals should this relationship support now? What lies outside its scope?

- **Contact:** Which meetings are protected? What belongs in a meeting, a short message or a written draft? What counts as urgent?

- **Preparation:** What should the researcher send or decide before a meeting? What will the mentor read, check or arrange?

- **Decisions:** Which choices can the researcher make independently? Which require discussion because of cost, safety, ethics, shared resources or project commitments?

- **Feedback:** How will each person ask for feedback on work, reasoning and the relationship? How quickly can each realistically respond?

- **Boundaries:** What working-time, privacy and leave expectations apply? Which matters should go to a programme, manager, committee or specialist service?

- **Development:** Which capability or opportunity will be developed next, and how will practice and feedback occur?

- **Review:** When will the agreement be revisited, and what event should trigger an earlier conversation?

Choose a meeting interval that fits the current work and test whether it produces useful decisions. A weekly meeting that repeatedly ends without a choice, action or feedback is not automatically better than a less frequent meeting with good preparation. Conversely, a new method, a difficult transition or a stalled project may justify temporarily closer contact. Early-career researchers surveyed in a 2026 preprint placed high value on useful regular meetings, open communication, boundaries and participation in decisions; the study does not imply one schedule for every relationship [[5]](#ref-mentoring-martinezgoni2026).

Do not require disclosure of private circumstances. A researcher can state the work consequence—“I cannot attend before 10:00 for the next six weeks” or “I need to change the travel plan”—without explaining a diagnosis, family matter or other personal detail. The mentor should explain which constraints can be changed, which require another office and how to reach that office.

Keep individual supervision and development in this agreement. Put practices shared across the group in the [lab manual](02-lab-manuals-and-shared-expectations.md#manuals-chapter), and keep safety, employment, data and programme requirements in their authoritative records.

<a id="mentoring-judgment"></a>

## Use meetings to develop judgment

Bring the material needed for one or two consequential questions. A useful agenda says what has changed, what decision is needed and what the researcher currently recommends. Attach a figure, short rationale or draft when the evidence itself matters; routine status can be brief so the meeting has time for the decision.

Ask the researcher to propose the next experiment and explain what its possible results would tell them. Discuss the reasoning before supplying your own answer. If they cannot choose a next step, find out why. They may need help interpreting a control, training in the method or a decision about access that only you can make. Asking them to be more independent will not resolve a decision they lack the authority to make.

Give a novice a task on which they can practise without putting irreplaceable material or a major project commitment at risk. Demonstrate the technique, explain common failure modes and begin with a task whose expected behavior is already known. As performance becomes reliable, shorten the instructions and move responsibility for planning, execution and interpretation to the researcher [[4]](#ref-mentoring-lukeman2013). When choosing a research problem together, consider whether it interests the researcher, whether it is feasible with the available expertise and what they would learn by pursuing it [[2]](#ref-mentoring-alon2009).

<a id="mentoring-judgment-example"></a>**Example: mentor a decision without taking it over**

In this fictional example, Aruna is a second-year doctoral researcher studying a signaling reporter in cultured cells. She can run the established assay and controls reproducibly and can plan and document a complete run without supervision. Two later pilot runs after a perturbation show different reporter timing. The second also has lower viability and uses a new reagent lot. There is time for one more run this week: either a limited diagnostic comparison or the first large downstream assay.

Aruna’s mentor begins: “Which run would you do this week?”

“Delay the downstream assay and compare the old and new lots in the same culture batch,” Aruna says. “I’ll use the established controls and record viability.”

“What would that let you conclude?”

“If the timing follows the lot and the controls and viability are acceptable, I’ll replace that lot and start the downstream assay next week. If it does not, or viability still differs, the comparison has not resolved the earlier inconsistency and I’ll bring back the options before the next run.”

The mentor asks what delaying the downstream assay costs. They agree to use this week’s run to check the reagent-lot explanation before committing to the larger assay. The mentor confirms access to the old lot and control material and offers to review Aruna’s one-paragraph rationale before she commits the material. Aruna owns the design, run and first interpretation. Their follow-up will examine the controls and viability before deciding whether to proceed.

If Aruna had never run the assay, the same handover would be premature. The mentor or an experienced colleague should first demonstrate the workflow, observe a practice run and check that Aruna can recognize failed controls. If the choice could irreversibly consume rare material, the mentor should join the decision even after Aruna has mastered the technique. Match support to demonstrated capability for this task and the cost of error.

Peer and specialist mentors can provide day-to-day methods knowledge that the primary mentor lacks. Agree which training they will provide and recognize that contribution. A junior researcher should not become solely responsible for another person’s training. The primary mentor must check that the teacher has time and credit, and that the learner knows who will review their progress and help when the teacher cannot.

<a id="mentoring-feedback"></a>

## Feedback

Be clear about what needs to change. “The conclusion exceeds these controls” concerns scientific reasoning. “I need the figure two days before the meeting if you want line-by-line comments” concerns workflow. “I spoke for most of our last three meetings; I want you to present your recommendation first next time” concerns the mentoring relationship. Mixing these can turn a correctable issue into a judgment about the person.

Point to the part of the work or the interaction that needs to change, explain the problem and agree what will happen differently next time. The researcher should also be able to say which support was useful, where advice conflicted or arrived too late and what they need next. Reciprocal feedback does not make the power relationship equal; it gives the senior person information they are responsible for acting on.

When the same problem recurs, compare it with the agreement. Ask whether the expectation was unclear, the promised support was unavailable, the capability was overestimated or circumstances changed. Revise the arrangement and set a review point. Repeated credit-taking, retaliation, blocked access to other advice, coercive disclosure or unsafe practice requires help beyond an ordinary feedback conversation. Use a committee, programme lead, manager, ombud or the institution’s current formal route. Do not require the more vulnerable person to repair the relationship alone.

<a id="mentoring-development"></a>

## Plan development beyond the immediate project

Hold a broader planning conversation periodically and at transitions. Both people prepare independently; the researcher speaks first. Review recent work and discuss what they want to learn or explore next, including career questions that their current project will not answer. Identify opportunities to do that work, the support needed and any limits on time or access. Agree a small number of actions and dates so that development receives time alongside project tasks.

Look for opportunities to practise the skill in real work. A researcher learning to write could draft part of a paper and discuss the revisions with a coauthor. Someone learning how publication decisions are made could join an editorial conversation or work through a review with an experienced colleague [[1]](#ref-mentoring-allis2017); [[8]](#ref-mentoring-chat2021). Obtain permission before involving a trainee in confidential communication, and agree their role.

A useful plan names one near-term opportunity and what evidence will show learning. “Improve presentations” is difficult to act on. “Give the next 10-minute project update; rehearse once with the postdoc; ask two colleagues whether the decision and uncertainty were clear; review the recording with the mentor on 12 November” creates practice and feedback.

Career development may reveal options that the primary mentor does not know well. Use [exploring career options](../09-career-development/01-exploring-career-options.md#career-options-chapter) to compare paths, then identify someone who can supply current experience. The mentor’s responsibility is to support an informed choice, including when it differs from their own path.

<a id="mentoring-network"></a>

## Build a mentoring network

One person rarely provides deep technical guidance, project authority, career knowledge, sponsorship and confidential perspective. List the roles the researcher needs now and where each can come from: primary supervisor, methods expert, collaborator, committee member, peer, career contact or institutional adviser.

The primary mentor should welcome complementary mentors, make introductions where useful and state that seeking other advice is expected. Multiple relationships can reduce dependence and make conflicting advice visible [[3]](#ref-mentoring-chopra2016). When advice conflicts, return to the decision: which adviser has relevant knowledge or authority, what assumptions differ and who ultimately owns the choice?

<a id="mentoring-checklist"></a>**Review a mentoring relationship**

- Both people can state the current purpose, boundaries and next review date.

- Meetings end with a decision, action or clearly named unresolved question.

- The researcher explains reasoning before the mentor supplies an answer.

- Support matches capability for the particular task and the cost of error.

- Feedback can travel in both directions without requiring private disclosure.

- The development plan includes real practice, feedback and a date, not only aspirations.

- Other mentors cover needs that the primary relationship cannot meet.

- Repeated power, safety, credit or conduct problems have a route beyond the direct supervisor.
