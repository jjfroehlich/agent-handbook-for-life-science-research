# Collaboration, authorship and credit

Discuss the shared question and what each collaborator hopes to gain from the work. Agree the contributions and resources needed to begin, then revisit responsibilities and credit as the project develops.

<a id="collaboration-question"></a>

## Start from the shared question

Describe the question and how combining your expertise or resources will help answer it. Ask what each participant contributes and hopes to learn. Decide whether the work calls for a consultation, a standard service or continuing scientific collaboration. A specialist invited only after data collection may be unable to repair the design. When their judgment can change the question, measurements or interpretation, involve them during design [[6]](#ref-collaboration-way2021).

Check capacity as well as interest. Estimate each contribution and identify the time, staff, computing, samples or access that support it. Routine analysis, data curation and software maintenance still require skilled labor. If one group must absorb unfunded work, say so and decide whether the project remains mutually workable [[6]](#ref-collaboration-way2021).

The starting conversation should leave room for different aims. One collaborator may want a biological finding; another may need a method that advances their own research. Agree whether developing that method is part of the project and whether the available time and resources support it. Otherwise, one person may expect a finished analysis while the other expects time to develop and test a new approach.

<a id="collaboration-plan"></a>

## The collaboration agreement

Record the agreed scope, responsibilities and resources while everyone can check that they understood the conversation in the same way. The agreement need not anticipate every event. Team-science guides recommend clarifying goals, roles, resources, communication, data practices and credit early, then revisiting them as the project evolves [[2, pp. 44–49, 77–82]](#ref-collaboration-bennett2018); [[3, pp. 203–207]](#ref-collaboration-rightmoves2006).

Include:

- the shared question, current scope and evidence that would answer it;

- each person’s work, dependencies, protected time and resources;

- decisions that one person can make and decisions that require the group;

- handoffs, quality checks and the records needed to inspect the result;

- meeting or update rhythm, including how a concern can be raised;

- data, code, sample and document locations, access and stewardship;

- likely outputs and a provisional credit process, including when it will be reviewed;

- conditions that trigger a change in scope, roles, timing or budget; and

- what happens to unfinished outputs, records and consultation if someone leaves or the collaboration ends.

Roles say who does work; decision rights say who can change it. A quantitative lead might choose the model implementation, while the group decides whether the analysis supports the biological claim. A facility scientist might set acquisition-quality requirements, while a project lead coordinates the schedule. Name both. Otherwise, a person can carry responsibility for a result without having authority over the choices that determine it.

The following fictional team revisits its plan as the work develops; its eventual credit decision appears later in the chapter.

<a id="collaboration-plan-example"></a>**Example: a collaboration plan**

**Question and scope:** Does transient hypoxia change the recovery of an endothelial barrier over 24 hours? This project covers the exposure experiment, time-course imaging and a prespecified recovery analysis. A mechanism study would require a new decision.

**Scientific value:** Aisha will test a biological hypothesis and develop the exposure system. Leo will develop a time-course model that is useful beyond this dataset. Mei will establish an imaging protocol whose limits are reported with the result. Rao will help connect the design and interpretation.

**Work and resources:** Aisha leads cell experiments, biological quality control and the integrated record. Leo joins before data collection to choose sampling times and replicate structure, then leads analysis, code and quantitative figures. Mei leads acquisition settings, segmentation checks and image interpretation. Rao has budget authority and contributes to design and interpretation. Leo has two protected days per week and named computing support.

**Decisions and handoffs:** Aisha can reject a culture batch against the agreed quality rules. Leo can reject an analysis run that fails the diagnostic checks. Mei can require reacquisition when image quality falls outside the protocol. Changing the exposure, sampling schedule or main claim requires Aisha, Leo and Mei to review the evidence together; Rao resolves a budget constraint after hearing its scientific consequences.

**Records and communication:** Data, code and decisions are stored in the shared project space. The team meets every two weeks while data are being collected and records actions and owners. A failed handoff is raised at the next meeting or sooner if it blocks another person’s work.

**Outputs and credit review:** Aisha is the likely first author and Leo the likely second author at project start. Mei’s position remains open because the imaging-method and manuscript contributions are not yet known. These are expectations, not promises. The group checks the anticipated target journal’s policy now, updates contributions after the pilot and complete dataset, and verifies the current authorship and equal-contribution policies before manuscript drafting.

**Change and exit:** A new mechanistic aim, more than one additional experiment cycle or loss of Leo’s funded time triggers a scope review. If a collaborator leaves, the team records which outputs remain planned, who stewards the shared materials and how the departing contributor will review work arising from their contribution, subject to institutional rules.

<a id="collaboration-expertise"></a>

## Coordinate across expertise

Ask collaborators which choices make their part of the work trustworthy and which limitations affect the shared claim. Before handing data over for analysis, discuss how measurements were acquired, which observations were excluded or missing, and what transformations and model assumptions the analysis requires. The analyst needs to understand how the measurements arose, and the experimentalist needs to understand what the analysis can distinguish.

Before a meeting, circulate the protocol change, diagnostic plot, discrepant result or draft claim that needs discussion. Record what the group decides, why, who will act and what they will check next. Invite disagreement while the group can still change its interpretation or planned work. If a collaborator cannot raise concerns about the design or interpretation, their expertise has little opportunity to shape the result [[2, pp. 45, 50–51]](#ref-collaboration-bennett2018).

Review the plan when the evidence changes the work. A new analysis may become a central intellectual contribution; a planned method may become routine; a contributor may leave or join. Revise responsibilities, resources and credit expectations together.

<a id="collaboration-credit-decisions"></a>

## Contributions, authorship and order

First describe each person’s contribution. Then apply the relevant authorship criteria, agree author order and decide how to acknowledge other work. A contribution record informs these decisions but does not automatically determine them.

**Describe contributions.** Record what each person actually did. CRediT offers 14 roles, including conceptualization, investigation, methodology, data curation, software, formal analysis, visualization, supervision and two writing roles. Use the vocabulary when it fits the work; do not turn the roles into points or assume that every role has equal weight [[5]](#ref-collaboration-niso2022).

**Decide authorship eligibility and responsibility.** Check the target journal and relevant disciplinary or institutional guidance. CRediT is descriptive and is not designed to determine authorship. For journals that follow ICMJE, authors must meet four requirements. In summary, each author must:

1. Have contributed substantially to developing the study’s ideas, planning it, or obtaining, analyzing or interpreting its data.

2. Have helped write the manuscript or critically assessed its intellectual content.

3. Have approved the version being published.

4. Accept responsibility for helping resolve concerns about the work, including parts carried out by others. [[4, section 2]](#ref-collaboration-icmje2026).

If someone questions the work’s reliability, authors must help ensure that the concern is investigated and any problem addressed. They should know who carried out the other parts of the study and have confidence in those contributions. This does not mean personally repeating every specialist check. Consult the full ICMJE criteria when deciding eligibility, and confirm that every author has reviewed and approved the version being submitted. [[4, section 2]](#ref-collaboration-icmje2026).

Corresponding authorship involves managing communication with the journal and ensuring that submission requirements are met; it does not by itself establish a larger scientific contribution. [[4, section 2]](#ref-collaboration-icmje2026).

**Decide order collectively.** Name the convention being used and the rationale. Order conventions differ across fields and journals. Shared-first or shared-senior designations also depend on journal policy. A contribution statement can clarify the work, but displayed order may still affect evaluation [[6]](#ref-collaboration-way2021).

**Recognize other work.** Name non-author contributions as the journal permits, specify the contribution and obtain any required consent. Recognition can also include named presentations, software or data citations and a contribution record for evaluation when those outputs reflect the work.

For an ICMJE-following journal, do not exclude a person who made the substantial contribution in the first criterion by withholding the chance to review or draft the manuscript and approve it. Offer a real opportunity with access and time to participate. Authorship follows only if the person then meets all four criteria [[4]](#ref-collaboration-icmje2026).

<a id="collaboration-credit-example"></a>**Example: a completed contribution and credit decision**

At the final dataset review, the hypoxia project no longer matches its opening expectations. Leo’s diagnostics reveal a batch effect; he redesigns the model and works with Aisha on an additional balanced collection. The change alters the claim. Leo also drafts the analysis and findings and creates the main quantitative figures. Aisha continues to lead the biological work and writes the first complete manuscript. Their central intellectual and practical leadership is now comparable in scope and duration, although the work differs.

Mei’s acquisition design and interpretation are also substantial. Because the selected journal follows ICMJE recommendations, the team gives her the opportunity to complete its manuscript responsibilities. She revises the imaging methods and interpretation, critically reviews the complete paper, approves the submitted version and accepts the journal’s accountability requirement. Rao’s documented conception, interpretation and substantive manuscript review qualify him under the selected policy; funding and supervision alone would not.

<a id="collaboration-credit-record"></a>**Target rule:** The journal permits a shared-first statement and follows ICMJE authorship recommendations. All four authors have critically reviewed or drafted the paper, approved this version and accepted the stated accountability obligation.

**Contribution record:** Aisha—conceptualization, investigation, validation and writing the original draft. Leo—methodology, software, formal analysis, visualization and writing the analysis and findings. Mei—methodology, investigation, validation and critical review and editing. Rao—conceptualization, supervision, interpretation and critical review and editing.

**Byline decision:** Aisha and Leo are shared first authors. Aisha is displayed first because the group agrees that her integrated study leadership and complete first draft justify that sequence. Leo’s comparable quantitative leadership is stated in the equal-contribution note and contribution statement. Mei follows them. Rao is last under the group’s field convention and his documented contribution.

**Trade-off:** The shared-first note represents the team’s judgment, but displayed order may still influence evaluation.

**Other credit:** The technician performed routine stock maintenance and trained Aisha in an established assay, without contributing to the study’s conception, design, data acquisition, analysis or interpretation. The team records the actual work and acknowledges it by name with permission. Aisha and Leo are assigned the biological and quantitative portions of two planned presentations, and each contributor receives this record for evaluation.

If the journal did not allow a shared-first designation, the contribution and eligibility decisions would remain the same. The group would need to choose one displayed order, document its rationale and record the unchosen alternative and trade-off. CRediT roles would still describe the work; they would not calculate the answer.

<a id="collaboration-disagreement"></a>

## Address a credit disagreement with evidence

Start with the disputed decision. “My analysis is missing from the contribution statement,” “I meet the journal’s authorship criteria,” and “I should be listed first” are different claims. Reconstruct the agreed plan and actual work, including changes in scope, drafts, decisions and approvals. Then apply the current target policy and ask what proposed outcome follows from that evidence.

Discuss the record with everyone whose credit would change and write down the revised decision. Early discussion and written updates reduce surprises, but the first agreement is not conclusive when contributions change [[1]](#ref-collaboration-albert2003). Avoid bargaining with unrelated resources or future favors. When a power difference makes direct discussion difficult, use the neutral route named in the agreement or seek confidential procedural advice from the institution.

Journal editors usually lack the evidence and authority to adjudicate who qualifies. Under current ICMJE recommendations, unresolved authorship-eligibility questions should be investigated by the institution or institutions where the work was performed, while author order is decided collectively by the author group [[4]](#ref-collaboration-icmje2026). Other journals and disciplines may use different procedures. Broader relationship conflict, retaliation concerns or formal allegations need their appropriate local route and more support than an authorship worksheet can provide; see [feedback and conflict](06-feedback-and-conflict.md#conflict-chapter).

<a id="collaboration-ending"></a>

## End or transform the collaboration

A project can end while a collaboration continues, and a person can leave while papers, software or datasets remain active. Name which event is happening. Review the outputs still expected, stewardship of shared records and materials, permitted access, remaining consultation, credit review and the date when obligations end. Follow the applicable institutional agreements for data, confidentiality, intellectual property and employment; a collaboration plan does not decide those rights.

State what was learned, what remains unresolved and who can answer questions about each part. If the team begins a new question, write a new scope and contribution record [[2, pp. 46, 132–134]](#ref-collaboration-bennett2018).
