# Career transitions

Before making a career move, work out what you want to change. A new position may leave the same workload or funding uncertainty in place. Consider whether a change of project, supervisor or role would address the problem.

<a id="transitions-decision"></a>

## Decide what needs to change

Think about what you would want to keep as well as what you want to leave. You may still enjoy research but need more predictable hours or a longer contract. If so, investigate whether another research setting offers those conditions before deciding whether to change the kind of work you do.

Compare the available options against the conditions you want to change and those you want to preserve. Include changing project or supervisor as well as leaving a degree or institution. Note what you still need to find out before deciding [[4]](#ref-transitions-perovic).

Discuss the options with people who know the work and can help assess its prospects. Working conditions, mentorship and alternatives to an academic career were recurring concerns in a survey of postdoctoral researchers [[1]](#ref-transitions-afonja2021). When a supervisor is part of the problem, seek advice elsewhere before deciding how to raise it.

<a id="transitions-decision-example"></a>**Example: deciding what to change**

Amir is a postdoc who still enjoys microscopy and troubleshooting. He no longer wants his next position to depend mainly on building an independent publication programme, and he needs to remain in the same region.

Staying for another year could complete an active paper, but it would not change the type of work or the uncertain funding horizon. A second postdoc would offer more bench research, yet preserve the same features he wants to leave. After visiting a microscopy core and speaking with two staff members, Amir learns that the role would replace much of his own-project time with user training, method development and responsibility for reliable shared workflows.

Amir accepts the core-facility role. The decision is a trade-off: he gives up some control over research questions in return for locally based, collaborative technical work. He agrees to a six-week start date only after identifying what the active paper needs and what can be handed over.

<a id="transitions-route"></a>

## Plan the move

Consider applying directly when you have investigated the new role and could start when needed. An intermediate step may help if something specific prevents the move or you still need to try the work before committing. A short placement could help you judge whether the work suits you. A course could teach a required skill; a short contract could provide income before the next position starts. Relocation or caring responsibilities may also affect the route. Decide what the intermediate step needs to accomplish and when you would be ready to move on. Compare it with applying directly before committing to another course or position.

Work backwards from the intended start date. List unfinished work and any materials or access that need to be transferred, then agree who will handle each item and when. Discuss any contribution you expect to continue after departure.

Allow time for delays and agree which unfinished tasks could be handed over if they cannot be completed before departure. Consider the workload during the transition alongside family and caring responsibilities. Agree the limits of any temporary overlap between roles [[5]](#ref-transitions-raffington2026).

<a id="transitions-handover"></a>

## Handing over projects

For each active project, explain the question, what the work has established and what remains to be decided. Record where a colleague can find the data, notebooks, code and samples, with the relevant versions and permissions. Include instructions for regenerating the current result. Label provisional analyses, identify assumptions still to be tested and describe approaches that failed. Flag supplies that may run out or access that will expire, and identify who can decide how to continue.

Ask a colleague to retrieve the material and reproduce a result from these instructions before you leave. Arrange continued institutional storage and access with the group; follow its rules for notebooks, data and samples [[3]](#ref-transitions-rightmoves2006).

<a id="transitions-handover-example"></a>**Example: a project handover**

Amir will start in a microscopy core facility in six weeks. His handover record assigns the remaining work and identifies where to find the material.

**Manuscript:** the main analysis is complete. The collaborating researcher will check a control by 14 May and discuss its implications with Amir on 16 May. Repository release `v1.3` and the figure-to-data table are in the lab project space.

**Pilot project:** no feasible next experiment remains with the available materials and time. The notebook, raw data and a note explaining the stopping decision are indexed in the project space.

**Access check:** a colleague will regenerate Figure 2 using the README before Amir leaves. Amir will correct any missing instructions. The lab administrator will confirm which group account retains access.

<a id="transitions-relationships"></a>

## Continuing responsibilities

Tell the people who need to plan around the change once the timing is settled. Explain what you can finish before leaving and agree any continuing responsibilities, such as contributing to manuscript revisions. If you are taking a project to a new group, discuss which questions, materials and collaborations will move with it [[2]](#ref-transitions-staffinglab2006).

Discuss authorship on unfinished papers with the contributors and record the agreement. Ask prospective referees whether they can support your application. Arrange any continuing access to data or systems with the institution.

If a relationship is difficult, reduce unnecessary dependence: keep clear records, obtain advice from more than one mentor and use the appropriate local route for a matter that cannot be resolved directly.

<a id="transitions-entry"></a>

## Begin the next role

Before starting, discuss your initial responsibilities and who can give feedback. Ask colleagues how decisions and routine communication work, and agree when to review your progress [[2]](#ref-transitions-staffinglab2006).

Agree what to work on before the first review, allowing time to learn unfamiliar procedures. A research scientist might learn a quality-system workflow and complete a defined analysis; an editor might assess a supervised submission while learning the journal’s decision process and audience. Find out whose work depends on yours and what they need from you. Adjust the plan with your supervisor or manager as you learn more about the role.
