Discussion and conclusions

The Results tell readers what happened; the Discussion asks what those findings establish. Begin with the study’s answer, then explain how it relates to previous work, where it remains uncertain and what follows from it.

The examples continue the fictional Brake study: an RNA-derived gene produces a protein that arrests bacterial growth and restricts phage propagation.

Opening and structure

Start with an answer the evidence supports

Answer the Introduction’s question in the opening paragraph, using the few findings needed to support that answer. State the conditions in which it holds. Save detailed comparisons and controls for the passages that discuss them. [1]; [2]; [3].

If the study answers only part of the question, say what was resolved and what evidence is missing for the rest. A study may show that a protein mediates an effect without identifying its molecular target; the unknown target limits the mechanistic explanation, while the functional conclusion can stand. [5]; [4].

Give the argument a clear order

The following sequence is a starting point; limitations may belong within an interpretation or comparison rather than in a separate block. [1]; [6].

A working structure for the Discussion

  1. Answer: State the central finding and the essential evidence behind it.
  2. Interpretation and comparison: Explain the major findings alongside the most relevant previous work. Show what agrees, differs or becomes clearer.
  3. Limits and alternatives: Evaluate the explanations the evidence can distinguish and the conditions under which the answer remains uncertain.
  4. Implications: Explain what the findings change and which unresolved question is worth pursuing next.
  5. Closing contribution: Leave the reader with a concise statement of what the study adds.

Follow the Results order when it supports the same argument. Group experiments that jointly answer one question, and give an unexpected finding early attention when it changes the interpretation. Use subheadings for a long Discussion; a short one may read better without them. [9]; [10].

A combined Results and Discussion can interpret each finding as it appears, then draw the findings together at the end. With separate sections, report new measurements in Results and their procedures in Methods; the Discussion can then concentrate on what they mean. [7]. Some formats permit a preliminary observation within Discussion, but it still needs enough methodological and reporting detail to be evaluated. Place any result essential to the central conclusion in Results. [6]; [10].

Explain findings alongside previous work

Comparing findings

Start with the studies that motivated the question, adding work needed to interpret the findings and relevant papers published during the project. The Introduction may only have needed their central conclusions; here, a particular comparison may require closer attention to the evidence. [10]; [5]. Read the relevant Results and Methods, noting the system, intervention, measured outcome and conditions before deciding whether the findings agree or conflict. [4].

Explain how your finding confirms, extends or conflicts with earlier work. Distinguish confirmation under similar conditions from extension to a new setting. [5]; [3].

Compare studies through their effect estimates and uncertainty, alongside experimental context and analysis choices. Different significance labels do not establish different effects; see comparing effects. Likewise, similar directions do not establish close agreement when estimates are too imprecise to distinguish substantially different effects. [8].

Differences in conditions may suggest why studies disagree, but they do not by themselves establish the explanation. Identify the evidence that would distinguish it from alternatives, and leave the conflict unresolved if that evidence is unavailable. Apply the same care to contrary and supportive findings: cite the specific result, and discuss methodological limitations where they affect the comparison. [5]; [4].

Build paragraphs around an interpretation

Build each paragraph around one point. A paragraph explaining a mechanism needs the evidence for it; a paragraph about disagreement needs the findings being compared. Give readers any unfamiliar background before asking them to follow the interpretation. Add a closing consequence or next question when there is something further to explain. [7]; [4].

As you move between your results and published work, make clear which experiment established each finding. Name the biological subject and use consistent terminology so that a phrase such as “this finding” does not leave readers choosing between several studies. [3]; [4]. The paragraph should explain what the evidence establishes together; a sequence of paper summaries that does not affect the interpretation is usually unnecessary. [5]; [6].

Evaluate limitations and alternatives

Explain why one interpretation is better supported

Consider plausible alternatives that would change the conclusion, and compare their predictions with the observations. Explain which control distinguishes the alternatives and where more than one explanation remains possible. An intervention may establish that a protein contributes to a response while leaving open how it acts. [5]; [4]; [8].

Unexpected findings require the same scrutiny. Examine the conditions and possible measurement problems before developing a biological explanation, and identify explanations developed afterward as hypotheses generated by the result. [8].

Explaining limitations

Explain what each limitation prevents you from concluding. Testing one cell model leaves generality uncertain; an assay that cannot distinguish two mechanisms leaves the mechanism unresolved. Qualify the affected claim and state what the checks did resolve. [1]; [9]; [2]; [5].

Instead of simply noting a small sample, explain whether the estimate is too imprecise to distinguish effects that matter. For a method tested under limited conditions, state which uses remain untested. [6]; [4].

Inconclusive results

Use the estimates and uncertainty described in the reporting chapter to explain what an inconclusive result leaves open. If the uncertainty includes effects large enough to change the interpretation, that uncertainty belongs in the conclusion. Establishing a practically negligible effect requires a suitable design and analysis, such as a justified equivalence analysis; a non-significant test alone cannot establish it. [8].

Example: connecting mechanism, limits and implications

Here RNA-templated defence-gene assembly is a new discovery, as in the main Brake case.

Brake assembles a functional defence gene from an RNA template. Sequence analysis identified a coding sequence spanning DNA repeat junctions. Genetic tests established the encoded protein’s role: blocking translation preserved DNA production but removed defence, and separate protein expression restored it. The effector therefore becomes available through gene assembly rather than expression of a conventional genomic coding sequence.

These experiments establish the protein’s role without identifying its molecular target. Growth arrest could result from interference with a host process needed for both cell growth and phage replication, but the current measurements do not identify that process. Determining the target would help explain how arrest is initiated and why activity differs among the tested phages.

The fate of infected cells is a separate question. They stopped growing during observation, but we did not measure subsequent recovery. Reduced infectious output therefore establishes phage restriction without showing that individual cells survive the infection. Recovery measurements would distinguish reversible arrest from a lasting loss of growth capacity.

These findings show that a functional defence gene can emerge in an RNA-derived DNA product. Searching those products for coding sequences could reveal effectors missed by conventional genome annotation.

The molecular target and cell recovery need different follow-up experiments, so they receive separate paragraphs.

Example: comparing a follow-up with its precedent

In a follow-up version of the Brake paper, RNA-templated antiviral gene synthesis is already known, and the study tests the effector’s function in another system. Tang and colleagues (2024) [11] serves as real prior work. This is also the premise of the second cover letter.

Question Published DRT2 study Fictional Brake study
What connects gene assembly to defence? RNA-derived DNA encodes Neo, whose expression causes growth arrest. A translation-disrupting change preserves DNA production but removes defence; separate protein expression restores it.
What happens to arrested cells? The study includes viability and recovery measurements. Recovery after infection was not measured.
What remains unresolved? The protein’s molecular function requires further investigation. The molecular target of the Brake protein is unknown.

RNA-templated gene synthesis was previously established as a route to bacterial defence in DRT2 systems. [11]. Our Brake experiments identify the protein encoded by the assembled gene as an effector of defence in this system. Blocking translation preserves DNA production but removes defence, and separate protein expression restores it. These results distinguish the protein’s contribution from a direct effect of the DNA product alone. Whether Brake-arrested cells recover remains unresolved: recovery was measured in the DRT2 study but was not tested here.

Develop implications and conclusions

Explain what follows from the finding

Explain how the finding changes an explanation, prediction or measurement capability. The implication should follow from the result: identifying a previously unrecognized source of protein-coding genes, for example, gives researchers a reason to examine material that conventional annotation overlooks. [3]; [7]. For a method, distinguish a newly available capability from better performance on an existing task. A performance claim needs a specified comparator and benchmark, together with the conditions tested. [4]; [6].

Use a model or schematic to connect findings when it clarifies the explanation. Distinguish established relationships from proposed links in the labels and legend. Explain the evidence for each important connection and a testable prediction for a hypothetical one. [6]; [4].

Identify the next question to resolve

Identify the most important remaining question and explain how its possible answers would change the interpretation. [1]; [9]. If that answer is essential to the present conclusion, obtain the evidence now or narrow the claim. [2]; [4].

If you propose an explanation that the study has not tested, show how it follows from the results or previous work and what evidence could challenge it. For a proposed practical application, identify the test still needed before recommending that use. [5]; [4].

Finish with what the study changes

Close with the contribution and its most important implication, retaining any qualification essential to its meaning. The ending should draw out what the findings change, without recounting the experiments again. [7]; [3]. Use a separate Conclusion when the format requires it or a substantial Discussion benefits from a final synthesis. A short Discussion may already have the ending it needs. [2].

Check the interpretation

Read the Introduction’s question and the Discussion’s opening and ending together. If the answer changed during writing, revise the framing while preserving the distinction between the original prediction and interpretations developed afterward. [3]; [8]. Then follow the argument paragraph by paragraph: check claims against results or publications, retain important alternatives, and use the paragraph openings to locate repetition or abrupt changes of subject. [4]; [5].

Before asking for sentence edits, ask a colleague to identify the main answer, unresolved issue and contribution. Where their account differs from yours, find the missing or misleading step in the reasoning. [1].

Before finishing the Discussion

  • Does the opening give a supported answer to the question in the Introduction?
  • Do the main comparisons explain what the study adds, including relevant disagreement?
  • Can readers follow the evidence favoring the interpretation over plausible alternatives?
  • Does each important limitation explain what cannot yet be concluded or recommended?
  • Can readers distinguish an established mechanism from a proposed one, and see what inconclusive findings leave open?
  • Would the proposed future work extend the findings, or is it needed to support a claim the paper already makes?
  • Does the ending leave a clear contribution without repeating the experiment summary?
