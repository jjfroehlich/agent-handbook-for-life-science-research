Titles, abstracts and introductions

A reader scanning search results needs to recognize what the paper is about and what it adds. The title, abstract and Introduction explain this at increasing depth. Draft the title and abstract from the main contribution identified in Planning the paper, then revise them as the manuscript develops. [5]; [6].

Three different jobs

Component What the reader needs What to include
Title Is this study relevant to me? The subject and a precise finding or scope.
Abstract What question was answered, and what was learned? Enough context, approach, results and interpretation to understand the contribution independently.
Introduction Why ask this question, and how will the study address it? Selected background, the unresolved problem and a clear account of the study’s purpose.

The examples continue the fictional Brake study, inspired by Tang and colleagues (2024) [15]. Brake assembles an RNA-derived gene whose protein product arrests bacterial growth and restricts phage propagation.

Choose an informative title

Identify the subject and contribution

Build the title around the biological subject and the main finding. For a methods paper, name the capability the method provides. Include the study system or condition when leaving it out would make the finding appear more general than it is. [1]; [2].

Use recognizable search terms and spell out unfamiliar abbreviations; secondary technical details can wait for the abstract. Replace phrases such as “new insights” with the finding itself. Choose a verb that matches the evidence. “Is associated with” reports a relationship; “activates” claims an effect, and “directly activates” makes a further claim about how it occurs. [3]; [4]; [2].

Choose a descriptive or finding-based title

A finding-based title works when one conclusion captures the paper’s main contribution; a descriptive title may better suit a paper that characterizes several related aspects of a system or resource. Draft both, then ask whether the promised finding or scope is what the paper actually delivers. [3]; [1].

Example: choosing a title

Brake produces an RNA-derived DNA intermediate whose protein product arrests bacterial growth and restricts phage output. The experiments identify the protein’s role but leave its molecular target and the recovery of infected cells unresolved.

Too vague: Characterization of the Brake defence system

The system’s name tells an unfamiliar reader little about the biological finding.

Overclaiming: An RNA-templated gene saves bacteria from viral infection

The study measures phage restriction and growth arrest, not recovery of individual infected cells.

Finding-based: An RNA-templated gene arrests bacterial growth and restricts phage propagation

Use this title when the protein’s origin and function are the paper’s central findings.

Descriptive: RNA-templated gene assembly in the Brake antiviral defence system

This suits a paper organized around several properties of the system. It conveys less of the functional result, so the abstract needs to supply it promptly.

Draft several titles and ask a colleague what each leads them to expect. Revise any wording that suggests a different contribution from the paper’s. [1]; [2].

Write an abstract that stands alone

Connect the question, findings and implication

An abstract should explain the question, what the study found and why that finding matters. Include enough background to understand the question and enough of the approach to understand the evidence. The Nature summary-paragraph guide illustrates one way to arrange these elements. [1].

The Nature summary-paragraph structure

Stage Purpose Sentences in Nature’s guide
1. Broad context Orient scientists outside the immediate field. 1–2
2. Specific background Supply the knowledge needed to understand this study. 2–3
3. Problem Identify the unresolved question. 1
4. Main result State the central answer clearly. 1
5. What the result adds Explain the finding through evidence and comparison with prior knowledge. 2–3
6. Wider meaning Connect the answer back to the larger scientific problem. 1–2

Nature (2019) [9] also allows an optional 2–3 sentences of broader perspective when the editor judges that they improve accessibility. The allocations describe that guide; use the target journal’s current word limit and format.

Draft the question and answer first, then add the background an unfamiliar reader needs. [6]. Test whether the opening sentence helps explain the question: if deleting it leaves the abstract just as clear, it may be too general. [7]; [11].

Give the main finding enough space for its supporting comparison. Briefly introduce an unfamiliar experiment before stating its result. For a familiar comparison, begin with the finding and include the method in the same sentence. [8]; [1]. Nature’s annotated example introduces its assay with the supporting results, without a separate Methods block. [9].

End by explaining what the finding adds. A biological study might identify how a process works; a methods study might make a previously difficult measurement possible. Keep this closing statement within what the study demonstrated. [9]; [4].

Example: giving an abstract a clear argument

Both drafts describe the same fictional Brake study. The numerical comparison is the illustrative dataset developed in the reporting chapter.

Draft organized around procedures

We tested the bacterial defence system Brake using phage infections, DNA sequencing and protein-expression experiments. Intact Brake reduced infectious phage A yield 79-fold relative to the empty-vector control. Repeated RNA-derived DNA was detected, and infection was accompanied by double-stranded DNA and protein production. A translation-disrupting change preserved DNA production but removed growth arrest and defence. Separate protein expression restored both. Phages A and B were restricted, whereas phage C remained productive. These experiments addressed how a defence region encoding a reverse transcriptase and an RNA template, without an annotated effector gene, inhibits infection.

Revised abstract

Some bacterial defence regions encode a reverse transcriptase and an RNA template but lack an identifiable effector gene. How these regions inhibit infection is unclear. Here we show that the Brake defence system assembles an RNA-derived gene whose protein product arrests bacterial growth and restricts phage propagation. Intact Brake reduced infectious phage A yield 79-fold relative to the empty-vector control, with activity differing among three tested phages. Sequencing identified a coding sequence spanning junctions in the repeated DNA product; double-stranded DNA and the encoded protein were detected during infection. Blocking translation preserved DNA production but removed growth arrest and defence, both of which were restored by expressing the protein separately. These findings reveal a defence pathway whose effector gene is assembled from an RNA template.

The revised opening explains why the gene’s origin is a puzzle before presenting the answer. The effect’s magnitude remains; the full interval and replication details appear in Results.

Select details that make the result understandable

Select details that establish what was compared and what the comparison showed. Name the system and any method central to the contribution; summarize routine procedures with the finding. [3]; [11]. Give the principal effect’s magnitude and reference group, adding uncertainty or sample information when interpretation or the journal’s format requires it. A percentage without its reference group, or an improvement without its comparator, leaves the result ambiguous. [4]; [9].

Include a limitation if leaving it out would misrepresent the answer. For Brake, stating that activity differed among the three tested phages prevents the abstract from implying that Brake restricts every phage. Secondary controls and exploratory findings can remain in Results. [4]; [1]. Read the abstract without the paper beside it: can readers identify the methods and groups, and understand its terms and abbreviations? Use the same names throughout. [12]; [4].

Adapt to the format and word limit

Check the article’s word limit, headings and required reporting elements before polishing. For a structured abstract, distribute the argument under the journal’s headings, commonly Background or Objectives, Methods, Results and Conclusions. Make sure the Results answer the stated objective. [5].

If the draft is too long, first cut background that does not lead to the question, repeated claims and secondary procedural detail. Then combine related statements, checking that the main comparison remains clear. Avoid saving words through unfamiliar abbreviations or overloaded sentences. [6]; [4]. Use the structural outline to check whether background has crowded out the findings. Studies linking writing features with citation counts do not establish that changing abstract length makes a paper clearer or more influential. [14].

Some journals also request a separate significance statement for a broader audience. Explain the problem and the value of the advance with less technical detail, following the journal’s instructions. Keep the approach and principal findings in the research abstract.

Give a methods paper its own emphasis

A methods abstract needs to explain what researchers can do with the method and how well it performs. Identify the limitation it addresses, such as an unreliable measurement or a time-consuming procedure, then explain what it changes. Give the benchmark or application that demonstrates its performance, including the conditions under which it was tested. Keep the method’s name and recognizable technical terms prominent. If the study also makes a biological discovery, explain how the method enabled it. [10].

Build the introduction around the question

Select the background the reader needs

Use the sequence below to select the background and plan the paragraphs. [1]; [12].

A common introduction structure

Stage Purpose
1. Scientific context Establish the process or problem and why understanding it matters.
2. Relevant knowledge Explain the findings, ideas or methods on which the study builds. Select what the reader needs for the next step.
3. Unresolved question Show what remains uncertain or inadequate, and why resolving it would matter. State the question or hypothesis the study addresses.
4. Present study Introduce the approach and, where appropriate, the principal answer and contribution. Prepare readers for the Results.

Combine these functions when they form one point: a paragraph describing a method can also explain the limitation your study addresses. Start a new paragraph when the point changes. [1, Rule 6]; [12].

Choose background in relation to the question, rather than trying to summarize the whole field. Explain what remains unresolved and what an answer would change. For a methods paper, this means identifying the measurement that is difficult or unreliable and why researchers need it. [13]; [4].

Use the literature fairly and precisely

Organize previous work by the points it establishes, using reviews for orientation and original studies for specific findings. Where disagreement motivates the study, check whether the earlier experiments asked comparable questions under comparable conditions. Place citations beside the claims they support, splitting a sentence if different references support different parts. [5]; [4].

Before claiming priority, search alternative terminology and examine the closest studies. If priority remains uncertain, describe the specific advance. [4]; [13].

Connect paragraphs and introduce the study

Connect paragraphs through the scientific reasoning. After describing a method’s limitation, explain how the proposed method addresses it. After identifying an unexplained observation, introduce the question it raises. Reuse the same scientific terms so readers can follow the connection. In the Brake example, the missing effector leads to a question about the DNA product, followed by tests of the protein’s function. [1]; [4]. A closing interpretation can explain why the next step is needed. Omit a closing summary that merely repeats the paragraph’s opening. [12].

End with the study’s question and approach, usually followed by its main finding. Follow the article’s conventions if it calls for an aims-focused ending. Include a section-by-section roadmap only if the paper’s organization needs explanation. [13]; [3]; [12].

Example: introducing a biological question

This short introduction develops the question raised by Brake’s apparently missing effector gene. A real manuscript would need background citations and more context about the system.

Bacterial defence relies on molecules that interrupt phage replication. A protein-coding gene provides a starting point for identifying and testing a defence effector. When a defence region contains no recognizable effector gene, the origin of the protective molecule remains unresolved.

Brake contains a reverse transcriptase and a conserved RNA template, but no annotated effector gene. Its reverse transcriptase suggests that an RNA-derived DNA product may be involved. What does the reverse transcriptase produce, and how could that product contribute to defence?

Here we combine sequence analysis with genetic perturbations to determine how Brake restricts phage infection. We find that repeated RNA-derived DNA forms a continuous coding sequence whose protein product arrests bacterial growth and restricts phage propagation. Preventing translation abolishes defence while preserving DNA production; expressing the protein separately restores it. These findings reveal a defence pathway in which the functional gene is assembled from an RNA template.

The final paragraph includes the genetic tests because detecting the product alone would not establish its role in defence. Phage range and the numerical effect can remain in the abstract and Results. The absence of an annotated effector gene is part of the fictional study’s starting analysis; a real manuscript would cite the source of that annotation.

Check the opening against the paper

Read the title, abstract and final Introduction paragraph together, then compare them with Results. Check that they describe the same contribution and that the evidence answers the question posed. [4]; [1]. Trace numerical statements back to their comparisons, including the population, condition and reference group. [5].

Some repetition between the abstract and Introduction is necessary because each must make sense independently. Within either section, remove explanations that do the same job twice and check that each paragraph advances the argument. [12]; [4].

Ask a colleague from the broader field to explain the question and answer after reading the opening. Revise where their understanding differs from the intended meaning. [7]; [1].

Final opening checklist

  • The title identifies the subject and gives a supported finding or clear scope.
  • The abstract explains the question, approach, main result and implication independently.
  • Quantitative statements name the comparison, and shortened passages retain the conditions needed to interpret the findings.
  • The introduction supplies the background needed for a specific, worthwhile question.
  • Citations support the precise statements made, and prior work is distinguished from this study.
  • Paragraphs lead into one another, and the ending prepares readers for the Results.
  • Terminology and claim strength agree across the opening, figures and main text.
  • The chosen article format is satisfied, and an unfamiliar reader can explain what was learned.
