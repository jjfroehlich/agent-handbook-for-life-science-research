# Layout, axes and readable comparisons

Read this when arrangement, labels, scales or type prevent a scientifically appropriate figure from being understood. Inspect the actual artifact and its intended size. A balanced full-screen composition can fail in a narrow manuscript column.

## Organize comparisons before decoration

Keep each result beside the control or context needed to interpret it. Arrange panels in a coherent reading sequence, using proximity, alignment and whitespace to express relationships. Preserve stable symbols and group identities across panels. Equal-sized boxes are not obligatory; allocate space according to the amount of information and the comparison. Use a shared axis or scale when it supports comparison, not merely to make a grid look tidy.

Create a clear first-look focus without suppressing evidence that could change the conclusion. Equally important groups need comparable weight; secondary guides can recede. Remove repetition before shrinking text or discarding data. Negative space separates groups and prevents crowding, but excessive gaps can sever related elements. A gray background, frame or grid can help unify facets or support value reading; do not remove every non-data mark by principle.

Separate three roles: background guides orient the reader, data marks remain readable, and emphasis directs attention to the intended comparison. A small difference in contrast can be enough. Changing emphasis should not remove the surrounding data or turn a scientific comparison into a promotional single-message slide.

Use labels near what they identify. Direct labels reduce legend lookup when they fit; a concise legend is better than colliding labels. Align a point label to its own point rather than to an unrelated column of labels. Put panel letters consistently where readers scan. Let annotations explain a relationship, not repeat every value already visible.

A captioned manuscript panel and a standalone graphic may need different titles, but both need recoverable quantities, units and normalization. Check that labels or reference guides do not cut a bar into apparently separate pieces or extend its apparent length.

## Axes encode the scientific comparison

Length bars ordinarily require a zero baseline because length carries magnitude. A positional dot or line plot need not include zero in every case, but its range and aspect ratio can still exaggerate a small change. Label ranges conspicuously and retain appropriate absolute context. Broken axes do not repair misleading bar lengths; a full-range overview and detail panel are often clearer.

Choose transformations for the question. A log axis makes multiplicative changes comparable and cannot contain zero or negative values. Distinguish original-value ticks on a log axis from pretransformed values on a linear axis, including the transformation base. Preserve controls at zero in an explicitly justified separate view, categorical position or other suitable display; do not silently drop them or invent a pseudocount. A square-root scale permits zero but has different interpretation. Skew alone does not force a log axis, and ratios need not always be logarithmic.

Preserve actual spacing of time, dose or other measured coordinates. Connecting observations at unequal times can be valid on their actual coordinates; equally spacing them while labeling unequal times distorts change. Use ticks, units and restrained guides for the reading task. A paired comparison of the same quantity can benefit from an equality line; x/y placement alone does not establish cause and effect.

Changed condition: common scales support absolute comparisons among compounds. Clearly labeled separate ranges may reveal within-compound changes, but no longer support direct cross-compound magnitude judgments. Explain that tradeoff instead of treating shared or free scales as universally correct.

## Typography and tables

Use a coherent type system and restrained emphasis. Keep mathematical, Greek and sequence symbols correct; monospace has a functional role for aligned sequences. Typeface and point-size recipes are starting preferences, not universal legibility thresholds. Carefully chosen combinations can work; consistency does not require every text element to be identical.

For tables, put identifiers predictably, align numbers for comparison and use meaningful precision. Counts and scaled measurements need different formatting; uniform decimal places must not imply unsupported precision. Remove repeated phrases and units before reducing type. Sparse row guides or subtle shading can help tracking in long tables even when a short table needs none. Quantitative cell color is an optional overview layer; exact values and a meaningful common mapping must remain recoverable.

## Inspect at the destination size

Distinguish the complete figure canvas, each panel and its data rectangle bounded by the axes. For a manuscript panel without settled dimensions, prefer a compact candidate layout: keep intended final-size text and mark weights stable while testing shorter physical axes and tighter padding. Replot at those dimensions instead of shrinking a finished image. Keep numerical limits and included observations unchanged unless the scientific task independently calls for a change. Check aspect ratio where slopes, shapes or equal-unit geometry matter.

Remove redundant internal titles or repeated keys when the caption or a clearly shared key already supplies them. Retain units, normalization and unambiguous panel associations. Useful whitespace still separates groups and directs attention; label clearance alone does not establish a good minimum size. Inspect whether points, intervals and curves remain distinguishable as well as whether text fits. Treat compactness as a starting preference, not an optimization target that overrides interpretation or accessibility.

Changed condition: three groups with short labels and sparse observations may need only a small data rectangle. The same groups with long condition names or many paired trajectories may need more width, height or a different arrangement. If using 7 pt ordinary labels as a starting setting, keep that final-size setting while comparing layouts; do not infer that every plot must fit at 7 pt or that a fixed half-page height suits every panel.

Check text, thin lines, small marks, legends, color contrast, image structures and panel associations at final physical dimensions or viewing distance. Compare alternative arrangements using the same data. If a figure only works when zoomed in, simplify or split it rather than declaring the export ready. For an 85-mm figure, inspect the actual 85-mm rendering; pixel dimensions alone do not establish print legibility. Report what was inspected and what remains unverified.
