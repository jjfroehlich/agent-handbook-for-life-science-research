# Reviewer Ethics

Use this when the user asks about conflicts of interest, confidentiality, use of unpublished information, suspected misconduct, confidential comments, review transfer, open peer review, or whether to accept a review.

## The job of this topic

Protect the integrity of the review process while still helping the reviewer produce useful feedback. Ethics guidance is a boundary layer around peer-review content.

## Default workflow

1. Identify the ethical issue: conflict, competence, confidentiality, privileged information, misconduct concern, review reuse, open review, or confidential communication.
2. Check journal instructions and reviewer invitation terms first.
3. Represent identity and expertise truthfully, then decide whether to accept, decline, disclose or ask the editor.
4. Keep unpublished manuscript information confidential unless policy and consent clearly allow sharing.
5. Before involving a student, colleague, co-reviewer, or review-transfer workflow, get editor or journal permission and name contributors when required.
6. Put sensitive concerns in the editor-only channel when appropriate.
7. Avoid public accusations, personal use of privileged information, citation coercion, or unapproved sharing with colleagues or trainees.

## Decision rules

- Disclose actual or reasonably perceived competing interests before accepting and recheck after seeing the files. Pause and consult the editor if a material conflict emerges. Personal confidence in impartiality does not remove disclosure, and a historical three- or five-year example is not a universal safe harbor.
- If you need help from another person to review, ask the editor before sharing the manuscript.
- If you suspect misconduct, plagiarism, image manipulation, or serious ethics problems, tell the editor with evidence and avoid author-facing accusations.
- If a review may be signed, published, or transferred, check each permission separately: releasing reports, identifying reviewers and moving material between journals are different choices. Check the policy's treatment of author responses and attachments as well as reports; do not infer reviewer identity from an anonymous published history.
- If the manuscript overlaps with your own unpublished work, disclose the overlap and avoid using privileged information.
- If you cannot complete the review on time, tell the editor early.
- If recommending citations, make sure they are necessary, relevant, and proportionate rather than serving reviewer self-interest.

## Variants and edge cases

- **Trainee involvement:** Involving trainees can be educational only when journal policy permits it and confidentiality is preserved.
- **Open review:** Public readability increases the need for context, fairness, and avoidance of language that can be weaponized.
- **Review transfer:** Obtain applicable originating-journal permission, declare prior involvement and reassess the new version and criteria. Identify which older comments no longer apply rather than silently presenting an old report as a fresh assessment.
- **Competing work:** A reviewer can be expert and conflicted at the same time; disclose and let the editor decide.
- **Confidential comments:** Keep the scientific reasons consistent with the author-visible report. A concise private synthesis can have a different emphasis, remain blank or refer to the report without duplicating it; do not hide a contradictory or otherwise invisible fatal critique.

## Permission and public context

Maintain confidentiality during and after the assignment. Publication of the paper does not automatically release every unpublished version, report, attachment or correspondence item. Review access does not authorize uploading confidential material to another person or service. Check actual applicable terms; archived advice does not establish a current technology policy.

Before authorized co-review, arrange how the contributor will be recognized and make sure the invited reviewer checks and remains responsible for the submitted assessment. Training can instead use permitted public reports or nonconfidential material. Do not infer sharing authority merely because mentoring is encouraged.

After submitting, answer relevant journal queries and report new information that could materially change the assessment. If a revision is requested, give a feasible answer about availability rather than silently accept a deadline you cannot meet.

“Open review” has several independent dimensions: when review happens, who is identified, how communication is mediated, whether content becomes public, who facilitates it and who owns the content. Author permission to publish history and reviewer agreement to sign are separate choices. A report can be signed privately or published anonymously; portability does not itself establish permission to publish or reveal a name.

Check the actual package, including author responses and attachments, and the permissions covering earlier-journal reports. State origin, date and manuscript version when reusing permitted material. A criticism may concern a removed experiment or another journal's impact threshold; verify whether it still applies to the final claim. Do not guess an anonymous reviewer's identity or assume publication proves the concern was resolved.

Weigh transparency's possible learning, credit and reader-context benefits alongside work/cost and unequal retaliation risks. Practitioner estimates and classroom anecdotes are not measured general effects. The available secondary findings on review quality are mixed and concern different outcomes; neither openness nor signing guarantees better science. A junior reviewer's dependence can justify a different signing decision. Policy advocacy and its proposed three-part summary/technical/impact form remain options, not obligations or authorization to contact anyone.

For suspected irregularities, give the journal relevant facts and uncertainty, then cooperate with its process. Do not investigate privately or claim guilt. Citation suggestions likewise need an academic reason: a missing attribution or decisive prior result can matter, but unrelated citations must not be demanded or added merely to placate a reviewer or improve a metric.

## Anti-patterns

- Sharing manuscripts with lab members without editor approval.
- Using ideas, data, or methods from a manuscript before publication.
- Accepting a review to delay or influence a competitor.
- Hiding conflicts because they seem obvious or minor.
- Making unsupported accusations in author-facing comments.
- Publishing or reusing review content without consent.
- Recommending unnecessary citations to benefit the reviewer or associates.

## Diagnostic questions

- Would the editor want to know this relationship, competition, or prior involvement?
- Can I review the manuscript without using privileged information?
- Does journal policy allow sharing the manuscript with anyone else?
- Is this concern something authors can fix, or something the editor must investigate?
- Would public release of this review expose confidential or unfairly framed information?
- Am I separating ethical concerns from scientific disagreement?

## Output patterns / mini-templates

**Conflict disclosure**

```text
Before accepting, I should disclose that [relationship/competition/prior involvement]. I can provide an objective review on [areas], but I understand if you prefer another reviewer.
```

**Editor-only ethics note**

```text
Confidential note to editor: I noticed [specific concern] in [location]. I cannot determine intent from the manuscript, but it may warrant editorial review before a decision.
```

**Decline note**

```text
Thank you for the invitation. I should decline because [conflict/expertise/deadline]. If helpful, possible reviewers include [names or expertise areas].
```

## Examples

- If a reviewer recognizes a collaborator's work in a double-blind process, the next step is disclosure to the editor, not silent continuation.
- If a lab member has the right expertise, the reviewer still needs journal permission before sharing the manuscript.

## When not to apply this

Do not use this playbook to make legal or institutional misconduct determinations. It helps the agent route concerns to the editor and follow journal policy.
