# Advisor Choice

Use this when the user is evaluating a PhD advisor, postdoc mentor, rotation lab, thesis committee member, or research group.

## The job of this topic

Help the user choose a training environment using evidence about mentoring behavior, power, safety, resources, and career fit. The goal is not to identify the most famous lab; it is to identify a place where the user can do good work, develop, and stay safe enough to make progress.

## Default workflow

1. Clarify stage and commitment level: prospective student, rotation, accepted offer, current trainee, postdoc applicant, or trainee in difficulty.
2. Define the user's nonnegotiables: field fit, geography, funding, visa status, family constraints, mental health, methods, independence, and career direction.
3. Compare advisors on observable behavior: meeting cadence, feedback, project choice, authorship, publication support, conflict handling, funding stability, and alumni outcomes.
4. Select feasible information channels: the advisor, privately consenting current members, alumni, neighboring groups, the department or direct observation. Consider what each person can know and what dependence or incentives may limit candor; no mandatory reviewer count applies.
5. Distinguish concrete harmful conduct, incomplete information and ordinary differences in working style. Name what could change the decision without requiring more harm or corroboration before support.
6. End with questions to ask, people to contact, a risk matrix, or a decision recommendation with caveats.

## Decision rules

- If the science is exciting but trainees describe fear, retaliation, isolation, credit problems, or ignored boundaries, prioritize safety and fallback options over project appeal.
- If current trainees are uniformly positive, still ask former trainees and adjacent lab members; the visible trainees may be the people for whom the lab already worked.
- If an advisor promises autonomy, ask for examples of how projects, authorship, and independence developed for recent trainees.
- If a lab depends on one grant, one project, or one advisor relationship, check what happens if funding, project direction, or fit changes.
- If the user reports a harmful relationship, take it seriously and help identify safe support and practical options. Consider documentation or institutional channels only with attention to access, confidentiality and retaliation risk; do not require a misconduct determination before support.

## Variants and Edge Cases

Mentoring needs can change. A useful starter project or frequent initial feedback can support later agency; immediate autonomy is not the only path to independence. Ask who provides daily teaching, whether that person has capacity, how feedback changes with experience, and what happens if a project or advisor leaves. A written philosophy or a successful alumnus does not establish that this trainee will receive the same support.

For harmful conduct, identify the concrete behavior and continuing dependencies: degree decisions, pay, residence, access, authorship, references and future opportunities. Offer feasible support or changes without diagnosing a mentor or insisting on confrontation, formal reporting, disclosure or immediate departure. A person may remain dependent after leaving. Before suggesting a channel, check its remit, who sees a record and what confidentiality it can actually offer; do not promise a remedy or safe reporting.

Match the response to the problem. Routine absence may call for realistic contact arrangements, advance booking or another available mentor; delayed required sign-off may call for an agreed deadline and an alternative authorized channel. Assess unrelated tasks against workload and training goals rather than treating every supervisory request as exploitation. Restricted outside advice or weak advocacy may call for complementary independent mentors who can supply expertise, opportunities or support. Appropriation of work or coercion can require different institutional or change-of-environment options. These are possibilities, not diagnoses or a required escalation sequence: safety, readiness and continuing dependencies override prescriptions to confront or leave.

Separate coping choices from institutional responsibility. Ask which mentoring, inclusion, reporting and accountability arrangements are implemented and accessible, including an alternative contact outside the supervisor's control. A trainee's resilience, boundaries or communication cannot replace institutional action. A policy or proposed reform is not proof that protection exists in practice.

- For rotations, optimize for direct observation: how quickly feedback arrives, how people talk to each other, how mistakes are handled, and whether expectations are explicit.
- For international trainees, include contract, visa, residence, funding, and institutional dependency in the risk assessment.
- For postdocs, add independence, project portability, job-market support, and whether the PI will let the trainee take a direction forward.
- For co-advising, ask who controls funding, authorship, meeting structure, conflict resolution, and final decisions.

## Anti-Patterns

- Choosing only by topic match, institutional brand, or publication venue.
- Asking "Is the advisor nice?" instead of asking for specific examples of behavior under pressure.
- Treating gossip and direct trainee evidence as the same kind of signal.
- Ignoring repeated vague answers about funding, authorship, expectations, or conflict.
- Advising confrontation when the user has not assessed retaliation risk.

## Diagnostic Questions

- What does a normal week of supervision look like?
- How often do trainees meet the advisor, and who drives the agenda?
- What happened to the last several trainees at the user's level?
- How are authorship, project ownership, and conference opportunities decided?
- What happens when a project fails, a trainee struggles, or the advisor disagrees with the trainee's career goal?
- Who can the user ask outside the advisor's direct influence?

Risk statement:

> The science is a strong fit, but the evidence on mentoring is incomplete. Before accepting, verify funding duration, authorship norms, current-trainee morale, and whether former trainees would choose the lab again.

## Examples

- Useful signal: a former trainee describes how timely feedback, credit decisions and support worked in a specific case; assess its relevance rather than counting endorsements.
- Warning signal: current students avoid direct answers, describe "thick skin" as necessary, or say people succeed only if they already know how to manage the PI.
- Missing evidence: the advisor says students are independent but cannot name how they help a struggling first-year student build structure.

## When not to apply this

Do not use this playbook for general lab-management advice from a PI's perspective. Use it only when the user is choosing, evaluating, or navigating a mentoring relationship for their own career.

## Advice Calibration

When outside advice conflicts, identify what each person is optimizing for before treating it as evidence. A happy person may recommend the path that worked for them; an unhappy person may warn against the path that failed them. Ask what values, constraints, career stage, lab size, and risk tolerance shaped the advice, then compare that context with the user's own goals.

Add these checks to advisor comparisons:

- What evidence is observed or reported, what could this informant know, and what remains uncertain?
- Do I respect this person and communicate well with them under ordinary disagreement?
- What values does this lab reward in practice?
- Which expectations and boundaries are already explicit?
- Which doubts remain, and what evidence would resolve them?

Avoid outsourcing a high-dependency career decision to another person's goals, prestige preferences, or personal history.
