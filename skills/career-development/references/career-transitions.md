# Career Transitions

Use this when the user is comparing academia, biotech, industry, editing, policy, business, consulting, entrepreneurship, or another path, or when they are deciding whether to leave a PhD, postdoc, lab, or academic track.

## The job of this topic

Move the user from vague attraction or fear to evidence-based comparison. Career-transition guidance should compare daily work, incentives, constraints, entry evidence, and reversible next experiments.

## Default workflow

1. Clarify the paths under comparison and the user's hard constraints: money, location, visa, family, health, timing, risk tolerance, values, and identity.
2. Define each option by daily work, problems solved, stakeholders, outputs, success metrics, collaboration patterns, and time horizons.
3. Translate the user's scientific training into target-role evidence.
4. Identify missing evidence and design small tests: informational interviews, job-ad analysis, short projects, coursework, internships, shadowing, or targeted networking.
5. Separate acute distress from durable mismatch when the user is considering leaving a path.
6. For offers, distinguish must-haves and preferences. For new roles, plan relationships, commitments, feedback and early evidence of fit over an initial period appropriate to the role and first review point.
7. End with a comparison table, experiment plan, translated materials, negotiation plan, first-month plan, or decision memo.

## Decision rules

Treat interests and nonwork priorities as revisable inputs, not a test of belonging. Sustainable pacing, caring, location and income can change which work is feasible. After a setback, use actual criticism to decide whether to improve the application, redirect the goal or stop pursuing it; neither temporary discouragement nor sunk effort settles the choice. Reconsidering a PI career does not necessarily mean leaving a doctorate. Selection does not measure personal worth, and persistence guarantees no outcome.

- If a role sounds attractive only by title, collect daily-work and success-metric evidence before recommending it.
- For a nonurgent decision about leaving a PhD or academic path, compare reversible and hard-to-reverse steps, support, finances, residence and feasible fallbacks. Immediate support or a protective choice must not depend on completing that comparison.
- If the transition requires missing skills, build a focused bridge project or training plan rather than a broad self-improvement list.
- If the user wants industry, translate research evidence into business, product, team, regulatory, communication, or operational language where relevant.
- If prestige and fit conflict, name the tradeoff and ask which constraint the user is willing to pay for.
- If an offer exists, negotiate from ranked priorities, market evidence, workable terms, and written confirmation rather than from a vague desire for "more."
- If the user is starting a role, plan early integration around relationships, expectations, feedback, and signals of structural fit or mismatch.

## Variants and Edge Cases

### Compare the work behind the title

Use the rows relevant to the person's options; they are distinctions to investigate, not a list of professions to recommend indiscriminately.

| Role family | Work and consequential questions |
|---|---|
| Discovery, development and manufacturing | Discovery asks new scientific questions; application/process development makes methods or products work reliably; production may involve shifts, quality, engineering, supply and technical troubleshooting. What regulated-work training is required, and is entry or later transfer realistic for this applicant? Advice for job-focused undergraduates is not automatically a PhD route. |
| Medical writing and corporate intelligence | What document, evidence synthesis or decision support is produced, for whom, and with what subject expertise? These differ from writing a manuscript or making a public explanation. |
| Regulatory and clinical work | Distinguish strategy/submission responsibility from administrative coordination, and monitoring from data, statistics, quality or safety roles. Ask about actual authority, required training and level; a doctorate neither qualifies everyone for all roles nor is required for every entry post. |
| Applications or field scientist | How much customer troubleshooting, teaching, installation, demonstration and travel occurs? Can the person accept the location and schedule demands? |
| Sales, business development and CRO work | Sales differs from partnership/licensing work. Establish whether a CRO supplies contract research, clinical services or something else, and what clients, project visibility, publication limits and team changes mean for this job. |
| Computational work | Identify the actual scientific question, disciplinary integration and demonstrated analysis/software contribution; a large spreadsheet or job title alone is not evidence of computational readiness. |
| Project management | Ask about the temporary project's scope, cost, schedule, stakeholders and decision rights, rather than treating coordination as generic ongoing management or assuming a credential alone demonstrates it. |
| Consulting or nonprofit work | Investigate actual case/client work or mission, funding and institutional constraints; neither label establishes autonomy, meaning or stable conditions. |

For biotech, inspect the specific role and company: resources, financing/runway, project priority, team and product constraints, publication opportunities, workload and realistic fallback employers. Startup breadth and acquisition risk differ from large-company specialization, but either can offer creative work or change priorities. A selected happy profile does not establish this person's odds, pay, balance or security.

### Choose preparation with a purpose

Find the gap the employer actually needs filled, and ask what it teaches new staff. Compare direct employment with focused practice, a course, a credential, internship or bridge role by recognition, cost, access and opportunity cost. Unpaid experience or another postdoc may be infeasible and need not improve entry. Define what an interim step should establish and when to reassess it; label unfinished preparation honestly. Do not accumulate credentials to avoid an application that is already feasible.

### Find opportunities and understand the process

Combine suitable public openings, relevant contacts, events and other channels. Build reciprocal connections through genuine interests, useful questions and feasible follow-up; peers and near-peers can know routes that a senior adviser does not. Quiet participation is legitimate. Do not impose contact quotas, costly attendance or a hidden-market percentage, and do not promise that networking causes a job.

Clarify who makes which decision. HR, the hiring manager, a committee, an employer-paid retained or contingency recruiter, an agency and a paid career service have different roles and incentives. Require the candidate's knowledge and approval before a recruiter submits credentials, and follow actual required channels. An intermediary's assurance or referral does not establish an opening, decision authority or access.

Keep a usable record of genuine interests, submitted versions, deadlines and promised actions. Ask about next steps; follow agreed/stated timelines with brief relevant thanks, updates or questions, then stop before repeated contact becomes pestering. Historical one-week, ten-day or repeated-call advice is not a universal schedule. Silence does not establish rejection or acceptance, and endless polishing can displace useful search activity.

### Negotiate the actual arrangement

Start from priorities, credible pay/package context, counterpart authority and truthful alternatives. Compare work, benefits, start date, location and other material terms together. Competing offers can supply real timing information or leverage, but may risk trust or withdrawal; a bidding contest is not obligatory. Do not invent alternatives, prescribe theatrical flinching or demand salary-history disclosure. Raise a feasible set of priority requests with reasons, consider trade-offs and document agreed terms through the people authorized to settle them. A friendly written summary is not necessarily their agreement.

### Learn the job and manage a departure

Clarify responsibilities, direction, feedback and promises with a new manager; show actual contributions and build workable relationships. Influence is not supervisory authority. Exclusion, bullying, exploitation or misrepresented duties are not necessarily a failure to network or adapt; repair or internal transfer is an option only if feasible and safe. Do not demand a fixed probation plan for a small current-role question.

On departure, agree responsibilities and dates for continuing manuscripts, credit, data/material access, references and contact continuity with collaborators and authorized offices. Written records help expose unresolved dependence, but do not establish that the PI owns all work, everything can transfer, a grant can move or retaliation ends with the appointment. Keep handover effort within actual role capacity and permissions.

### Interpret career evidence

Separate a historical workforce count, selected successful cohort, coded advice frequency, programme outcome and expert prediction. Keep date, population and denominator with any number that is actually needed. None alone supplies personal hiring odds, proves that a tactic works or establishes current availability/entitlement. Preserve unresolved arithmetic rather than repairing it silently or averaging incompatible figures. Use current role evidence for the decision instead of converting an archive's optimistic forecast into a promise.

- For biotech roles, compare team orientation, milestones, funding runway, product or platform model, and tolerance for changing priorities.
- For leaving-PhD decisions, include mental-health and safety support without pretending to provide clinical or legal advice.
- For informational interviews, ask about daily work, success metrics, frustrations, hiring criteria, and examples of people who thrive or leave.
- For networking through meetings, choose venues by relevance, access to target people, visibility, and cost; follow up with context.
- For biotech roles, avoid generalizing from one company: company stage, financing, product risk, leadership norms, and role family can change the work.
- For non-faculty negotiation, stay within the user's leverage and safety: timing, norms, visa constraints, and local labor conditions matter.

## Anti-Patterns

- Treating academia and industry as single monolithic choices.
- Using salary, prestige, or title as the only comparison.
- Applying to many roles without understanding the evidence each role requires.
- Framing a transition as failure rather than a decision under constraints.
- Making unfocused requests for jobs or referrals without explaining fit; direct applications or vacancy inquiries can be appropriate when that is the stated route.
- Treating a first month as passive onboarding instead of an active evidence-gathering and relationship-building period.
- Assuming every offer term is negotiable or safe to raise in every context.

## Diagnostic Questions

- What does the user want their ordinary workweek to contain or avoid?
- Which constraints are nonnegotiable, and which are preferences?
- What evidence does the target role need that the user's academic materials do not yet show?
- Who can describe the role from direct experience?
- What small experiment would reduce uncertainty fastest?
- Which offer terms are must-haves, which are tradeable, and what evidence justifies each ask?
- What should the user learn in the first month that would confirm fit or reveal a structural mismatch?

## Output Patterns / Mini-Templates

Career comparison row:

| Path | Daily work | Success metrics | Entry evidence | Fit signals | Risks | Next experiment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fictional platform scientist role | Team experiments toward specified platform milestones | Reproducible data, decisions, timelines | Methods plus collaboration stories | Likes this team's experimental work | Topic decisions set by platform priorities | Ask recent hires how priorities and decision rights work |

Leaving-path decision memo:

1. Current problem: acute crisis, durable mismatch, structural risk, or unclear evidence.
2. Constraints: financial, visa, health, family, timing.
3. Options: stay with changes, pause, transfer, master out, leave, or parallel search.
4. Evidence still needed.
5. Lowest-regret next step.

Offer negotiation plan:

| Ask | Why it matters | Evidence or benchmark | Priority | Possible tradeoff |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Start date | Allows relocation and project handoff | Current contract end date | Must-have | Later review date |

First-month integration plan:

| Area | Action | Evidence of progress | Risk signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Key relationships | Meet manager, peers, and collaborators | Expectations and communication norms are explicit | Conflicting priorities with no owner |

## Examples

- Strong transition evidence: a project that shows the user can solve target-role problems with relevant constraints.
- Weak transition evidence: "I have a PhD, so I can do anything" without role-specific translation.
- Good informational question: "What does a strong first six months look like in this role, and what backgrounds struggle?"
- Strong negotiation framing: "I am asking for this term because it reduces a specific execution or transition risk, and I can trade on lower-priority items."
- Strong first-month plan: clarify success measures, keep commitments small and visible, ask for early feedback, and document uncertainties without overreacting.

## When not to apply this

Do not use this playbook for routine CV formatting or cover-letter drafting unless the main problem is translating across career paths.
