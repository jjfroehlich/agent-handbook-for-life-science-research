# Figure Legends

Use this when the user asks for figure captions, panel descriptions, figure-title logic, statistical annotation wording, or self-contained legend review.

## The job of this topic

A figure legend lets readers understand what the figure shows, how to map panels and symbols to evidence, and what comparison matters without relying on the main text.

## Default workflow

1. Identify the figure's purpose, supplied evidence and destination/stage from context. Ask only for missing details that change this caption.
2. Draft an accurate result title or a neutral subject/analysis title. A result-first opening is not compulsory.
3. Describe panels in order and define labels, colors, symbols, abbreviations, and conditions.
4. Include essential methods, sample size, replicate information, and statistical notation when supplied.
5. Add a brief finding explanation when the title does not supply it, or multiple panels/results need clarification. It is optional when the display is already clear; do not duplicate Results.
6. Adapt explanation and condensation to the actual stage and destination. A more explanatory submission legend can help, but final publication does not always require shortening. Keep necessary local context and retain displaced essential detail in an accessible location.
7. Check that every panel reference has a visible referent and every main-text claim points to the right panel.
8. Run a standalone-inspection pass: a reviewer should be able to inspect the figure without guessing the comparison, condition, symbol, or statistical convention.

## Decision rules

- If a legend only repeats axis labels, add the biological or analytical comparison.
- If the main text depends on the figure, ensure the legend supplies enough context to inspect the evidence.
- If statistical symbols are present, define the test or threshold only when supplied by the draft or figure.
- If a panel is not discussed in the text, decide whether it is needed or whether the text needs a callout.
- If the legend becomes a mini-results section, cut broad interpretation and keep orientation.
- If a schematic title leaves its purpose unclear, add the relevant workflow or subject context. A neutral title can be sufficient; do not invent a biological finding to replace it.
- If the user supplies no sample size, replicate, or statistical-test information, flag the missing fields rather than inventing them.
- If panel phrasing is inconsistent, use parallel structure for panels that make parallel comparisons.
- If removing a table would hide necessary exact values or distinct information, retain that access even when a related graph exists. Body prose should connect readers to the display rather than repeat its caption.

## Variants and edge cases

- Multi-panel figures need a consistent panel order and parallel phrasing.
- Supplementary legends may need additional procedural detail; use the information needed for that display rather than assume a universally longer format.
- Initial submission may benefit from extra orientation, but a finding explanation is not strictly necessary in every submission legend. Stage-specific preferences do not override the actual venue or the figure's interpretive needs.
- Schematics need definitions of visual conventions even when no statistics appear.
- Methods figures may need workflow steps rather than result claims.

## Anti-patterns

- Undefined colors, symbols, abbreviations, or error bars.
- Panel letters that do not match the visual sequence.
- Claiming significance without visible statistical support.
- Long legends that repeat the entire results paragraph.
- Treating every neutral subject/analysis title as defective or forcing a takeaway before the evidence supports one.
- Shortening a final legend so far that readers lose samples, conditions, necessary uncertainty or methods context.

## Diagnostic questions

- What should the reader learn from the figure?
- Can each panel be understood independently?
- Are conditions, controls, sample size, and statistics clear enough for inspection?
- Does the legend define every non-obvious visual element?
- Do main-text citations and legend panel order match the visual order?
- Does the legend support, but not overstate, the main-text claim?

## Output patterns / mini-templates

- Legend aid: `[Supported finding OR neutral subject/analysis title]. (A) What panel A shows and key condition. (B) What panel B compares. Relevant data/statistics note if supplied. Abbreviations and symbols.` Use only applicable parts.
- Revision note: `Define [symbol/color], add [condition/comparison], cut [duplicated interpretation], and align [panel] with main-text claim.`
- Missing-info note: `Needed before final legend: [sample size/replicates/statistical test/error bar definition/abbreviation definition].`

## Examples

Illustrative fixed facts: treatment increases marker-positive late-stage cells; panel A contains representative control and treated images, and panel B quantifies marker-positive cells. No sample size or statistical-test details are supplied. Do not infer these facts from the before caption.

Before: `Figure 2. Results of assay.`

After: `Figure 2. Treatment increases marker-positive late-stage cells. (A) Representative images of control and treated cells. (B) Quantification of marker-positive cells across the indicated conditions.`

Unresolved before finalization: the actual sample/replicate definitions and any tests or uncertainty notation used in the display. Do not infer independent replication from a cell count.

Changed condition: if the supplied facts establish only the assay layout and comparison, use `Figure 2. Marker-positive cells in control and treated samples.` Then describe the actual panels without asserting an increase. If the destination permits the concise caption and no extra finding explanation is needed, do not add one solely because this is an initial submission.

An optional check is to ask a reader to reconstruct the display from the legend and investigate mismatches. That proposed check is not completed comprehension evidence. Inspect figure and caption at their intended size for readable labels/marks; detailed visual design and export choices belong to the visualization workflow, with no universal font, width or placement recipe here.

## When not to apply this

Do not invent methods, sample sizes, tests or error-bar definitions. Ask for consequential missing details or mark them as unresolved; do not fill assumptions into a final caption as facts.
