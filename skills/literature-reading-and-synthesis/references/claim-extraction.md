# Claim Extraction

Use this when the user needs to identify claims, evidence, methods, limitations, figure logic, or whether a result is strong enough to reuse.

## Use this when...

- The user asks what a paper claims or how strong the evidence is.
- A figure, table, experiment, or results section needs unpacking.
- The user is preparing journal-club, presentation, or literature-review notes.
- The user needs a table that separates claims from support and interpretation.

## The job of this topic

Inspect the connection between a claim and its evidence. The six questions below are a reading scaffold; a focused request may need only the relevant observation, inference and missing check.

## Default workflow

1. Name the evidence unit: whole paper, figure, table, experiment, or section.
2. Select the relevant questions from this scaffold:
   - Why was this done?
   - What was done?
   - Why was it done that way?
   - What was found?
   - What do the authors think it means?
   - What should happen next, if the task calls for it? Distinguish the authors' proposal from your own.
3. For each major claim, record the supporting evidence object.
4. Separate results from interpretation.
5. Check alternative explanations, bias, limitations, confounders, and generalizability.
6. State the strongest supported conclusion and its consequential limit. Add a reuse decision when the user needs one.

## Decision rules

- If a paper is central to a research decision, inspect the methods and follow supplemental material or citations needed to resolve a consequential inference. A resolved narrow question need not trigger a broad citation trail. When necessary material is unavailable, identify what remains unassessed.
- If a figure is hard to understand, stop summarizing and decode the figure before writing the take-home.
- If the article is a review, extract field map and major positions rather than forcing a primary-research claim table.
- If the article is a method or resource paper, extract procedure, validation, scope, and reuse constraints.
- If critique focuses on wording or citation irritation, recenter on evidence, design, bias, limitations, and generalizability.
- Distinguish association from a causal explanation. An intervention can test causality, but off-target effects, measurement definitions and missing controls can leave the proposed mechanism unresolved.
- Treat an alternative explanation as a hypothesis to test, not a finding. Record the inspected figure/section and reading status beside consequential evidence notes.

## Appraising the relevant inference

Use only the checks that could change the requested conclusion. These diagnostics do not replace specialist statistical review or establish a preferred analysis from a method label.

- Identify what was independently sampled or assigned. Cells, repeated measurements or images from the same animal are not automatically independent biological replicates. Examine how dependence was handled before judging the effective evidence; do not infer the analysis from a plotted point count.
- Match the claim to its comparison. A before/after change alone may reflect time or practice. A significant result in one group and a nonsignificant result in another do not establish a difference between their responses; inspect the direct contrast and uncertainty.
- Read estimates and precision alongside tests. A nonsignificant result alone does not establish no effect; an equivalence claim needs an appropriate scientific margin and analysis. Neither a small P value nor a large sample alone establishes importance, truth or reproducibility. Do not invent missing estimates, margins or power calculations.
- For associations, inspect the display, relevant groups and influential observations. Pooled and within-group associations may answer different questions. Genuine extreme observations should not be removed because significance changes. Robust or rank methods and bootstrapping still have assumptions; assess the estimand, justified model and sensitivity rather than prescribing one universal replacement.
- Check whether selection and testing reuse information in a way that biases the inference. Reuse is not automatically invalid when selection and the test are independent under the null. Distinguish planned from exploratory analyses and examine the relevant comparison family; do not assume every analysis needs the same multiplicity adjustment or diagnose intent from the published result.
- Causal interpretation depends on design and assumptions. A manipulated variable, mediation analysis or causal verb does not by itself establish the proposed mechanism; consider alternative directions and common causes when relevant.

Keep criticism proportional: a missing reporting detail leaves a specific point unassessed, while an identified design problem can limit a specific conclusion. Scrutinize confirming and conflicting evidence comparably; venue prestige and agreement with your expectations do not establish validity.

## Variants and edge cases

- **Methods paper:** Procedure, assumptions, validation, input requirements, failure modes, and comparison baseline matter more than narrative arc.
- **Review article:** Extract the map of positions, debates, source clusters, and gaps.
- **Resource paper:** Extract data/tool scope, access conditions, quality controls, and appropriate reuse cases.
- **Controversial topic:** Record the conflict, competing interpretations, and whether the authors acknowledge uncertainty.
- **Low-priority alert:** Capture claim, evidence type, and relevance, then defer deeper appraisal.

## Anti-patterns

- Copying the abstract as the note.
- Recording the authors' interpretation without the evidence.
- Treating a publication as settled truth.
- Treating minor errors as fatal while ignoring design quality.
- Mixing claim, method, result, limitation, and speculation in one field.

## Diagnostic questions

- Does the note answer the user's evidence question without implying that uninspected material was assessed?
- Is each major claim tied to a concrete evidence object?
- Are result and interpretation separate?
- Are limitations, bias, confounders, and generalizability recorded?
- Does the note say what the user should do with the claim?
- Is criticism substantive rather than mostly stylistic?

## Examples

- For a central figure, decode the axes, comparisons, groups, statistics, legend, and method link before writing "this supports X."
- For a journal-club critique, list alternative explanations and confounders before deciding whether the claim is weak.
- For a methods paper, focus on what was validated and where the method may fail.

## When not to apply this

- Do not run full claim extraction when the user only needs a quick relevance screen.
- Do not use this as a peer-review template; use it to understand and synthesize evidence.
