# Choosing Problems

## Use This When

Use this playbook when the user is deciding which scientific problem is worth pursuing, assigning projects across career stages, or asking how to balance novelty, payoff, and tractability.

## The Job Of This Topic

Make problem choice explicit. A good answer should distinguish the value of the question from the feasibility of answering it, then show why one option is better than nearby alternatives.

## Default workflow

1. List the candidate problems and the decision horizon.
2. Compare expected knowledge gain, personal interest and feasibility with the user's actual tools, data, collaborators and time. Keep scientific contribution separate from personal attraction; neither is a measured utility score.
3. Deprioritize options that are both less feasible and less informative, while checking whether personal interest, training value or existing commitments give them a distinct role.
4. For the remaining options, check stage fit, portfolio role, and whether the project can be broken into smaller decisive steps.
5. Recommend a problem, a narrowed version of it, or a short exploration that would make the choice clearer.

## Decision rules

- Compare feasibility with expected increase in knowledge, then consider personal interest, training and commitments separately. A project better on the first two dimensions need not be the right choice for someone prioritizing a different purpose.
- Match risk to available support and horizon. Beginners often need early interpretable progress; later trainees may pursue less predictable questions; groups can divide grand challenges into smaller projects. Career stage is a useful clue, not a fixed assignment.
- State what answering the question would add relative to particular prior work. A gap or claim of disruption is insufficient by itself; useful replication, description, a resource or a contextual test can have a real contribution.
- Look across fields when an actual concept, method or analogy could help. Import to the present question or export an insight to another problem; explain the connection and the expertise it needs. Co-location, field count and citation associations do not establish useful integration.
- Distinguish a frontier unknown from a preparation gap or mismatch between the task and available strengths. Learning, help or a different entry point may fit; discomfort is neither a requirement nor evidence of depth. A problem can remain unsolved without making the investigator deficient.

## Variants And Edge Cases

- For interdisciplinary projects, weigh breadth against necessary depth and transfer cost. A conceptual link, tool reuse or public-data reanalysis can suffice without a large integrated team.
- For trainee projects, adapt scope to preparation, support and learning goals. A supervisor-supplied starter question or a useful course can help someone build foundations before framing a project independently; they should still learn why the question matters. Reading/discussion can suit a prepared learner, while practical skill training may save later effort. No one training sequence fits everyone.
- Set exploration time from actual freedom and resources; a laboratory's three-month search is one practice, not a universal delay. Include funding, living responsibilities and usable guidance in feasibility rather than treating dedication as a substitute for support.

## Anti-Patterns

- Choosing the hardest problem because it sounds most important.
- Choosing the easiest problem because it is already set up.
- Confusing a vague gap in the literature with a tractable research question.
- Treating discomfort as proof of depth when the missing piece is ordinary background preparation.

## Diagnostic Questions

- What would be learned if this project worked?
- What concrete observation would make the question more or less interesting?
- Which candidate is dominated by another on both feasibility and expected knowledge gain?
- What assumption or practice could this project revise?
- Is the project a good fit for the user's stage, time horizon, and available support?

## Output Patterns / Mini-Templates

Use a comparison only when it helps distinguish several options; a direct reasoned paragraph may suffice. Choose columns for the actual tradeoff rather than fill every field:

| Candidate | Expected contribution | Personal/training fit | Actual resources and time | Main uncertainty | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Option A | What answering it adds | Why this person would pursue it | Established versus unconfirmed capability | What could change the choice? | Pursue, narrow, check, or set aside |

## Examples

- If an idea offers substantial learning but is not presently feasible, compare a smaller entry point, suitable reuse/collaboration or postponement; do not invent access.
- A feasible but low-interest idea may be useful for training or support; assess its scientific contribution and obligations against alternative uses of time.
- If a framing feels stuck, identify the assumption constraining the question and whether the problem actually requires it; do not presume why other researchers use it.

## When Not To Apply This

Do not use problem-choice triage as a substitute for ethics review, safety review, field-specific technical feasibility, or statistical design.
