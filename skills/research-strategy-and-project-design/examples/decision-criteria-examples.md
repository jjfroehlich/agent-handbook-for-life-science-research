# Decision criteria examples

These fictional cases illustrate research commitments, not universal assay thresholds or findings in the user's study.

## A failed pilot does not answer the biological question

**Request:** “We have six weeks left to ask whether a heat pulse changes yeast division timing. The handling checks passed, but cells leave the field in both conditions. Should we scale up or stop?”

**Response:** Suspend scale-up: the present movies cannot support a reliable comparison of first-division times. Missing cells are not cells that failed to divide, and restricting the comparison to retained cells could select a different population in each condition. The biological question remains open.

If a specialist and instrument session are available, consider a bounded mounting test. Agree on the observation window and the evidence needed to trust cell identity and event detection in both conditions. Check that the revised mounting still permits division in mock-handled cells; retention alone is insufficient. Review the losses and what they imply for the comparison. If the repair remains inadequate or the necessary support is unavailable, pause this imaging route and compare another approach. Bulk growth would change the question and needs an explicit decision.

The duration of a repair comes from the available resources and the specific fault; it is not a fixed number of attempts that establishes biological absence.

## A controlled result can challenge a narrower prediction

**Request:** “Two perturbations gave no clear response. Is that enough to abandon the proposed mechanism?”

**Response:** The number of perturbations does not settle that. First establish whether they achieved the intended changes, whether the assay worked in the tested matrix, and whether the estimate is precise enough to challenge what the model predicted at that dose, time and cell state. Shared compensatory effects or a shared readout problem can survive two methods.

If those checks support the comparison and the uncertainty excludes the predicted response, revise that prediction or its domain. If the perturbation failed, repair or replace it; if the estimate still permits the predicted response, the result is unresolved. Then compare the value and cost of a more informative test with the strongest alternative direction. A practical decision to pause need not imply that the mechanism is absent everywhere.
