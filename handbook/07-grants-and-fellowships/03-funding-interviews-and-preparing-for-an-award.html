Funding interviews and preparing for an award

Prepare to discuss the proposal’s most important questions and its weakest points at interview. If the application succeeds, check the award’s terms with your institution before arranging the start of the work.

Prepare for the panel’s questions

Read the invitation alongside the submitted proposal and assessment criteria. Confirm the presentation time, question period, permitted materials, submission arrangements and interview format. Use those instructions to choose what fits. A previous applicant’s slide deck can show how they explained their project; it cannot establish this year’s rules.

Prepare a concise opening that explains the question and the work proposed to answer it. Why does the question matter? Which comparison is decisive, and why can this team carry it out? Leave time for the panel to examine those choices. In his account of an ERC panel, Burchardi recommends short, focused answers about contribution and feasibility, with backup material for likely questions [1, points 4 and 7].

Reread the application for assumptions the panel may question. If a facility has not confirmed access, explain how you would obtain it and what happens if it remains unavailable. If an aim depends on an earlier result, prepare to discuss other outcomes. Check the evidence for an untested assay and the basis of a tight cost estimate. State what you have already checked and what still needs to be arranged.

Choose backup slides by the question they answer: a control, a sampling plan, a dependency or the basis of a cost. Label them so you can reach the relevant evidence without searching through the whole deck. Detailed slide construction belongs in designing a presentation.

Rehearsing the interview

Rehearse aloud with someone who can challenge the science and someone less familiar with the project. Ask them to interrupt, request a simpler explanation and question a central assumption. Notice where you answer a different question, rely on an unexplained term or take too long to reach the point. Revise those passages, then rehearse again [3, §§ 1–2].

Listen to the whole question. Clarify an ambiguity if it changes the answer, then answer directly and explain your reasoning. If the answer is unknown, say so. Explain how the proposed work would investigate it, or why it lies outside the project’s scope. When discussing an alternative experiment, explain whether it answers the same question or changes what the project can establish.

Example: answer the limitation directly

This fictional proposal compares two preservation methods using paired aliquots from an existing collection of freshwater samples. The outcome is recovery of viable microbial isolates under specified culture conditions. Sample access is confirmed, but the comparison has not been performed. The applicant has experience with the culture workflow. No preliminary result establishes which preservation method works better.

Panel member: Your culture assay misses organisms that do not grow in those conditions. How will you know whether you have preserved the community?

Applicant: We will not be able to claim that we preserved the whole community. Our question is narrower: which method gives better recovery of isolates with this culture workflow? We will split each sample between the two methods and an untreated recovery control, then keep the culture conditions the same. That makes the comparison useful for choosing how to preserve samples for this collection. It leaves uncultured organisms and other culture conditions outside the conclusion.

Panel member: What if both methods give very low recovery?

Applicant: We would first look at the untreated control and the detection limits. If the control also gives poor recovery, the experiment may not tell us much about preservation. If the control works, low recovery would show a problem under the tested conditions, but it would not establish that the methods are equivalent. We would use the paired estimates and their uncertainty to decide whether the difference is resolved. If it is not, we would report that limit rather than select a winner from noise.

Test the presentation setup, including opening the files and sharing slides. For a remote interview, check that you can hear questions and know how to reconnect or contact the organizer if the connection fails. Arrange accessibility or technical support with the organizer and keep a permitted backup copy. For pacing, voice and general question handling, see delivering a talk and answering questions [3, § 3].

Read the decision before making commitments

If the application is unsuccessful, separate the funding decision from the lessons in the review. Identify whether the main concern was fit, scientific reasoning, feasibility or explanation. Decide what evidence or redesign would address it before polishing the same text. Check the scheme’s current rules before assuming that resubmission is available. Ask the funder or research office if those rules are unclear. Feedback can help improve the project even when another opportunity is the better next step [2].

If the proposal is selected, read what the notification actually confirms. It may begin a period of award preparation rather than settle every term. Bring the notification and proposed work to the institutional team responsible for research awards. Establish the agreed start date, funded activities, available resources, outstanding conditions and who has authority to make commitments. Check any proposed early spending through the applicable institutional and funder process; do not infer permission from an encouraging message.

Pina’s guide to EU research grants treats preparation of the grant agreement as substantive work for the researcher and institutional support staff. Its broader lesson is to understand the conditions attached to the award, including its financial form, rather than reading only the scientific description. The specific obligations come from the applicable agreement, not from a general guide [4, Rules 3–6].

Preparing to start

Reconcile the approved work with what is available now. A facility may have changed its schedule, a collaborator’s commitment may need renewal or the awarded budget may differ from the request. Identify which changes affect the scientific comparison and which affect only its timing. Bring a concrete proposal to the appropriate funder and institutional contacts when an adjustment needs agreement. Do not promise the original scope with resources that no longer support it [2].

Before the first substantive work, agree who owns the initial tasks, what records will be kept and when progress or problems will be reviewed. Include administrative colleagues where their work controls purchasing, appointments or reporting. For a collaboration, settle the partner contributions and access arrangements needed at the start; collaboration, authorship and credit covers those agreements in more detail.

Example: selection with an unresolved start condition

Later in the same fictional project, the proposal is selected for funding and award preparation begins. The core facility now reports an eight-week delay in access needed for the first experimental batch. The final start arrangements remain unconfirmed.

Decision. Hold the dependent sample-processing and purchasing commitments while the applicant and institutional grants officer seek agreement on the revised schedule. Do not substitute a different recovery assay merely because it is available sooner; that could change the comparison on which the award was assessed.

Action and owner. The applicant will obtain a revised access date from the core and show which milestones depend on it. The grants officer will establish what the award process permits and whether the proposed sequence or start date needs formal agreement. Together they will submit a specific adjustment with its scientific and budget consequences.

Work that can proceed. The team can prepare protocols and analysis documentation using resources already authorized for that preparation. This does not authorize new grant charges, appointments or processing of the unique samples.

Release condition. Begin the dependent work after the necessary award arrangements and facility access are confirmed. If access remains unavailable, assess an adequate alternative or seek an agreed change; financial authorization alone does not make the assay feasible.

Keep a short record of the agreed start arrangements. Name who will do the first tasks and which commitments are confirmed. Include reporting dates and where to raise a proposed change. The amount of coordination depends on the award: an individual fellowship usually needs less than a multi-institutional programme. Plan required outputs early and retain the records needed for reporting [4, Rules 7–10].
