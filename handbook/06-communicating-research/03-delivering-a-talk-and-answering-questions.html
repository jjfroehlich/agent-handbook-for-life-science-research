Delivering a talk and answering questions

Rehearse and revise

Rehearse the complete talk aloud while it is still easy to change. Listen for awkward wording and results whose explanation takes longer than expected. Where the move from one result to the next feels abrupt, check whether you have explained the question the next experiment answers. Revise the transition or reorder the material before trying another run. [3].

Time the complete talk, including the opening, transitions and close. If the run reaches the end of the allotted slot, remove a secondary result, shorten a detour or move a detail to material for discussion. Keep the comparison that makes the central claim credible, and leave the agreed time for questions. [2].

Mark a few timing checkpoints in your notes, and decide which slides you can skip if questions or interruptions use the spare time. Use a visible timer during the talk. If you fall behind, move to the prepared shorter version and preserve the conclusion. [1, p. 10].

Rehearse the shorter version, including what you will say between the slides that remain. Skipping a result may leave a later reference or explanation without its context.

Practice the beginning and end until you can deliver them without searching for the next sentence. They need not be memorized verbatim. The opening can introduce the question and help listeners follow the first result; the close can state the answer and what remains unresolved. If possible, rehearse with someone outside the immediate project, who is less likely to fill in missing explanations from memory. Ask what they took to be the main claim, which comparison supported it and where they lost the thread. [4].

Rehearsal tool: revise after each complete run

Run Observe Revise before the next run
First complete aloud run Where did a sentence become hard to say or a transition sound like a jump? Rewrite the spoken link; state the question that the next result answers.
Timed run Which material puts the close or discussion period at risk? Cut a supporting detour or reserve it for a question; do not compress the decisive comparison.
Listener run Can a colleague outside the immediate project identify the claim and explain the key comparison? Add the missing premise or replace a term that obscures the comparison.
Recovery run After a planned interruption, can the shorter route still reach the conclusion without dropping its decisive control? Skip a named secondary result and rehearse the bridging sentence into the next essential comparison.
Delivery check Can you speak to the displayed evidence without relying on a moving pointer or an unreadable note? Add a label or annotation, and simplify the spoken cue.

Record one run if you can. Check whether you rush through transitions or spend long stretches speaking to the screen. Notice whether a repeated movement distracts from what you are explaining. Choose a specific change to try in the next run. [3].

Explaining figures aloud

Before explaining a dense figure, tell the audience what to look at. Describe the observation and what you conclude from it, then introduce the next question: “This shows that the DNA is made. The remaining question is whether its encoded protein causes defence.” [3].

Give listeners time to read unfamiliar axes and locate the groups being compared before explaining the result. A pause after the display appears can help. Speak clearly enough for the room or call, at a volume you can sustain. If nerves make you rush, pause at the next transition and take a breath before continuing.

Use a pointer briefly to identify a feature, then hold it still or switch it off. Put essential arrows, labels or staged annotations in the displayed material so the explanation still works without it. Explain the visual material in the order in which you want it inspected, then turn back to the listeners. [3]; [2].

Room, equipment and remote setup

Before an in-person talk, check the room’s display and audio, or ask the host what is available. Confirm how your computer connects and whether you need an adapter. Open the deck you will use and check that the figures are legible from the audience’s seats. Test the slide controls, bring a backup copy and decide how you will continue if the pointer or clicker fails. Confirm whether questions will come during or after the talk and how much time is reserved for them. [3].

For a remote talk, test screen sharing, microphone and camera in the meeting software. Check what a participant can see and hear, and how you would reconnect if the connection drops. Ask the host about captions and question handling. Tell listeners whether to use chat or speak when both are available.

Keep additional slides ready for likely questions, such as a control or methods detail omitted from the main talk. Practice finding them and returning to the slide where you left off. [3].

Answering questions

Let the question finish. If its target is unclear, ask a short clarification or repeat the part you will answer: “Are you asking whether the protein, rather than the DNA intermediate, accounts for phage restriction?” Repeating the question lets the room hear it and gives you a moment to formulate the response. Then answer that part first, using the relevant comparison. [3].

If you do not know the answer, say so. Explain what the study can establish about the question and, when useful, suggest how the unresolved point could be tested. If a challenge reveals a limitation, acknowledge it and explain how it affects the conclusion. If it rests on a false premise, correct that premise before answering. When repeated interruptions prevent you from continuing, ask the chair to help or suggest returning to the discussion in the question period. [1, pp. 11–12].

When a question requires a separate conversation, say so and offer to continue after the session. If the chair closes the discussion, stop. [2].

The fictional Brake study below is inspired by Tang and colleagues (2024) [5].

Example: a focused answer to a mechanistic challenge

Question: “If the translation-disrupted Brake construct still makes the DNA intermediate, how do you know the DNA does not itself cause phage restriction?”

Answer: “The DNA could still contribute, but this experiment shows that it is not sufficient. When we block translation, the DNA is still produced, yet growth arrest and phage restriction disappear. Supplying the protein separately restores both. That is why we interpret the defence as protein-mediated.”
