Choosing a journal and submitting a paper

While planning the paper, identify the readers who would use its findings and journals that reach them. Return to that shortlist once the results and argument are settled, and compare each journal’s expectations with the evidence you have.

The examples continue the fictional Brake study, inspired by Tang and colleagues (2024) [10].

Choose a journal for the contribution

Match the paper to its readers

Summarize the contribution and identify who would use it. Match both the subject and kind of advance: a journal that publishes work on your organism may have little interest in the particular question or method. [1, pp. 175–180].

Read the aims, scope and article types, then several recent papers close to yours in purpose. Compare their questions, evidence and intended readers with your own. One similar article can suggest a candidate journal; a pattern across several papers gives a firmer basis for judging fit. [1, p. 178]; [3].

Use the journal’s editorial criteria to judge what kind of advance it expects. A broad journal may expect relevance beyond the immediate system; a methods journal will usually look for a useful capability and evidence of its performance. [3].

Ask a colleague familiar with the journals to read the abstract and main figures. Which journal would they recommend, and what additional evidence might its editors expect? [1, p. 178].

Make a realistic shortlist

Agree a first choice and a few alternatives with coauthors, weighing scientific fit, practical constraints and likely delay. An ambitious first submission can be reasonable when the case for that readership is clear and the authors accept the time cost of a possible rejection. [2, pp. 125–126]; [1, pp. 177–179].

First rule out journals whose charges you cannot cover, whose license conflicts with your requirements or whose format would force you to omit essential explanation. Then compare the remaining choices. When weighing delay, check whether advertised decision times cover only the initial editorial assessment or also external review. They are estimates, not promises. Recheck the chosen journal’s current instructions before submission.

Journal comparison

Record brief reasons for keeping or rejecting each journal, including any question that must be resolved before choosing it.

Question What to examine Decision to record
Who would use this paper? Scope and recent papers addressing comparable questions The readers and the contribution that interests them
Does the evidence meet the journal’s expectations? Article type, editorial criteria and comparable studies A good match, or a specific unresolved concern
Is this the right first choice? Scientific fit, practical constraints, likely delay and coauthor judgment First choice, alternative, or remove from the shortlist

Example: matching a biological finding to a journal

Brake reveals a previously unknown pathway in which an RNA template supplies a protein-coding defence gene.

Journal profile Relevant readership Assessment
General science, such as Science Readers interested in gene organization, expression and cellular defence, including researchers outside microbiology. The unexpected origin of a functional gene provides the case for broad interest. The sequence and genetic evidence must establish the proposed pathway.
Molecular microbiology Researchers investigating bacterial defence and phage–host interactions. A close fit for the pathway and the experiments establishing its function; also a natural audience for a narrower follow-up.
Microbial biotechnology Researchers seeking useful traits in production strains or other applications. The current study lacks application tests; an appealing use alone would not make this the strongest fit.

The team chooses Science as its ambitious first submission because the origin of the coding sequence could interest researchers beyond bacterial defence. A molecular-microbiology journal remains an alternative for readers focused on the pathway and its function.

Presubmission inquiries

Send a presubmission inquiry when an answer about scope, article type or expected validation would change where or how you submit. Check whether the journal accepts inquiries and use its requested route. [4].

Briefly explain the scientific question, finding and approach, adding the abstract if requested. Ask the question that affects your decision. For example, if your paper tests a method in one system, ask whether the journal would consider that evidence or expect validation in additional systems.

An encouraging reply does not guarantee external review. Keep the correspondence and refer to it accurately when submitting. You can contact an editor directly without a prior meeting or senior introduction. [4]; [1, p. 179].

Check the terms and requirements

Investigate unfamiliar journals

For an unfamiliar journal, verify its identity, publisher contacts, editorial board and stated review process. Check indexing and preservation claims with the named services, and verify directory listings or professional memberships at the organization’s own site. Confusingly similar journal names, unclear charges or promises of guaranteed acceptance warrant further investigation. [7].

Establish the total charges, when payment is due, available waivers, the publication license and the terms for sharing the work in a repository. Confirm that the proposed route meets your funder’s and institution’s requirements. A librarian or research-support office can help resolve unclear terms before you commit. Record the policy page and date consulted. [7].

Distinguish initial submission from later production

Read the instructions for the exact article type and submission stage. Some journals allow flexible initial formatting; others require specific forms or structures. Distinguish what is needed for initial assessment from what will be required later, while keeping editable text, figures and data ready for production. [5, pp. 8–10].

Check limits on text, figures and supporting material. If they prevent a clear account, reconsider the article type or journal before removing essential explanation. The reporting chapter helps decide what the scientific account needs.

Check policies on preprints, related manuscripts and previous dissemination. Disclose overlapping work where requested. Pursue alternative journals sequentially: the usual declaration requires that the manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere. [2, p. 126]; [5, p. 7].

Prepare the submission package

Assemble the agreed manuscript and supporting files

Assign a submission coordinator and agree on corresponding-author responsibilities. Circulate the submission version, resolve coauthor comments and confirm names, order, affiliations and contribution statements. Resolve authorship disputes before submission. [2, pp. 125–126].

Assemble the agreed manuscript, figures, legends, tables, supporting files and forms in one submission folder. Label versions clearly and assign the remaining checks to named coauthors.

Start repository preparation early because metadata and access queries can delay release. Before submission, test accession numbers and reviewer links, check access permissions, and identify the code version accompanying the paper. [8, p. 5].

Check which declarations apply to the study and collect them from the responsible authors, including funding, conflicts of interest, ethics, consent, contributions and data availability as required. Include requested reporting checklists and follow anonymization instructions for blinded review.

Writing the cover letter

Use the cover letter to explain what was unresolved, what the paper establishes and why the finding matters to the journal’s readers. Draw on the abstract, keeping the findings that establish the advance and omitting secondary results or unnecessary technical detail. A concise letter, often about a page, is usually sufficient unless the journal specifies otherwise. [5, pp. 4–7]; [6].

Open with the title and article type, then state the scientific advance early. For a broad readership, explain how the finding changes understanding of a wider question. For a specialist readership, identify the particular uncertainty it resolves. Mention earlier work in the journal when the connection helps explain the contribution. [5, pp. 5–6]; [1, pp. 179–180].

Include requested declarations and relevant editorial information, such as an earlier inquiry, related manuscript or potential reviewer conflict. Use designated portal fields where provided. If you claim to be first, check the relevant prior work and state exactly what is new. [5, pp. 5–7].

Example: a discovery paper and a narrower follow-up

The letters use the same genetic findings in two different publication contexts. Both assume coauthor approval and exclusive submission.

Discovery paper Follow-up paper
Starting knowledge The route from an RNA template to a functional defence gene is unknown. The pathway is known; the effector’s function in Brake remains unresolved.
Main contribution Establishes an unexpected way of producing a protein-coding gene. Distinguishes the protein’s role from a possible direct effect of the DNA product.
Reason to read Changes how researchers can look for functional genes and defence effectors. Resolves a mechanistic question for researchers studying bacterial defence.

Discovery paper — letter to Science

Dear Editor,

Please consider our manuscript, “An RNA-templated gene arrests bacterial growth and restricts phage propagation,” as a research article in Science.

We identify a bacterial defence pathway that assembles its protein-coding gene from an RNA template. Brake contains a reverse transcriptase and an RNA template, but no recognizable effector gene in its genomic sequence. We show that repeated RNA-derived DNA creates a continuous coding sequence whose protein product arrests bacterial growth and restricts phage propagation.

Genetic tests connect gene assembly to function. Blocking translation preserves DNA production but abolishes growth arrest and phage restriction; expressing the protein separately restores both. These experiments identify the protein as the defence effector and distinguish its activity from a direct effect of the DNA product.

The discovery connects bacterial immunity to an unexpected route for producing a functional gene. It suggests that genome annotation can miss an effector whose coding sequence emerges only after DNA assembly, a possibility relevant to studies of gene organization and expression beyond bacterial defence.

All authors have approved the manuscript, and it is not under consideration elsewhere.

Thank you for considering our manuscript.

Sincerely,
[Corresponding author], on behalf of the authors

Follow-up paper — letter to a molecular-microbiology journal

Dear Editor,

Please consider our manuscript, “An RNA-derived protein mediates phage restriction in Brake,” as a research article in [Journal].

RNA-templated antiviral gene synthesis provides a route to bacterial defence, but the product responsible for defence in Brake remains unresolved. We distinguish a protein-mediated effect from a possible direct effect of its RNA-derived DNA product.

Preventing translation abolishes phage restriction while preserving DNA production. Expressing the protein separately restores restriction and growth arrest. Together, these experiments identify the Brake protein as the functional effector.

By linking the DNA product to an active protein, the work explains how a reverse-transcriptase-associated defence region restricts phage propagation. It should interest researchers investigating the function of these regions and their encoded effectors. The protein’s molecular target remains an open question.

All authors have approved the manuscript, and it is not under consideration elsewhere.

Thank you for considering our manuscript.

Sincerely,
[Corresponding author], on behalf of the authors

The same experiments make different contributions depending on what was already known. In the discovery letter, the genetic tests help establish the new pathway; in the follow-up, they resolve the identity of its effector.

Suggest reviewers for complementary expertise

Suggest reviewers with complementary expertise in the paper’s main methods and scientific questions. Give a short reason for each nomination and follow the journal’s requested number and information fields. [6].

Check potential conflicts involving institutional colleagues, recent collaborators, mentors or mentees. Follow the journal’s definitions, disclose uncertain relationships and verify contact details independently. [9, p. 4].

For permitted exclusions, give a concise reason relevant to fair review. Scientific disagreement alone does not establish bias. Keep exclusions proportionate; the editor makes the appointments. [5, p. 6]; [9, p. 4]; [3].

Submit and follow the paper

Check the actual submission

Enter metadata from the agreed manuscript, checking author order, affiliations, funding and special characters after pasting. Assign files to the correct categories and remove superseded uploads before generating the review PDF.

Inspect the assembled review PDF for text and figure order, legibility, equations and symbols. Open supporting files and check any figures converted or compressed by the system. [5, pp. 8–10].

Once the assembled files are correct, complete the final approval step and retain the receipt, manuscript identifier and submitted files. Share them with coauthors and agree who monitors correspondence. If no receipt arrives, check the portal or contact the office before submitting again.

Following the submission

Journal staff may return a submission for missing files, declarations or unreadable material. Supply the correction with the manuscript identifier. Direct portal problems to staff and scientific questions to the handling editor. [5, p. 3].

The editor assesses fit, contribution and whether the evidence warrants review. Read the reason for an early rejection before deciding what to change. After external review, the reports inform the editor’s decision. [3].

Follow the journal’s timing guidance. If an expected interval has passed, or a deadline makes an update necessary, send a brief inquiry with the manuscript identifier, submission date and question. Avoid parallel inquiries to several editors. [4].

If you discover a material error after submission, tell the editor promptly, identify the affected files or conclusions and ask how to supply the correction. Keep the coauthors informed. When a decision arrives, circulate the complete message and reports. For a requested revision, continue with Responding to peer review, which covers revision planning and the response letter.

For a rejection or transfer offer, see Rejection, transfer and published reviews.

Before you submit

  • The journal and article type suit the contribution and intended readers; coauthors agree on the choice.
  • Publication costs, rights and applicable institutional or funder requirements are resolved.
  • The submitted version, author information and declarations are agreed and complete.
  • The cover letter explains the contribution and journal fit; reviewer suggestions provide relevant, independent expertise.
  • Figures, supporting files and data or code access work in the form supplied for review.
  • The assembled submission has been checked, and the team knows who will confirm receipt and monitor correspondence.
