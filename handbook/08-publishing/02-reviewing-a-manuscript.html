Reviewing a manuscript

Explain what the paper establishes and identify any problems that affect its conclusions. For each concern, tell the authors what needs to be resolved and explain to the editor how it affects your recommendation. [1].

Decide whether to accept

Read the invitation, abstract and reviewer instructions before accepting. Consider whether you can assess the central methods and conclusions within the requested time. You need not cover every technique in a multidisciplinary paper: tell the editor which parts you can evaluate and where another reviewer is needed. If you cannot take the assignment, decline promptly and, when possible, suggest suitable alternatives. [2, rules 1 and 5]; [10].

Disclose relationships or interests that could affect—or reasonably appear to affect—your judgment. These can include recent collaboration, supervision, financial interests or close competition. Follow the journal’s definitions and ask the editor about uncertain cases before reading further. If a conflict becomes apparent only after you receive the manuscript, pause the review and contact the editor. [3, pp. 4–5].

Check what the journal asks reviewers to judge. Some decisions depend mainly on technical soundness; others also require a particular advance or relevance to a defined readership. Distinguish disagreement with a conclusion from lack of interest in the question. A result that challenges your preferred explanation deserves the same examination of methods and evidence as one that supports it. [2]; [3, pp. 5–6].

Confidentiality and co-review

Establish whether your report will be published, whether your identity will be disclosed, and how communication with authors is handled. Published reports can be anonymous; signing a review and publishing it are separate choices. Follow the agreed process, including any later request to share a report. [3, p. 3].

Keep the submission and review correspondence confidential. Do not circulate them to colleagues, use unpublished information in your own work or contact the authors directly without the journal’s permission. Before involving a trainee or specialist, obtain the editor’s agreement and arrange how their contribution will be acknowledged. The invited reviewer remains responsible for the submitted report. [3, pp. 5 and 8].

Apply the same confidentiality check to external tools. Before entering manuscript material or an identifiable draft report into an AI service, check the journal’s current policy, the service’s handling of the content and any required disclosure. Ask the editor if permission is unclear. Permission to use a tool does not transfer responsibility for the assessment or its accuracy.

If you can no longer meet the agreed deadline, tell the editor early and propose a date you can meet. [2, rule 1].

Assess the manuscript

Read the complete submission, including relevant supporting material. Summarize the question, approach and main finding in a few sentences, then assess which weaknesses affect that finding. Check apparent omissions elsewhere in the submission before raising them. [4]; [9].

Return to the results on which the main conclusions depend. Read each figure with its legend and the relevant Methods. Note where a description is missing and where you have found evidence of a problem. “The legend does not identify the experimental unit” calls for clarification; “The analysis treats technical measurements as independent experiments” requires evidence that this is how the analysis was performed. [4]; [6].

Examine the evidence needed for the claim

Ask whether the design distinguishes the proposed explanation from plausible alternatives. Identify the role of the controls and whether the intervention changes other features that could explain the result. When important information is missing, identify the comparison you cannot assess and request the relevant detail. The whole-paper assessment chapter develops this reasoning in more detail. [6].

For quantitative conclusions, first identify the comparison and its independent units, including any pairing or repeated measurements. Check the uncertainty around the estimated effect. Examine how observations were selected and whether the analysis accounts for testing many outcomes. If an analysis was chosen after seeing the results, consider how that choice affects the conclusion. A significant result in one group and a non-significant result in another do not establish a difference between groups; non-significance alone does not establish that an effect is absent. If you cannot assess a statistical issue that could change a main conclusion, ask the editor for specialist review. [6]; [3, p. 6].

Inspect the supporting data and code to the extent needed for your assessment and expected by the journal. Check that the files are accessible, identifiable and connected to the reported results. If access is restricted, assess the stated reason and available review arrangements rather than assuming unrestricted release is possible. Tell the editor when inaccessible material prevents an important check. [4].

When novelty or interpretation depends on an earlier publication, check the relevant passage in that paper. If the manuscript overlooks it, explain the consequence: perhaps a claimed discovery was already reported, or an apparent disagreement arises from different experimental conditions. Give the reference and the specific connection. A list of loosely related papers, especially your own, does not establish a substantive omission. [1, pp. 1029–1030]; [3, p. 7].

Distinguish essential revisions from extensions

Sort your concerns by their effect on the paper’s conclusions and your ability to assess them. A major concern challenges an important conclusion or the claimed contribution, or prevents you from judging whether they are supported. A minor concern is a local clarification or presentation problem that leaves the main conclusions intact. The amount of work is a separate question: an unsupported claim may be fixed by deleting a sentence, while an optional experiment could take months. [1, pp. 1028–1029]; [5].

State what remains unresolved before proposing a remedy. If the Methods omit information, ask for it. If the analysis does not answer the stated comparison, explain the mismatch. If the evidence supports a narrower conclusion, identify that option. Make clear when a concern prevents you from assessing the paper, rather than presenting your suspicion as an established flaw. [1, pp. 1029–1030]; [7].

Request experiments for a stated reason

An additional experiment is justified when its answer is needed for a claim the paper retains. Explain which alternative it would distinguish or which essential observation is missing. You can suggest a design, but allow another valid way to resolve the concern. Sometimes an existing analysis, a clearer explanation or a narrower claim is sufficient. [3, p. 7]; [7].

Consider whether the requested work is needed for the paper’s conclusion or asks a new question. Identifying a protein’s molecular target may be valuable without being necessary to establish its role in a phenotype; label such work as an optional future direction. For an essential concern, consider whether a different analysis or narrower claim could resolve it without costly new experiments. If neither would leave the paper’s conclusions supported, explain to the editor which evidence is still needed. [8]; [9].

Write the report

Use the journal’s form. Where the format is open, begin with a short summary of the question, main result and overall assessment, then present numbered major concerns followed by minor points. Identify strengths precisely—for example, a comparison that separates two competing explanations—rather than adding generic praise. You do not need to manufacture a list of weaknesses in a sound paper. [1, pp. 1028–1029]; [5].

Writing specific comments

For each substantive concern, identify the passage or figure, explain the problem and state what would resolve it. Quote a short phrase when its wording is the issue. If a comment contains several independently answerable questions, split them. Use continuous numbering or a clear section scheme so the response can refer to each point. [1, pp. 1029–1030]; [5].

Write directly about the manuscript. “Figure 2 does not distinguish these explanations because…” is more useful than a judgment about the authors’ competence. Be equally clear when the problem is serious: courteous wording should still let the reader recognize a conclusion you consider unsupported. When you may have misunderstood, say what you could not determine and ask the authors to clarify it. [3, pp. 6–7]; [7].

Concentrate language comments on scientific meaning and readability. Show where an ambiguous sentence changes the interpretation or a missing transition obscures the argument. Correcting every stylistic preference can bury more important concerns; widespread language problems can be described with a few representative examples. [3, pp. 6–7].

Example: a report on the Brake manuscript

Brake is the fictional bacterial defence study introduced in Planning the paper, inspired by Tang and colleagues (2024) [11]. Here a submitted draft describes an RNA-derived DNA intermediate that encodes a growth-arrest protein. The experiments connect protein production to phage restriction, but recovery of infected cells was not measured. The draft nevertheless claims survival, gives an ambiguous replication description and introduces the rationale for a key control only in the Discussion.

Comments to the authors

The manuscript describes Brake, a bacterial defence system in which reverse transcription of an RNA template produces a protein-coding DNA intermediate. Disrupting translation preserves DNA production but removes growth arrest and phage restriction; separate expression of the protein restores both. This comparison provides strong support for a protein-mediated defence mechanism. The main concerns are the inference about infected-cell survival and the description of replication in the phage-yield analysis.

Major concerns

  1. The abstract states that Brake enables infected cells to survive. Figure 4 shows growth arrest during observation, but I could not find a measurement of recovery after infection. These observations do not establish survival. Please remove that conclusion and describe the observed growth arrest, or provide evidence of recovery if such measurements are available. The RNA-to-DNA-to-protein pathway remains an important finding without the survival claim.

  2. Figure 1B reports a reduction in infectious phage yield, but the legend’s description of replicates does not let me assess the uncertainty around that estimate. Please state how many independently started cultures were tested for each genotype, whether genotypes were assayed together on the same days, and how technical plaque measurements were combined. The Methods should explain which values entered the comparison and how any matching by day was handled. If the current analysis already accounts for this design, a clear description would resolve the concern; otherwise, please revise the analysis and the resulting estimate and interval.

Minor point

  1. The reason for preserving DNA production in the translation-disrupted construct is introduced in the Discussion. Please explain it when introducing Figure 3A. This would make it easier to see how the loss of restriction in Figure 3B, followed by complementation in Figure 3C, separates the protein’s contribution from loss of the DNA intermediate.

Optional future direction

Identifying the protein’s molecular target would help explain how growth arrest occurs. I do not consider this necessary for the present paper, provided the mechanistic conclusion remains at the level established by the translation and complementation experiments.

The replication concern cannot be settled until the reviewer knows how the observations were analyzed. The survival concern is already clear: it requires a change to the conclusion or additional evidence, even though removing the claim would take little work.

Confidential comments to the editor

I recommend revision. The evidence connecting an RNA-derived DNA intermediate to a defence protein is compelling, particularly the separation of DNA production from protein function in Figure 3. The survival claim should be removed unless supported by additional evidence, and I need the replication and analysis details described in point 2 before reaching a final judgment on the phage-yield comparison. I would be willing to assess a revised manuscript addressing these concerns. Identification of the protein’s molecular target is not a condition of my recommendation.

Give the editor a consistent recommendation

In the editor’s recommendation field, explain whether the problem can be corrected, requires a different study design or concerns the journal’s scope and expected advance. State any clarification you need before deciding. Do not request a substantial revision if you would still recommend rejection after the authors resolved your concerns. [1, p. 1030]; [10]; [9].

The editor makes the publication decision, but may ask for your assessment of both scientific merit and journal fit. Explain which criterion underlies a negative recommendation. A sound paper may fall outside the journal’s scope or offer less of an advance than it requires. A result that would interest its readers may still lack the evidence needed for publication. [2]; [9].

Keep scientific criticisms visible to the authors. A favorable author-facing report paired with a hidden negative assessment leaves them unable to respond and makes the editor’s decision harder. Use confidential comments for matters requiring editorial handling, such as a competing interest, a limit to your expertise or a suspected ethical irregularity. [3, pp. 5–6]; [1, p. 1030].

For a suspected irregularity, describe the observation and give precise locations or a relevant reference. Do not turn a suspicion into an accusation or conduct your own investigation with the authors. Ask the editor how to proceed. A substantive problem may need correction regardless of its cause; establishing misconduct is a separate matter. [3, p. 5].

Review the revision

Read the decision letter, your earlier report and the response alongside the revised manuscript. For each substantive point, assess whether the underlying concern has been resolved. The authors may have used a different analysis, supplied an explanation you overlooked or narrowed the conclusion. Judge that answer on its merits rather than requiring your preferred solution. [3, p. 8]; [9].

Check the revised evidence and wording, not just the response letter. A clear reply may describe a change that has not reached the manuscript, or a new analysis may alter the interpretation elsewhere. If an experiment remains inconclusive, reassess what the retained claim requires. [9].

Avoid adding a new list of desirable experiments after the authors have addressed the original review. A revision can introduce a problem, and you may discover a serious issue missed earlier; raise it with a specific explanation of why it now affects your assessment. If your original criticism was mistaken, acknowledge that and withdraw it. [3, p. 8]; [9].

If another journal asks you to review a manuscript you have seen before, tell the editor about your earlier involvement. Assess the current version afresh against the new journal’s criteria. Obtain the original journal’s permission before sharing your earlier report. [3, p. 6].

Tell the editor which important concerns are resolved, what remains and how that affects your recommendation. When no substantive issue remains, a short report can be sufficient. [10]; [9].

Before submitting the report

  • The summary identifies the paper’s contribution and the evidence that matters to the assessment.
  • Each major concern has a location, a reason and a clear criterion for resolution.
  • Essential revisions are distinguished from optional extensions and stylistic preferences.
  • Scientific criticisms in the recommendation are also visible to the authors; confidential matters are directed to the editor.
  • The report states any limits to the review and meets the journal’s instructions on conflicts, collaboration, confidentiality and disclosure.
