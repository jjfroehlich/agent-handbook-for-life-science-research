Drafting, reviewing and revising a paper

Writing can reveal that you have not explained why an experiment supports a conclusion. Resolve such problems before polishing sentences. As the draft develops, check the evidence, arrange the results so readers can follow the reasoning, and then edit the paragraphs and sentences. New findings or feedback may require you to revisit earlier decisions.

The examples use the fictional Brake study, inspired by Tang and colleagues (2024) [12].

Starting a draft

Choose a passage you can develop

If the question and figures are settled, start with a Results passage whose comparison you understand well. Explain the finding and draft the corresponding Methods from the records. [1]; [2].

If you have results but are unsure how they fit together, draft a working title and a short statement of what the study adds. Sketch the question and background for the Introduction. Then check the figures: do they support the answer you have proposed? [3].

State the paragraph’s purpose, then develop it with the figures, analysis outputs and sources beside you. Mark missing information precisely—for example, “confirm which samples enter this comparison”—and continue where you can. [4]; [2].

Make writing sessions manageable

Choose a task you can tackle in the available writing time: draft one result, revise a transition or check a Discussion claim. If you stall, make the task smaller. Allow a longer session when you need to reread the whole argument. [5]; [2].

If sentence polishing prevents progress, explain the finding aloud or write an informal version before refining the terminology. Correct errors that block your thinking, but leave equivalent wording choices for later. [5]; [6].

When stuck, ask why. If you do not understand a result, inspect it or discuss it with a collaborator. If only the opening sentence is troublesome, draft the evidence and return to it. A break may help when concentration has faded. A shared writing session or an agreed date for sending a draft can help you return to the work. [5]; [2].

Read for the question and answer

Read the draft through before making extensive local edits. If you have just finished writing it, a break can help you notice what the page actually says. Record places where the explanation stops making sense, but continue far enough to see whether the missing information appears later. [8, pp. 124–126]; [14, rule 8].

Write a brief account of the paper’s question, answer and decisive evidence. Include the condition or unresolved issue that most limits the answer. Then compare that account with the title, abstract, final Introduction paragraph and Discussion. These passages can differ in detail, but they should identify the same contribution. An abstract that promises a mechanism while the Results establish an association creates a substantive discrepancy, even if each section reads smoothly on its own. [4, rules 1 and 5–8]; [9, tips 4 and 9].

Ask whether the answer addresses the question the Introduction led readers to expect. Sometimes the experiments have resolved a narrower question, or an unexpected finding has become the main contribution. In that case, revise the framing without presenting an explanation developed afterward as the original prediction. If the answer is supported but difficult to recognize, identify the missing connection before deciding to collect more data. [14, rules 1–5]; [15].

Check what the paper adds to existing knowledge. If the contribution depends on differing from an earlier study, reread that study’s relevant result and method. Did it use the same system, measurement and intervention? A difference may explain why the findings disagree, or show that your work extends a finding to another setting. See comparing with previous work. [9, tips 1 and 10].

Ask a colleague who is less immersed in the project to explain the paper’s question and answer. If their account differs from yours, reread the passage that led to it and check whether the problem lies in the explanation or the evidence. [4, rules 2 and 10]; [2].

Checking claims against the evidence

Start with the claims needed for the paper’s main conclusion. Follow each claim through the cited panels and legend to the Methods, supporting material and analysis. What was measured, what was compared, and why does that comparison support the claim? Pay particular attention when the conclusion combines several experiments or proposes a biological explanation for a measurement. [14, rules 4–5]; [9, tips 6 and 9].

A legend may omit the meaning of n even though the analysis uses the correct units. Check the analysis before deciding whether the problem is an unclear description or repeated measurements treated as independent samples. [15]; [16].

Reconstruct the comparison

For a quantitative claim, identify the measured quantity, reference group, sampled or treated units and any pairing or nesting. Then check which observations entered the analysis and how the reported effect and uncertainty were calculated. A label such as “six replicates” does not explain whether the six are independently treated cultures, measurements within a culture or matched experimental runs. The appropriate unit follows from the design and the intended inference. [16, pp. 1–3].

Follow the same samples through exclusions, transformations and summaries. If the reported counts differ between a figure and Methods, establish whether different subsets were intended and whether the distinction is explained. Reconcile the reported number with the final analysis output rather than choosing whichever version appears more plausible. For a ratio or fold change, check its direction and denominator as well as its magnitude. See reporting quantitative findings. [9, tips 15–17]; [17, pp. 1514–1515].

Examine whether the analysis answers the comparison stated in the prose. A significant change in one condition and a non-significant change in another do not establish that the changes differ. Likewise, an imprecise estimate may remain compatible with a biologically substantial effect. If a claim depends on the absence of that effect, a non-significant test alone cannot supply the required evidence. [15].

Test the interpretation against an alternative

Choose a plausible alternative that would change an important conclusion and identify what distinguishes it from the preferred explanation. For an intervention experiment, ask what else the intervention changes and which control separates those effects. If the two explanations predict the same observed result, that experiment cannot choose between them. [15]; [9, tip 9].

Include findings that challenge the preferred explanation. Check whether the difference reflects conditions, samples or analytical choices; if it persists, decide how it limits the answer. Preserve the distinction between a prediction tested as planned and an explanation suggested by inspecting the results. In particular, selecting observations because they show an effect and then using them to confirm that effect can make apparently strong support circular. [15].

A study can establish that a protein is required for a phenotype without identifying its molecular target. But if the perturbation also removes an upstream product, the loss of the phenotype could result from either change. Further evidence would be needed to distinguish them. Decide which unresolved question affects the conclusion you intend to retain, and update the claim inventory accordingly. [9, tips 4 and 9]; [4, rule 7].

Figures and legends

Read the figures and legends independently of the main text, then compare the inference they invite with the analysis actually performed. Visual grouping can suggest a comparison the authors never tested; connecting points can imply pairing that did not occur. Check whether the display reveals the variation needed to assess the summary and whether exclusions or overlapping points hide relevant observations. [17, figure 8].

For a schematic, follow the material or process from beginning to end. Check that symbols, colors and names remain consistent and that the sequence agrees with Methods. [18].

For light-microscopy results, compare the selected image with the range of observations it is meant to illustrate. Check that readers can identify the specimen, channels and scale, and trace image processing into the reported measurements. Example input and output data can expose a segmentation or processing problem that a polished final image conceals. Use the applicable Schmied and colleagues (2024) [19] checklist to investigate missing information; it does not establish that the experiment itself was well designed.

Inspect the assembled figures at their intended reading size for unreadable labels and inconsistent colors. [9, tips 31–32].

Deciding what to revise

For each concern, name the passage, explain the problem and state what needs checking. Replace “Strengthen the Discussion” with a task such as “The abstract infers survival from growth arrest; check whether recovery was measured and revise the claim accordingly.” [14, rules 8–10].

If the evidence supports the claim but the explanation is incomplete, clarify the comparison or explain what a control rules out. Put any needed background before the result that depends on it. If the analysis is wrong, correct it and reassess the claim before rewriting the conclusion. If essential evidence is absent, obtain it or narrow the claim. Another interesting question can remain future work when its answer is not needed for the conclusion. [4, rules 7–8]; [15]; [9, tips 4 and 9].

Example: assessing the complete Brake draft

An early draft of the fictional Brake study, inspired by Tang and colleagues (2024) [12], describes RNA-templated gene assembly, growth arrest and phage restriction. Its abstract additionally claims survival, although recovery was not measured. The numerical result uses the handbook’s illustrative dataset.

Draft packet

The title is An RNA-templated gene arrests bacterial growth and restricts phage propagation. The Introduction asks what the reverse transcriptase produces and how that product contributes to defence. Figures 1–4 respectively establish phage restriction, identify the protein-coding DNA product, test the protein’s function and examine activity across the tested phages.

Abstract, final sentence

By arresting growth during infection, Brake enables infected bacteria to survive phage attack.

Figure 1B, display and legend excerpt

The plot shows endpoint infectious phage A titres for empty vector, intact Brake and inactive reverse transcriptase. Lines join observations across the three genotypes. The Results report a 79-fold reduction for intact Brake relative to empty vector, with a 95% confidence interval of 55–116-fold.

Endpoint infectious titres for the three genotypes. Six replicates were measured in duplicate; lines connect matched measurements.

Figure 3, Results excerpt

We next disrupted translation of the Brake protein. DNA production was retained (Figure 3A), but growth arrest and phage restriction were lost (Figure 3B). Expressing the protein separately restored both phenotypes (Figure 3C).

Only in the Discussion does the draft explain that retaining DNA production is necessary to distinguish the protein’s contribution from an effect of losing the upstream DNA product.

Checks against the full manuscript and records

The author checks the cell-tracking records and Methods behind Figure 4B: growth arrest was observed, but subsequent recovery was not measured. The phage assays establish restriction of A and B; C remains productive. None of these findings measures whether an individual infected cell survives.

For Figure 1B, the culture log records one independently started culture per genotype on each of six experimental days. Technical duplicate plaque counts were combined into one titre per culture. The analysis already compares intact Brake with empty vector within each day, using six log10 titre ratios to estimate the mean and a two-sided Student t confidence interval with five degrees of freedom. Converting back to a ratio and taking the reciprocal reproduces the reported fold reduction and interval. The Methods give this analysis; the legend fails to identify its units clearly.

For Figure 3, the author checks the construct description and product measurements against the panels. The translation-disrupting change preserves the RNA and DNA products, and separate protein expression restores arrest and restriction. The control needed for the protein inference is present. Its purpose becomes apparent too late in the written explanation.

Author’s assessment

The checked evidence supports an RNA-templated route to a protein effector of growth arrest and phage restriction. The repairs are specific: remove the unsupported survival claim, clarify the six day-matched cultures and comparison in Figure 1B, and move the protein-versus-DNA rationale before the Figure 3 results. These repairs require no repeat experiment on the records checked here. The protein’s molecular target and recovery of arrested cells remain unresolved.

Prioritized revision plan

Work and responsibility Completion criterion
1. Settle the claim. The lead author and coauthors retain gene assembly, growth arrest and phage restriction as the contribution. Remove the survival inference throughout the manuscript. Title, abstract and Discussion agree; none infers survival or irreversible death from growth arrest. Recovery remains an explicit unresolved question.
2. Clarify Figure 1B. The analyst verifies the culture log, plotted values and analysis; the figure author clarifies cultures, technical duplicates and day matching. Proceed alongside task 1. Figure, legend, Methods and Results agree: six cultures per genotype; six day-matched contrasts; 79-fold reduction (95% confidence interval, 55–116-fold).
3. Explain the Figure 3 test. The Results author introduces the protein-versus-DNA question before the perturbation results. The contributing experimentalist checks the explanation against panels 3A–C. Readers can explain why retained DNA production matters and how complementation supports the protein’s role. The wording does not identify an unmeasured molecular target.
4. Review the assembled revision. After the changes are integrated, the lead author circulates one version with the revised figures and supporting material. Technical contributors check their results; a colleague less familiar with the project reconstructs the argument. The scientific concerns are resolved in the manuscript itself, and no stale claim, inconsistent legend or broken transition remains elsewhere. Any new substantive concern returns to the plan.

Two targeted repairs

Replace the abstract’s survival sentence with a conclusion consistent with the paper’s discovery:

These findings reveal a defence pathway whose effector gene is assembled from an RNA template.

Replace the Figure 3 paragraph’s first sentence with the question its controls answer:

We next asked whether the protein encoded by the RNA-derived DNA mediates defence. We disrupted translation while preserving the RNA and DNA products, allowing us to test the protein’s contribution without removing the upstream products.

The existing Figure 3 results then provide the answer. Had the translation-disrupting change also prevented DNA production, moving this explanation would not resolve the concern: the evidence would no longer distinguish the protein’s contribution from loss of the upstream product.

Revise the argument and organization

From the argument to the paragraphs

Once you know which claims the evidence supports, decide how to explain them. Check the paragraph order before polishing passages that may move or disappear. If rearranging the draft reveals a claim you cannot explain from the evidence, investigate it before continuing. [7]; [4].

Reviewing the paragraph order

Summarize each paragraph’s point in one sentence. Read these sentences in sequence to find repeated points, paragraphs trying to do too much and missing steps in the reasoning. Revise the paragraph order before editing individual sentences. [3]; [4].

Example: reorganizing a Results draft

The Brake draft follows the order in which experiments were completed. Protein expression appears before readers know why that protein was tested. The paragraph summaries below expose where the explanation arrives too late.

Paragraphs in the draft

  1. Expressing the Brake protein arrests growth.
  2. Details of expression-vector construction.
  3. Intact Brake restricts phage output.
  4. DNA sequencing identifies a coding sequence across repeat junctions.
  5. Phage activity differs among the three tested phages.
  6. Infection is accompanied by double-stranded DNA and protein production.
  7. Blocking translation preserves DNA production but removes defence; separate protein expression restores it.

Revised paragraph plan

  1. Establish the defence phenotype and the reverse-transcriptase control (Figure 1).
  2. Identify the RNA-derived DNA product and the coding sequence across its repeat junctions (Figure 2A, D).
  3. Describe the DNA products and protein expression before and during infection (Figure 2B–C).
  4. Test the protein’s role using the translation-disrupted construct and separate expression (Figure 3).
  5. Define the tested phage range and describe infected-cell growth (Figure 4).

Protein expression now answers a question raised by product identification. Vector-construction details move to Methods. Rewrite the transition into Figure 3 so it asks whether the protein is responsible for the defence already observed.

Once the order is settled, check what each paragraph contains. Keep an explanation or limitation beside the finding it concerns; move detailed procedures to Methods and remove background already established. If successive additions have obscured the paragraph’s point, list the facts and qualifications to retain and write it again. Compare the versions for changed meaning. [9]; [4].

To meet a word limit, first remove duplicated explanations, tangential background and details already clear in figures or Methods. Then shorten sentences. Read across each cut to check for lost definitions or premises. [9]; [4].

Rough word and figure budget

For a 3,000-word body, start with about 500 words for the Introduction, 1,700 for Results and 800 for Discussion. For 4,000 words, the corresponding starting allocation is 600, 2,400 and 1,000 words. Shift space between sections as the argument requires. These totals exclude Methods; check the journal’s counting rules. Plan around 4–6 main figures and tables combined. [13].

Make each paragraph advance the explanation

Open with the finding when the comparison is familiar; otherwise introduce the question and essential design first. Develop that point with the observations and reasoning it needs. A closing interpretation helps when it connects several observations or leads to the next experiment. If it merely repeats the opening, remove it. [3]; [4]; [9]; [7].

Example: giving a Results paragraph a clear point

Both passages use the illustrative titres from the reporting chapter.

Draft

We measured phage A titres in empty-vector cultures, intact-Brake cultures and cultures carrying inactive reverse transcriptase on six experimental days. The inactive reverse-transcriptase cultures retained high titres. The matched comparison of intact Brake with empty vector gave a 79-fold reduction in geometric mean titre (95% confidence interval, 55–116-fold; Figure 1B). Brake therefore restricted phage output.

Revision

Brake reduced infectious phage A output. The geometric mean of the six day-matched titre ratios indicated a 79-fold reduction with intact Brake relative to empty vector (95% confidence interval, 55–116-fold; Figure 1B). Cultures carrying the inactive reverse transcriptase retained high titres, supporting a requirement for reverse-transcriptase activity.

The control follows the main comparison because it addresses whether restriction requires reverse-transcriptase activity. The revision retains the matching by experimental day.

Edit sentences for clarity

Subjects and verbs

Start with the main clause: who or what did what? Direct verbs often make it easier to find—“we measured induction” instead of “we performed an assessment of induction.” Keep the subject and verb close, and place modifiers beside what they describe. Long qualifications may need a separate sentence. [9]; [3]; [7].

Check that introductory phrases attach to the right subject. In “Using regulatory analysis, the candidate was identified,” the candidate appears to perform the analysis. “Using regulatory analysis, we identified the candidate” makes the actor clear. [9]; [7].

Use a short phrase to identify an unfamiliar subject: “Brake, the candidate defence system, was tested in phage infections.” Once it is familiar, omit that reminder. Give longer explanations their own sentence and check that moving a qualifier preserves its meaning. [10].

Choose the subject that continues the paragraph’s topic. “We measured induction” attributes an action; a passive construction can keep attention on the sample or process. Read both in context. [3].

Connect sentences and comparisons

Connect each sentence to what the reader has just learned: after introducing the RNA-derived DNA, explain what that DNA encodes. In comparisons, parallel constructions put interventions and effects in corresponding positions, making differences easier to follow. Repeat the relevant terms rather than introducing synonyms that might suggest a different measurement or condition. [3]; [4, rule 4]; [10, pp. 217–218].

Example: choosing the focus of a comparison

In the Brake study, preventing protein production removes defence; expressing the protein separately restores it. Choose the subject that connects to the preceding sentence.

Continuing a discussion of defence

Intact Brake restricted phage propagation. Phage restriction was lost when translation of the Brake protein was blocked and restored when the protein was expressed separately.

Continuing a discussion of the genetic tests

We used two genetic tests to examine the Brake protein’s role. Blocking its translation removed phage restriction; expressing the protein separately restored restriction.

Words such as “however” and “therefore” signal a relationship that the sentences must also explain. Check what is being contrasted or what evidence supports the stated consequence; add the missing connection if necessary. [10, pp. 239–240].

Keep related findings together when one sentence makes their relationship clear. Split a sentence when competing points hide the subject or comparison. A short sentence can emphasize a conclusion; a longer one can keep a result beside its necessary qualification. [10, pp. 9–10]; [6].

Read aloud to find awkward phrases and ambiguous pronouns. Then read silently, checking the text against figures and equations. [6]; [9].

Keep terminology and notation stable

Use one term for each scientific concept. Distinguish measurements from inferred quantities, such as RNA abundance and regulatory activity. Agree on terminology with coauthors. [9]; [3].

Use past tense for completed procedures and observations, and present tense for interpretations or figure descriptions where appropriate. Check that tense changes reflect the intended meaning and the journal’s conventions. [9].

Define useful abbreviations on first use, including in the abstract and legends where readers may encounter them independently. Spell out terms used only once or twice unless the abbreviation is familiar. Likewise, name quantities before using symbols and define terms beside equations. For ratios, specify the numerator and denominator, including whether they are measurements or means. Check units, normalization and notation against the figures. [9].

Delete phrases such as “It is important to note that.” Replace vague results with the direction, comparison and relevant magnitude, including uncertainty where needed. [7]; [9]; [3].

Cut words without cutting meaning

Remove repeated caution but retain qualifications that affect the conclusion. Check that edits preserve the subject, comparison, scope and strength of the original statement. [9].

Example: replacing vague caution with a specific limit

The genetic tests establish a role for the Brake protein, but the study has not identified its molecular target.

Draft

The results suggest that the Brake protein may play a role in phage restriction, although further work is needed to understand the mechanism.

Revision

The translation and complementation experiments identify the Brake protein as the defence effector. Its molecular target remains unknown.

“May play a role” understates the genetic evidence. The uncertainty concerns the molecular target, so the revision places it there.

Word searches and readability scores can flag passages to inspect; read those passages before changing them. Studies relating writing features to citation counts do not establish which edits improve understanding. After an edit, check that readers can still identify the comparison and its limitations. [11].

Use coauthor feedback

Ask for the review the draft needs

Match the reader and request to the draft’s main problem. A knowledgeable collaborator can check the science; someone less familiar with the project can identify missing context. When the contribution is disputed, ask about the argument and missing evidence before requesting language edits. Once it is stable, ask readers to mark passages they reread and to summarize the contribution. [2]; [8]; [4].

Send one current version with those questions, a response date and a note identifying unfinished material. [4]; [2].

Working through comments

Read the comments together before accepting edits. Group comments about the same passage or claim, then identify what confused the reader. Their proposed wording may not solve the problem, but the confusion still needs attention. [4].

Discuss edits that change scientific meaning with the coauthors responsible for that work, using the sentence and its supporting figure. Ask why the change was proposed before choosing wording. For stylistic alternatives that mean the same thing, choose the version that fits the paragraph and agreed terminology. [2]; [9].

Maintain one working version with recoverable history. Assign an editor to integrate separate files, or agree ownership during simultaneous editing. Check surrounding definitions and transitions after substantial changes, and return unresolved scientific questions to the responsible coauthors. [9]; [2].

Resolving substantive disagreements

If coauthors disagree about a central conclusion, settle what the evidence supports before polishing that conclusion’s wording. Investigate possible analysis errors early and update any changed estimate in the figure, Results and abstract. Coauthors can continue work on unaffected sections. [14, rules 8–10]; [4, rules 9–10].

For a disputed control, state which explanations it would distinguish. An author may reasonably want to finish a paper with a narrower conclusion while another wants to pursue the larger mechanism. Agree first on what the existing evidence supports, then discuss whether the additional question belongs in this paper. Combining their preferred wording will not settle a disagreement about its scope. [15]; [4, rule 10].

Check whether the authors are answering different questions. An experiment can be technically successful yet leave the proposed mechanism unresolved. If disagreement remains, record the assumption or missing evidence that would settle it. [2]; [4, rule 10].

Check the revised paper against the original concern

Return to each concern with the revised passage and its evidence beside you. Check whether the change resolves the problem. If a claim has narrowed, read its other appearances; if an analysis has changed, verify every reported value derived from it. [9, tip 30]; [2].

Local repairs can leave an Introduction promising an abandoned conclusion, a Discussion relying on an old figure or a legend describing an earlier analysis. Read the complete revision for those consequences. [14, rules 8–9]; [4, rule 10]; [2].

Finish and verify the manuscript

Check the final version as a connected document

Check the manuscript against figures, tables and analysis outputs. Verify numbers, units, group names, citations and cross-references. Inspect the rendered output even when citation and cross-reference tools maintain the links. [7]; [3]; [2].

Allow time for repository records, metadata corrections and coauthor approval. Check the journal’s submission requirements, then inspect the PDF and figures at their intended size for conversion errors. [9]; [8].

Decide when to stop revising

Continue revising while the contribution is unclear, support is missing or coauthors disagree about the interpretation. Once those issues are resolved, circulate the final version for approval. Use this reading to catch errors; return to structural editing if it reveals a problem with the argument, and leave equivalent wording choices settled. [2]; [8].

Before sending the final version

  • Can an unfamiliar reader summarize the contribution and follow the evidence supporting it?
  • Do paragraph purposes, transitions and figure references form a coherent sequence?
  • Have sentence edits preserved comparisons, terminology, scope and uncertainty?
  • Are substantive coauthor comments resolved in one agreed version?
  • Have numbers, units, citations, cross-references and supporting files been checked together?
  • Does the rendered manuscript satisfy the journal’s requirements and have final coauthor approval?
