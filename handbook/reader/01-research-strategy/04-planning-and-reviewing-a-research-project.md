# Planning and reviewing a research project

Plan the work in stages. Decide what you can do now and which later steps depend on the results. Estimate the time and resources each stage needs, then revise the plan as you learn.

<a id="project-plan-scope"></a>

## Scope and milestones

Define what the next stage should establish. “Understand heat stress” is too broad to plan. “Check whether this imaging method can measure division timing after a heat pulse” gives the first stage a specific purpose. The method may work even if the heat pulse does not change division timing.

Work out what you can do with the time, equipment and help available. Check sample access and any commitments already made to collaborators. If a stage depends on an instrument booking, confirm the booking before scheduling work that requires it. Do the same for other resources you are counting on [[1, pp. 127–130]](#ref-project-plan-rightmoves2006).

Agree on scope with the people who will do and support the work. Include what this stage will leave unanswered. A first experiment may establish a response without identifying its mechanism; a technical pilot may establish whether a response can be measured at all. These are different milestones. For the scientific design itself, use [connecting questions, models and experiments](03-connecting-questions-models-and-experiments.md#models-chapter).

<a id="project-plan-feasibility"></a>

## Feasibility checks

Check early for problems that would prevent the later work. Can you obtain the essential material? Can the assay distinguish the outcomes you want to compare? If these checks take little time or expense, do them before committing to the larger experiment [[2]](#ref-project-plan-li2021failfast).

Define what the pilot needs to establish. For an imaging experiment, you might check whether cells remain visible long enough to measure division and whether the control cells behave as expected. Decide what would justify proceeding and which failures would require changes to the method. A small pilot may establish that the assay works while providing too few independent observations to estimate the biological effect precisely.

Not every important uncertainty has a cheap test. A larger commitment may still be worthwhile, but consider what it would teach and which other work it would displace.

An unexpected observation may suggest a better question. Yanai and Lercher distinguish this evolution of a research programme from the design of each study within it: the programme can change direction while its experiments retain clear comparisons. Before following a new direction, decide which existing work to stop or postpone and whether the change affects commitments to collaborators. Keep exploratory work distinguishable from tests of predictions specified beforehand. [[6, p. 667]](#ref-project-plan-yanaiopenness2025).

<a id="project-plan-sequence"></a>

## Scheduling the work

Break the near-term work into tasks, with a responsible person and a clear endpoint for each. Tasks taking one or two weeks can be a useful starting scale; unfamiliar procedures may need smaller steps. Plan distant stages more broadly and add detail as you learn what they will require [[1, pp. 129–130]](#ref-project-plan-rightmoves2006).

Identify which tasks must wait for others. You can sometimes check an analysis script on existing data while preparing new samples. But two experiments that could run independently may still need the same microscope or the same person’s time. Check these conflicts before scheduling them together.

Estimate elapsed time as well as hands-on work. Culture growth, deliveries, training, access queues and feedback can determine the completion date even when they require little active work. Use records of comparable tasks to improve estimates, and name uncertainty where a method is new. Include data inspection, documentation and interpretation; a schedule that ends at acquisition has left part of the research unplanned [[1, pp. 131–133]](#ref-project-plan-rightmoves2006).

A short table or shared document may be enough. For a larger project, a timeline can help you see which tasks overlap and where a delay would hold up other work.

<a id="project-plan-example"></a>**Example: plan a feasibility stage before expanding**

In this fictional project, Mira asks whether a short heat pulse changes the time to first division in yeast after return to standard temperature, compared with mock-handled cultures. Six weeks are available for the initial project stage. The imaging method has not yet been shown to retain identifiable cells through the observation period. These are local planning constraints, not recommended durations.

**First two weeks — establish feasibility.** Before imaging, Mira and her supervisor will agree on the observation window, the evidence needed to trust track identity, and the amount and pattern of cell loss that would prevent a timing comparison. Mira will use two confirmed pilot sessions to check handling, retention and event detection in both heat-pulsed and mock cultures, including whether mock cells can divide. She will inspect the raw movies alongside the tracks and record cells lost from view. The milestone is a documented judgment about whether the assay can answer the timing question, including unresolved limitations.

**Support and preparation.** Leo has agreed to one training session before imaging and a technical review afterward. Mira can prepare the tracking workflow on existing practice movies while waiting for the first session. The practice movies can reveal software problems; they cannot establish that the new experimental setup works.

**Decision at the end of week two.** Mira and her supervisor will review feasibility before committing the remaining four weeks. If tracking and handling are adequate, they will settle the biological sampling and analysis plan, confirm further instrument access and begin the comparison. If a specific technical problem remains, they will assess a bounded repair. If the method cannot answer the question within the available resources, they will reconsider the approach rather than collect a larger uninterpretable dataset.

**Work held back.** No additional conditions or mechanistic assays will be added during the pilot. Later imaging access is provisional, so the six-week allocation is not a promise of a completed biological answer.

<a id="project-plan-review"></a>

## Reviewing the plan

For an extended project, start with a monthly review, as Gosling and Noordam suggest. Compare progress with the previous plan, discuss why work took a different course and agree on the next goals [[3, pp. 37–41]](#ref-project-plan-goslingnoordam2022thesis). Use a shorter interval when a decision cannot wait until the next month.

Review sooner if an essential control fails, access is lost or new evidence changes the question. Routine troubleshooting can usually stay within the current plan.

Revise the plan with the people affected. Record what will change and what will be postponed. If the remaining work no longer fits the time available, agree on a smaller scope, a later deadline or additional help. When a deadline slips repeatedly, address the cause of the delay before setting another date [[1, pp. 134–135]](#ref-project-plan-rightmoves2006).

<a id="project-review-evidence"></a>

## What have you learned?

Begin with the current question and what you agreed to do at the previous review. Explain any change of direction. Show what you learned, including control results and technical problems that limit the conclusions. Mention changes in access or support that affect what you can do next.

Explain what the work establishes, with a link to the experiment or analysis record. “We completed four imaging runs” says how much was done. “We can follow cells through the required period in one condition but not the other” explains why the planned comparison is not yet possible.

Compare this account with the previous plan. Was an estimate unrealistic? Did access disappear? Did the experiment answer a different question than expected? Or did useful new evidence change the aim? A regular comparison of planned and actual work can reveal recurring obstacles that a list of achievements conceals [[3, pp. 37–41]](#ref-project-plan-goslingnoordam2022thesis).

<a id="project-review-diagnosis"></a>

## Understanding setbacks

A result that disagrees with the preferred explanation is not necessarily a failed experiment. If the relevant controls, measurement and comparison are sound, that disagreement can constrain the explanation. If the control fails or the measurement cannot distinguish the possibilities, the experiment may leave the question unresolved. KamounLab makes this distinction central to judging progress [[4]](#ref-project-plan-kamounlab2021failed).

Even a technically successful experiment can be inconclusive. An uncertain estimate may allow both a meaningful effect and little or no effect. A negative finding may apply only to the tested dose, time, system or detection limit. Before treating it as evidence against a model, ask what that model actually predicted under those conditions. The fuller reasoning belongs in [connecting questions, models and experiments](03-connecting-questions-models-and-experiments.md#models-chapter) and [showing observations and uncertainty](../03-data-visualization/02-showing-observations-and-uncertainty.md#uncertainty-chapter).

If work has stopped because samples, equipment or help are unavailable, discuss whether and when access can be restored. The scientific question may still be worth answering, but you may need another way to study it or a different project while you wait [[1, pp. 131–135]](#ref-project-plan-rightmoves2006).

When discussing a setback, ask what prevented progress and what help would make a difference. If a control is missing, agree on how to establish it. If equipment is unavailable, resolve access or change the plan. Assess the work without treating the setback as a judgment of the researcher [[3, pp. 43–48]](#ref-project-plan-goslingnoordam2022thesis).

<a id="project-review-options"></a>

## Deciding what to do next

Decide whether the next period of work is worth its time and cost. Having already spent months on a project is not sufficient reason to continue. Raj recommends setting dates to reconsider the project and seeking outside advice when there always seems to be “one more” experiment to do [[5]](#ref-project-plan-raj2025quit).

Before adding an experiment, ask what it could resolve and how the answer would affect the plan. If every possible outcome leads to the same next step, the experiment may not be needed for that decision. It could still be necessary to document a method or complete an agreed obligation; be clear about that purpose.

Compare credible alternatives. Continuing may be justified when the method is sound and the remaining question is valuable. A bounded repair may be preferable when one identifiable technical problem blocks an otherwise useful comparison. Changing direction can make sense when the evidence points to a more informative question. Pausing may be appropriate when a necessary resource cannot be obtained soon enough. Closure can be reasonable when the remaining contribution no longer warrants its cost [[1, pp. 128, 134–135]](#ref-project-plan-rightmoves2006).

If another group publishes on the same question, read what their study establishes before deciding to stop. It may answer your question, leave a limitation you could investigate or make independent replication more valuable. Assess a new project just as carefully before replacing the current one.

When seeking advice, explain the current plan and the strongest alternative. Say what each could establish and what work it would take. Choose someone who knows enough of the science to challenge your assumptions, and explain the constraints they may not know about [[5]](#ref-project-plan-raj2025quit).

<a id="project-review-decision"></a>

## A constrained choice

The next step can remain debatable even after everyone agrees on the evidence. In the example below, repairing the assay preserves the original question but consumes time that could support a different project. The decision depends on how much that question matters and whether the proposed repair is credible.

<a id="project-review-example"></a>**Example: repair the measurement before expanding the study**

This fictional review follows a two-week microscopy pilot within a six-week project allocation. Mira asks whether a short heat pulse changes time to first division in yeast after return to standard temperature, compared with mock-handled cultures. The pilot was intended to establish feasibility. The following outcomes are introduced for this review exercise; they are not real experimental findings.

**Evidence reviewed.** Temperature handling met the pilot’s checks. Mock-handled cells divide while visible, but cells frequently leave the field in both conditions, preventing reliable assignment of first-division times. The raw movies show the losses. The current records do not establish whether loss is unrelated to division timing or treatment.

**Scientific conclusion.** The heat-pulse question remains unanswered. A cell lost from view cannot be counted as a cell that did not divide. Comparing only the cells that stayed would require assumptions the pilot has not justified.

**Options.** Expanding the current assay would repeat the tracking problem. A specialist has identified a possible mounting change that can be tested in one further week; its effects on growth and observation still need checking. Measuring bulk growth is another possible project, but it would answer a different question from the timing of individual first divisions.

**Decision.** Suspend the biological expansion and spend one week on the mounting test. Mira will run it with the imaging specialist in one confirmed core session. Before the test, they will agree on the observation window and the criteria for trustworthy tracks and acceptable cell loss. They will inspect retention and event detection in both conditions, check whether mock-handled cells can divide under the revised mounting conditions, and assess whether losses could select cells by treatment or division timing. Retaining cells is not sufficient if the mounting prevents normal division or remaining losses still make the timing comparison unjustifiable.

**Next review.** Mira and her supervisor will review the records at the end of that week. If the revised assay supports an interpretable comparison, they will replan the remaining three weeks and confirm access before expanding. If tracking remains inadequate, they will pause this imaging route and assess a different approach. The review does not assume the repair will work or promise a completed biological result within the original allocation.

The one-week repair is a defensible choice because it targets an identified problem with confirmed help. It is not the only defensible choice. Mira could instead decide that learning about bulk growth is worth more than preserving the single-cell question within this short allocation. That would be a change of question, not an alternative measurement of the same outcome. If the specialist or core session becomes unavailable, the case for the repair weakens. A failed repair would not by itself show that choosing it was unreasonable with the information available.

For a pause or closure, record what the work established, where the data and materials can be found, and what would make reopening worthwhile. Agree on who will complete outstanding commitments. If the project continues, update its plan to reflect the decision. When the same obstacles affect several projects, consider [reviewing how the group works](../10-mentoring-and-management/03-leading-and-reviewing-a-research-group.md#group-review-prepare).
