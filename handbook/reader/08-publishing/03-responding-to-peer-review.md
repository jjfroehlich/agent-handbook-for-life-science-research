# Responding to peer review

Reviewer comments often mix questions about evidence with requests for clearer writing or additional work. Identify the concern behind each request before deciding what to change. The revised manuscript must address those concerns, and the response should explain what changed, why, and where the editor and reviewers can find it.

Start the response letter while planning the revision. A reply you cannot yet write may reveal an analysis to check or a question to discuss with coauthors. Mark proposed work clearly, then replace those notes with what was actually done as you revise the manuscript. [[2]](#ref-wilke2014).

The examples continue the fictional [Brake study](../04-writing-a-paper/01-planning-the-paper.md#brake-case).

<a id="revision-workflow"></a>**From reviewer reports to resubmission**

[[2]](#ref-wilke2014); [[1]](#ref-noble2017); [[5]](#ref-chat2021).

1. **Read the decision and all the reviews.** Record the revision conditions and deadline.

2. **Map the concerns.** Separate each comment or subpoint and identify the conclusion or explanation at issue.

3. **Plan with coauthors.** Assign the work, dependencies and schedule in the response document.

4. **Clarify unresolved requirements.** Ask the editor before work that depends on an unclear condition or changes the paper’s scope.

5. **Complete and assess the work.** Revise the manuscript and conclusions when the results change what can be claimed.

6. **Write the replies.** State what was done, why and where, with each reviewer’s section complete on its own.

7. **Reconcile and submit.** Check the final files, locations, data and coauthor approval together.

<a id="decision"></a>

## Understanding the decision

Start with the editor’s decision, which sets the conditions and deadline for revision. Read those conditions alongside the reports, and ask the editor which requests are essential if this is unclear. [[4]](#ref-editors2019); [[5]](#ref-chat2021).

Read all reviews before answering individual points. Identify recurring concerns and challenges to the central result; these may require more work than a long list of minor corrections. Read the relevant manuscript passages beside the comments. A question whose answer seems obvious may reveal an unstated assumption, while several questions may point to one poorly explained idea. [[1, rules 2–3 and 10]](#ref-noble2017); [[2]](#ref-wilke2014).

Work through possible responses privately with coauthors, then draft the reply around the scientific concern.

<a id="planning"></a>

## Planning the revision

<a id="triage"></a>

### Comments and tasks

Copy the full comments into a working document and number the points. Separate the issues within a compound comment so that each receives an answer. Beneath each point, sketch a response or specify the work needed. [[2]](#ref-wilke2014); [[1, rule 5]](#ref-noble2017).

Name what to inspect and what to change: “Define the experimental unit in the legend and check that the analysis uses independent cultures.” An unclear error bar might need only a better legend, or its calculation might be wrong. Inspect the analysis before choosing the remedy. Likewise, check whether an overstated claim results from an analysis error or an interpretation that goes beyond the findings.

<a id="scope"></a>

### Additional experiments

Identify the conclusion the reviewer is questioning and ask what the requested experiment would establish. If you intend to retain that conclusion, you need evidence that resolves the concern—for example, a test of a necessary assumption or a comparison that distinguishes plausible explanations. The requested experiment may provide that evidence; propose a different test if it answers the same question more directly. [[6, p. 7]](#ref-cope2017).

Other checks may strengthen a conclusion that is already supported. Weigh the concern they resolve against the time and resources needed. If you do the work, describe its result in the response and include evidence necessary for the published conclusion with the paper. [[1, rules 1 and 8]](#ref-noble2017); [[6, p. 7]](#ref-cope2017).

A request may also open a new project. Establishing a cellular effect does not necessarily require explaining its mechanism or testing a treatment application. Whether that further work is needed depends on what the paper claims. Discuss the intended scope with the editor when a reviewer expects work beyond that question. [[3]](#ref-ploegh2011); [[1, rule 8]](#ref-noble2017).

<a id="alternatives"></a>

### Alternatives and narrower claims

A narrower claim may resolve the concern when the available evidence supports it. Explain what the revised paper would establish and what would remain unanswered, then change the claim wherever it appears, including the title and abstract when affected. If you propose an alternative experiment instead, explain how it answers the reviewer’s question. Distinguish a scientifically unsuitable test from one blocked by access, equipment or time. [[6, p. 7]](#ref-cope2017); [[1, rule 8]](#ref-noble2017).

Consult the editor when an alternative changes a revision condition or the paper’s scope. If essential evidence is unavailable, assess whether a narrower contribution remains sound and suitable for the journal. [[5]](#ref-chat2021); [[4]](#ref-editors2019).

<a id="case-excessive"></a>**Example: narrowing a claim after review**

An early Brake draft claimed that the system helps infected cells survive. A reviewer asks for recovery measurements. The authors have measured growth arrest and phage output, but not recovery. They propose removing the survival claim and ask the editor whether the paper can be assessed on that basis. In this scenario, the editor agrees.

- *The manuscript states that Brake promotes survival after infection, but growth arrest does not establish survival. Please track whether infected cells recover and resume division.*

We agree that our measurements do not establish recovery. We have not performed the requested recovery experiment. Following discussion with the editor, we have removed the survival claim and focused the conclusion on growth arrest and phage restriction.

The abstract now states: “The Brake protein arrests bacterial growth and restricts phage propagation.” In the Discussion, “Fate of infected cells,” we now write: “Reduced infectious output establishes phage restriction without showing that individual cells survive the infection. Recovery measurements would distinguish reversible arrest from a lasting loss of growth capacity.”

Recovery evidence is necessary for the original survival claim. The narrower paper can proceed only if the remaining contribution is supported and the editor agrees to assess it.

<a id="responsibilities"></a>

### Responsibilities and timing

Agree on substantial changes early and assign the work. Account for dependencies when setting the schedule: a reanalysis must be settled before the affected figure, Results and abstract can be finalized.

Plan with the people doing the work, allowing time for equipment access, sample preparation, analysis and coauthor review alongside their other commitments. When one trainee carries most of the revision, check whether the proposed workload is feasible. Request an extension if the resulting schedule exceeds the deadline. [[3]](#ref-ploegh2011).

Include those doing the work in scientific discussions and relevant exchanges with the editor. Record decisions and open questions in the revision plan. [[5]](#ref-chat2021).

<a id="response-map"></a>**Revision worksheet**

Use one entry per comment or subpoint to track the work and record the final answer. A shared document is often enough; a spreadsheet can help with a large revision. [[2]](#ref-wilke2014); [[1]](#ref-noble2017).

<a id="editor-questions"></a>

### Questions for the editor

Contact the editor before substantial work if the revision conditions are unclear. Describe the concern, propose the work that would address it and ask whether that plan is suitable. If the work will exceed the deadline, explain why and propose a realistic date. [[5, p. 221]](#ref-chat2021).

<a id="revision-results"></a>

## Carrying out the revision

After each experiment or analysis, check whether the result answers the reviewer’s question. Revise the plan if the question remains unanswered or the result reveals a new problem.

<a id="new-work"></a>

### New experiments and analyses

For new experiments, report the additional data and findings. For reanalyses, identify the existing data and explain what changed in the analysis and why. If you uncover an error, describe its consequences and check all affected figures and passages before stating that the conclusions are unchanged. [[1, rule 9]](#ref-noble2017).

Record which analyses were specified before examining the new results and which arose during exploration. Explain changes to the analysis plan and report relevant outcomes, including those that challenge the expected interpretation. [[7]](#ref-makin2019).

<a id="unexpected-results"></a>

### Unexpected or inconclusive findings

When a result is inconclusive, check why. If the intervention or measurement failed, explain what failed and whether the requested test can still be completed. If the experiment worked but the estimate is imprecise, report the estimate and uncertainty; a non-significant result does not establish absence of an effect. To claim that an effect is too small to matter, define the magnitude that would matter for the question and use an analysis that can assess that claim, with assumptions you can justify. See [estimates and intervals](../03-data-visualization/02-showing-observations-and-uncertainty.md#uncertainty-estimates). [[7]](#ref-makin2019).

If new evidence weakens the interpretation, revise it throughout the paper and explain what changed your judgment. Discuss loss of support for a central conclusion with coauthors and the editor before continuing the old plan. [[4]](#ref-editors2019); [[5]](#ref-chat2021).

<a id="evidence-location"></a>

### Supporting material

Include the evidence needed to assess the published conclusions in the paper and its supporting material, including findings that qualify those conclusions. A figure or table may remain only in the response when it answers a reviewer’s specific question and the published account is complete without it. Label response-only material distinctly and state whether any part was added to the paper. [[1, rules 1 and 8]](#ref-noble2017).

<a id="letter"></a>

## Writing the response

<a id="letter-structure"></a>

### Letter structure

Thank the editor and reviewers, then summarize the major changes and the concerns they address.

Follow the overview with the reviews in order, placing your answer directly beneath each comment. If a long comment raises several issues, split it at natural boundaries and answer each part. Retain the full wording, order and meaning, including any shared context, and note that the comment has been divided for clarity. Keep the original point number when it helps the reviewer recognize the comment. [[1, rule 5]](#ref-noble2017).

Make each reviewer’s section readable on its own. When concerns overlap, repeat the explanation, relevant quoted changes and manuscript locations in each section. A reviewer should be able to assess your reply without reading your answers to someone else.

<a id="point-by-point"></a>

### Point-by-point replies

Lead with the correction, experimental result or reason for retaining the approach. Explain how it addresses the concern and identify the corresponding manuscript passage or figure. [[1, rules 1 and 7]](#ref-noble2017).

Distinguish old and new material explicitly. “This is described in the Methods” leaves the reviewer unsure whether the description was already there or has just been added. “We have added a description of the exclusion criteria to the Methods” makes the change clear.

Quote short changes directly: ‘We have clarified the Figure 1B legend: “Each point represents one independently started culture.”’ Identify the revised figure, table or section for every change. Add page or line numbers when the passage is harder to find or the journal requests them, and state that they refer to the revised manuscript. For a long addition, summarize its purpose and give the section title. [[1, rules 3–4 and 9]](#ref-noble2017).

Use consistent typography, spacing and indentation to distinguish comments, replies and manuscript quotations, including in print. [[1, rule 6]](#ref-noble2017); [[2, footnote 2]](#ref-wilke2014).

<a id="case-accepted"></a>**Example: clarifying the experimental unit**

A reviewer asks about replication in the Brake phage-yield assay. The authors confirm that the analysis already uses one titre per culture and matches conditions by experimental day. They split the two-sentence comment below.

Dear Editor,

Thank you for the comments. We have clarified the sampling and analysis behind Figure 1B. We have divided the reviewer’s comment into its two questions; locations and quotations refer to the revised manuscript.

- *Please define what each point represents.*

Each point represents one independently started culture. The Figure 1B legend now states: “Each point represents one independently started culture; lines connect the three cultures assayed on the same day. Six days were measured, with technical duplicate plaque counts combined for each culture.”

- *Explain how the technical duplicates and measurements on different days were handled.*

We combined the duplicate plaque measurements into one titre estimate per culture, then compared intact Brake with empty vector within each experimental day. The six day-matched contrasts were the observations used to estimate the effect and its uncertainty. The analysis and results are unchanged.

Methods, “Infectious-titre analysis,” now states: “We divided the intact-Brake titre by the empty-vector titre within each day and took the log10 of that ratio. We estimated the mean log10 ratio and its two-sided 95% confidence interval using a Student t distribution with five degrees of freedom.” The preceding paragraph explains that technical duplicates were combined into one culture-level estimate.

The analysis already used one titre per culture and matched the cultures by day, so the reply describes a reporting correction. If technical plates had entered the analysis as independent cultures, the authors would also need to correct the analysis and report its consequences.

<a id="disagreement"></a>

### Disagreements and unchanged text

When you disagree with a request, explain how the existing evidence answers the concern or how an alternative would resolve it. If the request concerns a different question, name that question and explain why answering it is unnecessary for the claim you retain. The phrase “beyond the scope” leaves that reasoning unstated.

<a id="no-change"></a>Reread the relevant passage from the reviewer’s perspective. If it omits an explanation, leaves an assumption unstated or makes information difficult to find, improve the manuscript as well as the reply. The analysis may be sound even when its description needs revision. [[1, rule 3]](#ref-noble2017).

If the passage already answers the concern or there is a sound reason to retain the approach, explain that reason, give the evidence and manuscript location, and explicitly say what is unchanged. Quote the passage when useful. Before finalizing the reply, check whether it contains an explanation the manuscript itself needs. [[1, rules 7 and 9]](#ref-noble2017); [[6, p. 7]](#ref-cope2017).

Discuss unresolved disagreements with the editor when they affect a revision condition or the central claim.

Read suggested references and add those that provide relevant context or correct an omission. Briefly explain why an unrelated citation was not added. [[6, p. 7]](#ref-cope2017).

Concerns about a conflict of interest or inappropriate conduct belong in a separate communication to the editor. Describe the relevant facts and the action you are asking the editor to consider. Keep the point-by-point scientific reply focused on the manuscript. [[1, rule 2]](#ref-noble2017).

<a id="conflicting-advice"></a>

### Conflicting requests

First ask whether both requests can be met. More explanation and shorter Results can often be reconciled by moving analytical detail to Methods. If requests require incompatible analyses or contradict the editor’s conditions, explain which approach you propose and why, then ask the editor to clarify the requirement. [[1, rules 2–3 and 8]](#ref-noble2017); [[5]](#ref-chat2021).

<a id="case-conflict"></a>**Example: more explanation in a shorter Results section**

Reviewer 1 wants the day-matched comparison explained. Reviewer 2 wants a shorter Results section, which currently gives the calculation over several paragraphs. The authors keep the comparison and finding in Results and move the calculation to Methods.

**Reviewer 1**

- *Please explain why cultures were compared within each day and how the effect and confidence interval were calculated.*

Each experimental day included all three genotypes. Matching intact Brake to its same-day empty-vector control accounts for shared differences between experimental days. We have retained that comparison in Results, “Brake restricts phage propagation,” and moved the calculation to Methods.

The revised Results read: “We compared intact Brake with empty vector within each experimental day. Across six days, the geometric mean infectious titre was 79-fold lower with Brake (95% confidence interval, 55–116-fold; Figure 1B).”

Methods, “Infectious-titre analysis,” now states: “We divided the intact-Brake titre by the empty-vector titre within each day and took the log10 of that ratio. We estimated the mean log10 ratio and its two-sided 95% confidence interval using a Student t distribution with five degrees of freedom.” It then explains how the estimate and interval were converted to a fold reduction. The calculation is unchanged.

**Reviewer 2**

- *The Results contain too much detail about the titre calculation. Please shorten this passage and keep the focus on the finding.*

We have shortened the Results by replacing the calculation paragraphs with two sentences under “Brake restricts phage propagation”: “We compared intact Brake with empty vector within each experimental day. Across six days, the geometric mean infectious titre was 79-fold lower with Brake (95% confidence interval, 55–116-fold; Figure 1B).”

The calculation now appears in Methods, “Infectious-titre analysis”: “We divided the intact-Brake titre by the empty-vector titre within each day and took the log10 of that ratio. We estimated the mean log10 ratio and its two-sided 95% confidence interval using a Student t distribution with five degrees of freedom.” The following sentences describe conversion to a fold reduction. This retains the day matching needed to understand the result while moving the analytical detail out of Results. The analysis is unchanged.

<a id="checklist"></a>

## Checking and submitting

Read the paper and response side by side to check that they describe the same revision. Verify every cited manuscript location and follow changes to results or conclusions through to the abstract and Discussion.

Ask each responsible researcher to check the relevant result and reply, then have a coauthor read the complete package for consistency. Resolve disagreements about the interpretation or response before submitting.

Update data, code and materials information affected by the revision. Test repository access and verify versions against the revised analyses. [[4]](#ref-editors2019).

Prepare the required clean and marked manuscripts from the same final text. Check response locations against that version and remove working notes.

Before resubmitting, check the following. [[1]](#ref-noble2017); [[2]](#ref-wilke2014).

- Every editor and reviewer comment, including each subpoint, has an answer.

- Each reviewer’s section includes the explanations, quotations and manuscript locations needed to assess the reply on its own.

- The letter reports the work completed and its actual results.

- The manuscript contains the explanations and evidence readers will need.

- Revised methods, sample counts, figure legends and supplementary files match the work reported.

- New analyses and revised conclusions agree across the text, figures and supplements.

- Inconclusive findings are described accurately, and any retained text or approach is justified in the reply.

- Data, code, materials information and access links reflect the revised work.

- All manuscript locations refer to the files being submitted.

- Coauthors have reviewed the revision, and the required clean, marked and supporting files are ready.
