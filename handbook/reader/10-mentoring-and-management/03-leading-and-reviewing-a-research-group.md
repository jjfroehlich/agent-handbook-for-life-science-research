# Leading and reviewing a research group

Before taking on new work, consider what the group would need to do it. A promising project may require a method nobody knows yet or more supervision than you can provide. Plan those commitments alongside existing projects and the support already promised to group members.

<a id="leadership-direction"></a>

## Priorities and planning

Translate the group’s longer-term questions into near-term priorities. To understand recovery from stress, for example, you may first need to establish an assay. Decide whether that takes priority this month over completing another analysis or recruiting a specialist, and explain the choice to the group [[2, pp. 53–54]](#ref-leadership-rightmoves2006).

For each priority, agree who will do the work and what support they need. Check it against existing obligations, including thesis completion and the maintenance of shared material. Decide when to review progress before adding further work.

Keep near-term goals concrete while leaving the longer direction open to discovery. Three to six months for immediate priorities and one to three years for scientific growth can be useful starting horizons; shorten them when access or funding is uncertain. Discuss the plan with the group so that people can question the assumptions and explain what their part will require [[4]](#ref-leadership-greersamuel2019).

Scientific goals should specify what can be learned, not demand a preferred result. A milestone can be a working assay with interpretable controls, a complete dataset or a decision about feasibility. If evidence undermines the original idea, completing that decision can be good work even when the project changes direction.

<a id="leadership-resources"></a>

## Match resources to the work

Review what is available, what is committed and what is only hoped for. A submitted grant, anticipated fellowship or informal offer of instrument access should remain a separate scenario until it is confirmed. Ask the local finance or research-support team to help translate the plan into costs over time. Include the full appointment cost of people, required student costs where applicable, consumables, facility charges and continuing maintenance. Spending restrictions and end dates can make a nominal balance unavailable for the work you intend [[4]](#ref-leadership-greersamuel2019).

Check what would happen if a grant were unsuccessful, an instrument arrived late or a key person left. Which promised work could no longer be completed? Decide what to reduce or postpone before the group runs out of the time or resources needed for it. Buying facility access may let a project continue while an instrument is unavailable; another project may need to wait.

Equipment decisions also need an operating plan. Compare ownership with facility access using anticipated use, booking constraints, maintenance, technical support and delivery time. A purchase that depends on an unavailable room, missing component or untrained user cannot yet support an incoming researcher. Ask who can help with procurement, installation and access before promising a start date [[3, pp. 3–4]](#ref-leadership-goldsteinavasthi2021).

Time is another limited resource. Estimate the training, supervision and coordination that a new commitment requires, including the demands it places on senior researchers. Count the leader’s teaching, writing, administration and existing supervision. A salary can fit the budget while the person’s training cannot fit the week. When the problem concerns how workload or access is distributed, use [sustainable and inclusive research workplaces](05-sustainable-and-inclusive-research-workplaces.md#workplace-chapter) to redesign the arrangement with the people affected.

<a id="leadership-hiring"></a>

## Hiring

Identify the work that needs someone to do it. A group may need reliable daily culture maintenance, help with a specialist analysis or a researcher to develop a new question. Consider whether a facility service or collaboration could meet the need, or whether the group should reduce its planned work. If hiring is the appropriate choice, describe the responsibilities and required skills. State what the person can learn in the role and who will train and supervise them.

There is no universal first hire. An experienced technician may establish continuity and support training but cost more. A novice may be an excellent choice when the leader has time to teach and the work can wait for that learning. A postdoc needs scope for scientific judgment and development as well as funded time. Compare the role’s contribution with the support it needs, rather than treating one career stage as a cheaper substitute for another [[1, pp. 8–17]](#ref-leadership-staffinglab2006).

Check the appointment route with the relevant programme, department or human-resources team before advertising. Student placements, fellowships and staff appointments can have different procedures and obligations. Follow the current local process for classification, pay, recruitment and offers.

<a id="leadership-selection"></a>

### Evaluate evidence for the role

Choose the criteria before reviewing applicants. Separate what someone must already be able to do from what you can teach. Use the same core questions and a comparable opportunity to demonstrate the required work. A short, relevant scenario might ask how a candidate would investigate an inconsistent control or document a handover. Explain what it assesses and avoid asking applicants to perform an unpaid piece of the group’s research.

Ask about the candidate’s actual contribution, how they handled uncertainty and how they worked with others. For someone without research experience, examples from coursework or other work can demonstrate careful records, learning and coordination. Evaluate those examples against the role rather than requiring an experience the position is meant to provide [[5, pp. 16–20]](#ref-leadership-rabinak2026).

Record evidence against the criteria and ask other interviewers to do the same. “The candidate described checking and correcting a sample-label error” is useful evidence; “they seemed like one of us” is difficult to inspect. Collaboration matters, but similarity, confidence, eye contact or enthusiasm are poor substitutes for what the work requires. Give candidates time to think and opportunities to ask questions. Follow the agreed reference-check process, including permission before contacting a current supervisor [[2, pp. 83–89]](#ref-leadership-rightmoves2006).

The candidate is also choosing an environment. Explain the available funding, current limitations, supervision, responsibilities and scope for development. Arrange a conversation with current members in which the leader is absent. Answer what you can honestly provide; separate an intention from an authorized commitment.

<a id="leadership-staffing-example"></a>**Example: choose a hire and revise the operating plan**

Nadia leads a fictional cell-biology group with two doctoral researchers. One is completing a thesis; the other recently joined. The technician who maintains cultures and reagent records will leave in eight weeks. Nadia wants to replace that role and begin a second assay, but she can currently provide six hours a week of direct training. The new doctoral researcher needs four of those hours.

Nadia and the department’s finance adviser cost two appointments over their full proposed duration, including the associated running costs. Both are affordable from committed funds. A novice appointment would leave enough money for the second assay; an experienced appointment would require postponing it. They do not include a pending grant in either budget.

**Work required:** Maintain cultures and stocks, identify routine quality problems, keep complete records and provide a usable handover. Developing the second assay is a separate aim.

**Support needed:** Nadia estimates that a novice would initially need four hours of direct training each week, in addition to the doctoral researcher’s four. An experienced hire would still need local training, estimated at two hours. These are assumptions for this group, to be checked after appointment. The finishing researcher has no unallocated training capacity.

**Decision:** Recruit for the experienced role and postpone the second assay. Six hours of planned training fits the six available; eight does not. The group retains an important existing activity and protects the student’s training. The trade-off is a slower expansion of the programme. Core-facility imaging already meets the present study’s needs, so Nadia also defers a proposed instrument purchase.

**Continuity:** Nadia checks the hiring timetable with the department. If the appointment starts after the technician leaves, she will pause new experimental runs and maintain only essential cultures herself. The expansion will remain deferred during that gap.

**Selection:** Candidates receive the same culture-record scenario and explain what they would check before declaring a batch usable. Interviewers assess the required technical judgment, documentation and communication. The selected candidate supplies concrete examples of these capabilities and asks for training on the group’s local system. Nadia and the candidate agree that this training will precede independent responsibility.

**Start and review:** The outgoing technician prepares the inventory and handover. Nadia arranges access and required training, protects the two weekly training hours and reviews the first complete work records with the new member. Independence requires reliable execution and recognition of failed controls, rather than a desired experimental result. If local training takes longer than estimated, Nadia revises the work allocation before adding tasks.

**Reconsideration:** At the next resource review, Nadia will revisit the second assay if funding and supervision capacity are both available. A grant award alone will not establish that the group can support another trainee.

The experienced appointment is justified by this group’s bottleneck and capacity. With sufficient teaching time and a less urgent continuity need, the novice route could be preferable. Experience also does not remove the need to check readiness for the particular task.

<a id="leadership-onboarding"></a>

## Onboarding

Arrange access, workspace and training before the new member arrives. Name who can answer everyday questions and who is responsible for supervision. Discuss the relevant [shared expectations](02-lab-manuals-and-shared-expectations.md#manuals-chapter), then make a separate [mentoring agreement](01-mentoring.md#mentoring-agreement) for the person’s support and development.

Choose an early task that teaches the local workflow and has an interpretable outcome. Supply the protocol or starting material, expected controls, record location and a person to consult when something differs. Observe enough of the work to check that the new member recognizes errors and knows when to pause. Include technical training and the practical work of finding records, sharing progress and making a handover [[5, p. 20]](#ref-leadership-rabinak2026).

Set a review point around that first task. Ask what remained unclear, which promised support was available and what the person can now do independently. Adjust the next assignment accordingly. If a senior researcher provides training, agree the time and its effect on their own project; do not quietly add responsibility because they happen to be approachable.

<a id="leadership-delegation"></a>

## Delegating responsibility

When delegating work, agree what the person is expected to accomplish and which decisions they can make. Specify the available resources and the changes they must discuss with you first. Check that they understand and can accept the responsibility, and agree when to review progress. Tell others whose cooperation is needed, and support the person’s decisions within the agreed scope [[2, pp. 55–57]](#ref-leadership-rightmoves2006).

Match independence to capability for the task and the consequence of error. Someone can independently maintain an established workflow while needing close discussion for a new method. Review at meaningful points without reclaiming every choice because you prefer a different style. Retain decisions involving commitments or authority that cannot be delegated by the group itself.

<a id="leadership-delegation-example"></a>**Example: a bounded handover of booking decisions**

For an experienced researcher coordinating a fictional imaging pilot, a written handover could say:

You will coordinate imaging for the current pilot within the approved facility allocation. You can arrange slots with the users, change the order of runs within the agreed design and ask the facility to resolve an access problem. Keep the schedule and reasons for changes in the project record. Bring any change to sampling, extra expenditure or a clash with another project’s agreed time to me before committing it. We will review the schedule after the first acquisition cycle. I will tell the team that these booking decisions are yours and remain responsible for resolving a resource conflict you cannot settle.

If the coordinator has never used the facility’s booking system, first provide training and supervised practice.

Detailed agreements between collaborating groups belong in [collaboration, authorship and credit](04-collaboration-authorship-and-credit.md#collaboration-plan).

<a id="leadership-oversight"></a>

## Meetings and review

Use different conversations for different decisions. An individual meeting can examine a researcher’s reasoning and support needs. A project meeting can resolve a shared dependency. A group meeting can compare interpretations and discuss scientific direction. Give each a purpose, useful preparation and a record of decisions, owners and next checks; choose frequency around the work rather than filling the calendar [[2, pp. 58–60]](#ref-leadership-rightmoves2006).

When work stalls, first establish what happened. A valid negative result, a failed procedure, a blocked resource and a missed agreed action need different responses. Compare the expectation with training, access, workload and supervision before deciding that the person lacks motivation. Discuss the specific observation privately, hear their account and agree the support, action and review needed. The leader may need to change a promise or priority as well as asking the member to change their work [[5, pp. 33–36, 54–57]](#ref-leadership-rabinak2026).

Keep enough contact with methods, controls and original records to understand the science being produced. Invite concerns before results are polished, give people room to disagree and recognize work that enables others: maintenance, curation, training and coordination. A recurring problem should produce a decision about support or responsibility, not an indefinitely repeated expression of disappointment. Detailed feedback and difficult conversations are treated in [feedback and conflict](06-feedback-and-conflict.md#conflict-chapter). A safety or integrity concern needs the appropriate institutional route promptly; it should not wait for the next routine project review.

Give your own leadership work time and support. Protect thinking, writing and supervision in the calendar; tell the group how to reach someone when you are unavailable. Seek advice from experienced leaders, peers and administrative specialists. To improve a particular practice, examine where it causes difficulty. For example, record which decisions wait for your approval for a fortnight, then delegate a suitable recurring class of decision and check whether the team can act within its scope [[2, pp. 51–53]](#ref-leadership-rightmoves2006).

<a id="group-review-prepare"></a>

## Preparing the review

Choose a period that allows you to compare the last agreed priorities with what actually happened. An annual discussion can provide a broad view, with shorter reviews after a substantial change in staffing, funding or workload. Do not wait for that meeting to address an urgent concern. [[7, pp. 115–121]](#ref-leadership-bennett2018); [[8, pp. 719–720]](#ref-leadership-vincent2015).

Prepare a brief account of what the group has learned and can now do. Include progress that has not yet led to a paper, such as establishing a method or completing an analysis. Recognize training and maintenance that allow others to work. Then identify commitments that have changed and obstacles that recur. If a paper is delayed, explain what is holding it up: an unresolved scientific question may require a different response from work that repeatedly loses its allotted time. [[8, pp. 719–720]](#ref-leadership-vincent2015).

Bring enough information to discuss resources honestly. Which work is already promised? Which skills, facilities and supervision does it require? Where will a departure, a funding end date or a new responsibility change what is feasible? Distinguish confirmed support from an application or an informal offer. Share the constraints needed for the decision without circulating individual salary, health or personnel information. Beltrao (2022) [[6]](#ref-leadership-beltrao2022) offers one example of considering people, research and funding together; its particular costs and group size are not benchmarks for other laboratories.

Invite members to prepare their own observations before seeing the leader’s conclusions. Ask which arrangements help them do good work, where work repeatedly gets stuck and what change would make a meaningful difference. A specific example gives the discussion something to examine. People should also have a way to raise concerns outside the group meeting, especially when they involve a supervisor or colleague on whom they depend. [[8, pp. 718–720]](#ref-leadership-vincent2015); [[7, pp. 112–113]](#ref-leadership-bennett2018).

Agree who will read written responses, what will be retained and how concerns requiring another route will be handled. In a small group, a supposedly anonymous comment can identify its author through its details. Do not promise anonymity that the collection method cannot provide. The [feedback and conflict chapter](06-feedback-and-conflict.md#conflict-chapter) explains how to distinguish a discussion of shared practice from a concern that needs individual or institutional attention.

<a id="group-review-discuss"></a>

## Discussing recurring problems

Begin with what is working well enough to preserve. A new procedure can unintentionally remove something valuable, such as informal technical help or time to explore a surprising observation. Then examine the concerns, allowing members to describe what they see before the leader offers an explanation. Differences between accounts are useful information: someone who receives quick help may not see how much work it creates for the person providing it. [[7, pp. 112–117]](#ref-leadership-bennett2018).

Follow a concern far enough to distinguish plausible explanations. If analyses are late, ask whether the data are ready, the method is established, the necessary expertise is available and the assigned researcher has the time the plan assumed. A problem shared by several projects may require changing how the group allocates work. A difficulty specific to one person’s training may be better addressed in a [mentoring conversation](01-mentoring.md#mentoring-chapter). Neither conclusion follows simply from the missed date. [[8, p. 720]](#ref-leadership-vincent2015).

Include the effects of routine work on access and development. Who repeatedly trains newcomers, maintains equipment or resolves urgent requests? Who has opportunities to learn, present and take responsibility? Use the [workplace review](05-sustainable-and-inclusive-research-workplaces.md#workplace-chapter) to examine these patterns in more detail. The purpose is to understand how the arrangements operate, not to ask individuals to justify private circumstances in front of colleagues.

Keep the discussion specific when views differ. Record the observation, the competing explanations and what would help distinguish them. A vote cannot determine why an assay is failing, and a majority’s comfortable experience does not settle another member’s report of poor treatment. Address concerns about conduct through the appropriate route; do not use a group review as an improvised investigation.

<a id="group-review-decide"></a>

## Choosing changes

Compare a proposed change with the work it would displace. Training a backup, improving documentation or changing a meeting format takes effort now, even when it will save effort later. State where that time will come from. Asking everyone to fit it around existing commitments leaves the capacity problem unresolved. [[8, pp. 719–720]](#ref-leadership-vincent2015).

Members can help compare alternatives without being made responsible for securing a budget they do not control. Explain which changes the group can agree itself and which require a decision elsewhere. Do not promise additional funding or support until the person with that authority has agreed.

Choose a small enough change that the group can support it and tell whether it helped. The following example postpones an attractive collaboration because the existing work already depends on support that is spread too thinly.

<a id="group-review-example"></a>**Worked example: protect an existing project before adding another**

This fictional microscopy group has a PI, two postdocs, two doctoral researchers and a technician. Two projects use the same imaging assay and analysis workflow. A collaborator has proposed a third pilot. One postdoc will leave in four months, and the technician handles most troubleshooting.

The PI initially thinks more frequent progress meetings would help a delayed analysis. Preparing for the group review changes the picture: sample processing is on schedule, but the researcher doing the analysis is repeatedly interrupted to help resolve assay problems. The technician also lacks a trained backup. Another meeting would reveal the delay without supplying the missing time.

**Decision record — 2 October**

**Priority.** Bring the existing analysis to its agreed checkpoint and establish a second person who can troubleshoot the assay before the postdoc leaves.

**Change.** Defer the collaborator’s pilot. Protect two analysis sessions each week by routing routine assay questions through the technician during agreed support periods. Urgent problems that could lose samples still receive immediate attention; repeated urgent requests trigger a review of the arrangement.

**Training and resources.** One doctoral researcher will complete a supervised troubleshooting run with the technician. The PI will remove a planned exploratory experiment from that researcher’s schedule and postpone a nonessential imaging batch assigned to the technician. Those changes provide time for both people. The PI will also ask the core facility whether it can provide continuing support.

**Responsibility.** The PI will explain the delay to the collaborator this week. The technician and doctoral researcher will agree the training run. The analyst will bring the completed analysis, or a specific remaining obstacle, to the next review.

**Review — six weeks later.** Check whether the analysis reached its checkpoint, the backup can complete the troubleshooting run and interruptions have become manageable. Reconsider the pilot only with an explicit estimate of its workload and available support.

At the six-week review, the analysis has reached its checkpoint and the trainee has completed the supervised run. Independent troubleshooting still needs to be demonstrated. The core facility cannot offer continuing support. The group retains the protected analysis time and keeps the pilot deferred while the PI and collaborator reduce its scope. Progress on the existing work has improved, but the extra capacity needed for a third project has not yet been established.

The collaborator could reasonably be disappointed: postponement may cost a scientific opportunity. Accepting the pilot would also have a cost, borne partly by the people whose current work would slow down. The decision therefore depends on the value and timing of both projects, not on a general rule against new work. Here, the unresolved support problem favors postponement. Check that the new arrangement has not quietly transferred every interruption to the technician.

<a id="group-review-follow-up"></a>

## Following up

Distribute a concise account of the agreed decisions and invite corrections. Keep personal disclosures and sensitive case details out of the shared record. If a change affects a standing practice, update the relevant [lab-manual section](02-lab-manuals-and-shared-expectations.md#manuals-chapter). If it changes a collaborator’s responsibilities or delivery date, revise the [collaboration agreement](04-collaboration-authorship-and-credit.md#collaboration-chapter).

At the next review, distinguish an action completed from a problem resolved. A training session may have happened without making the trainee ready to work independently. A revised schedule may meet a deadline while shifting the same workload into evenings. Ask the people affected whether the arrangement works, using an appropriate private conversation when a public answer would be difficult. Compare that account with the agreed evidence of improvement. [[7, pp. 115–121]](#ref-leadership-bennett2018); [[8, p. 720]](#ref-leadership-vincent2015).

When a change has not helped, revisit the explanation and the resources before repeating the instruction more firmly. Keep the reason for changing or retaining the arrangement with the decision, so a later review can build on what the group learned.
