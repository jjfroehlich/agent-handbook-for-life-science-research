# Exploring career options

Scientific training can lead to research, teaching, publishing, technology development, policy or managing shared facilities. The titles alone tell you little about the working day. An editor evaluates manuscripts; an applications scientist helps researchers use a technology; a facility manager organizes equipment, expertise and services for many groups. Compare what you would do repeatedly in each role and who would use the work [[1]](#ref-career-options-severjanssen2017).

<a id="career-options-work"></a>

## Compare the work

Think about the work you would like to do regularly. You might enjoy troubleshooting instruments but prefer less time writing papers, or want to teach while staying involved in research. Being good at an activity does not necessarily mean you want it to fill your week. Also note conditions that could rule out a role, such as frequent travel or a location you cannot move to.

Compare actual posts within those categories. Research roles differ in freedom to choose projects, expectations for publication and the importance of a commercial application. Ask who sets priorities, how success is assessed and which decisions you would make yourself [[2]](#ref-career-options-pain2009).

Scientific training develops skills in framing questions, evaluating evidence, learning unfamiliar subjects and explaining results. Identify which of these the role uses, then assess what else you need to learn. A supervised project or placement may provide relevant experience; a course may be sufficient for a specific knowledge gap.

Distinguish the work behind similar titles. Developing a production process differs from discovery research [[3]](#ref-career-options-kling2004manufacturing). Customer troubleshooting and demonstrations differ from selling a product or arranging a licensing partnership [[4]](#ref-career-options-jensen2010applications); [[5]](#ref-career-options-jensen2009business). Regulatory strategy and submission coordination also carry different responsibilities [[6]](#ref-career-options-jensen2009regulatory). Ask what this particular post produces, who relies on it, and what training the employer provides. Neither a doctorate nor a short credential establishes readiness for all of these jobs.

Compare preparation with direct entry. Another postdoc, course or internship is useful only if it supplies something the intended role needs at an acceptable cost. A placement may be inaccessible because of income, caring or location constraints. Choose a finite inquiry or learning step rather than accumulating credentials while waiting to feel certain about a lifelong career identity [[7]](#ref-career-options-goslingnoordam2022thesis).

<a id="career-options-example"></a>**Example: comparing two roles**

Lena enjoys troubleshooting assays and explaining results to collaborators. She wants to remain in one region and prefers working with others to spending long periods writing alone.

**Applications scientist:** Lena spoke with two scientists who support genomics users and tried teaching a workflow to a new colleague. She enjoyed diagnosing the problem and watching the user become independent. Both conversations, however, described recurring travel outside her region. She will look for local or remote support posts and check the travel requirements of each one.

**Research project manager:** Lena observed a planning meeting and coordinated one defined part of a collaboration. She liked clarifying decisions and making sure one group’s delay did not block another. She missed doing the technical work herself, so she narrows the search to roles close enough to the science for her assay knowledge to matter.

Lena now gives locally based scientific project roles priority. She will accept less bench time for collaborative problem-solving, but not routine travel or work detached from the technical decisions.

<a id="career-options-conversations"></a>

## Learn from people doing the work

Where access permits, speak with people who hold the role in different organizations or career stages. A peer or near-peer can be as useful as a senior contact for learning an entry route. Ask them to describe a recent week and a difficult decision. Which responsibilities surprised them when they started? Find out how they learned the role and what experience they would look for in a new colleague. Ask how their work is assessed. Quiet participation and a few relevant conversations can be enough; there is no required contact count or event budget.

Ask similar questions across conversations. If people in the same role describe different weeks or constraints, ask what comes from the role and what comes from their organization or manager.

A conversation can suggest a way to try the work. Shadowing someone or taking on a short project may reveal more than another general discussion. For a postdoctoral position, ask what you expect to gain from the appointment and whether the group can provide it. Discuss how you would respond if the promised training or opportunities did not materialize. [Choosing an advisor and research environment](02-choosing-an-advisor-and-research-environment.md#advisor-chapter) explains how to assess that support.

<a id="career-options-evidence"></a>

## Experience and next steps

Keep a brief record of relevant work you have done. Describe what you did, who used it and what happened as a result. Include evidence you could show or someone who could confirm your contribution. Work beyond your research project may also be relevant: teaching a workshop can demonstrate training experience, for example. Compare these experiences with the responsibilities of the roles you are considering.

“Kept a shared project schedule up to date” shows coordination; it does not show that you led the project. “Completed a training module” may show interest in quality practice; it is not the same as having worked in that system. Check where you still need practical experience before applying.

Reconsider the options after the conversations or trials. Which work did you want more of after trying it? Did a working condition rule out an otherwise attractive role? Use what you learned to narrow the search or choose the experience you need next.

Treat career statistics as context, not a forecast for one person. A selected group of successful applicants, a historical workforce count and the frequency of advice in a survey describe different things. Keep the date, population and denominator with a number you need; none by itself establishes your hiring probability or proves that a recommended tactic works. For example, free-text advice in a survey identifies the respondents’ concerns, rather than testing whether following that advice causes success [[8]](#ref-career-options-afonja2021).
