# Finding and applying for positions

Begin with the work you want to do and the experience you can offer. Search advertisements and organizations working in that area, and ask colleagues about opportunities. Keep track of promising positions and their deadlines.

<a id="positions-targets"></a>

## Choose the appropriate application route

The application route depends on whether you are seeking research training, a postdoctoral project or a role with defined responsibilities.

For each serious option, note why it interests you, which requirements you meet and what you still need to find out. Keep the requested materials and contact details with the deadline.

The selection conversation also differs. A PhD interview usually tests preparation for training and gives you evidence about supervision and the programme. A postdoc discussion asks what scientific direction you could develop together, what you already contribute and what the group can support. A non-academic employer is recruiting for defined work: an early screen may test the basic match, while later technical, behavioral or panel interviews may ask how you handled decisions, collaboration and setbacks. Ask for the format rather than assuming that one academic research talk prepares you for every route.

<a id="positions-search"></a>

## Finding opportunities

Combine programme and employer sites with job boards, professional networks and conversations. Ask colleagues which organizations work on the questions or problems that interest you. Follow useful leads beyond familiar institutions.

For a postdoc, list groups connected to the questions, methods or research direction you want to develop. Read recent work, then narrow the list through conversations with people in the field, current members and alumni. Include relevant groups without advertised openings: some recruit through direct inquiries. [[1]](#ref-positions-sabbagh2021postdoc).

First ask what the postdoc would enable compared with direct entry to the intended work or more focused training. It may supply a new capability, a distinct research direction or necessary experience; it may also postpone a transition without adding relevant evidence. Strong recommendations for further training and warnings against a default postdoc apply to different goals. Compare actual support, financial and household feasibility, and the cost of delay; no appointment guarantees a permanent position [[1]](#ref-positions-sabbagh2021postdoc); [[4]](#ref-positions-goslingnoordam2022thesis).

For a PhD, check how a supervisor is assigned. A programme may offer rotations or several potential supervisors; a project-based position may already have a defined question and supervisor. Confirm availability, entry requirements and the funding arrangement before preparing an application. Ask what support covers and for how long. See [Choosing an advisor and research environment](02-choosing-an-advisor-and-research-environment.md#advisor-chapter) for assessing supervision. [[2]](#ref-positions-fomina-phdguide).

Separate a supervisor taking students, a lab having funds and this applicant receiving a funded place. Confirm duration, restrictions, total costs and insurance, teaching or qualifying obligations, and backup support with the responsible programme or funder. Ask what happens if the project, advisor or location changes, including co-advising, leave and permitted outside work. A scholarship directory is a lead, not an award [[2]](#ref-positions-fomina-phdguide). New students can develop agency through starter work and purposeful learning; they need not arrive with a lifetime thesis topic or earn support through immediate autonomy [[4]](#ref-positions-goslingnoordam2022thesis).

For a non-academic role, identify what the person hired will do, which capabilities are essential and who they will work with. Match each requirement to evidence from your experience. Separate requirements you do not yet meet from experience you have but need to explain in the employer’s terms. If the advert leaves a requirement unclear, ask someone familiar with the work before deciding whether you meet it. Use [CVs, resumes, letters and references](03-cvs-resumes-letters-and-references.md#applications-chapter) to turn these matches into application material.

<a id="positions-contact"></a>

## Write a specific inquiry

Before contacting someone, read their group’s work and any application instructions. Explain the connection to your experience and ask a question the recipient can answer, such as whether the group anticipates recruiting in your intended period.

For an academic-postdoc inquiry, name the research connection, give one or two examples of relevant work and say when you hope to start. Ask whether the group expects to recruit or would be willing to discuss a possible project. Include or link materials only when requested or when they help the recipient assess the inquiry. [[1]](#ref-positions-sabbagh2021postdoc).

If a direct inquiry receives no reply, send a short follow-up after allowing time for a response, then continue with other opportunities. A conversation does not replace a required formal application: keep track of that deadline separately. [[1]](#ref-positions-sabbagh2021postdoc).

Keep manuscript status and intended timing exact; an unfinished project need not be described as published to justify contact. Use the recipient’s actual instructions rather than a universal publication count, contact month or shortlist size [[1]](#ref-positions-sabbagh2021postdoc). If a recruiter is involved, establish who pays them, what they can decide and whether they have permission to submit your credentials. A referral or recruiter assurance does not replace the required route or guarantee consideration [[7]](#ref-positions-jensen2012sharks).

<a id="positions-target-record"></a>**Example: a short, specific postdoc inquiry**

Nadia is finishing a cell-biology PhD in September 2027. She has built a live-imaging workflow that segments and tracks epithelial repair fronts. Dr Imani’s fictional group studies immune–epithelial interactions during tissue repair. Nadia has read two recent papers from the group and wants to develop a project that combines imaging with quantitative analysis.

**Subject:** Postdoctoral inquiry: live imaging of epithelial repair

Dear Dr Imani,

I expect to complete my cell-biology PhD in September 2027. I developed a live-imaging workflow to segment and track epithelial repair fronts, and would now like to study how immune cells influence that repair. Your group’s work on immune–epithelial interactions is closely connected to this question.

Do you expect to recruit a postdoc in autumn 2027? I would welcome a conversation about whether my imaging and analysis experience could contribute to your research. I have attached my CV and a brief research summary.

Best wishes,
 Nadia Patel

Questions about supervision and possible projects can follow if Dr Imani is interested in discussing recruitment.

<a id="positions-interviews"></a>

## Prepare for the interviews

An interview can include an initial screening call, a research talk, meetings with possible colleagues, an assessment task or several conversations over a visit. Ask for the agenda, who you will meet and any presentation or task instructions. Use this information to prepare: a recruiter may need a concise explanation of how your experience meets the role’s requirements, while a scientific colleague may ask how you chose a method or interpreted a result. Prepare questions about the work and support you would need before accepting.

For a postdoc, prepare to explain your PhD question, your contribution and what the results establish. Read the prospective group’s recent papers and bring ideas to discuss. If a question falls outside your knowledge, explain what you understand and ask for clarification where needed. Ask about supervision, resources, project ownership and support for your next career step. [[1]](#ref-positions-sabbagh2021postdoc).

For a non-academic role, prepare brief examples that connect your experience to the problem described in the advert: a technical decision, a collaboration, a setback or a result that changed a next step. Be ready to distinguish what you did from what the team did. Ask how the team defines a successful first period in the role, what it needs from the person hired and how the work is evaluated. For a PhD, explain the experience that prepares you to learn and investigate the proposed area, then ask about the programme route, available supervisors and training environment.

An account of conflict or failure need not end in a tidy success or lesson. State the actual problem, your actions, the outcome and what remains unknown; do not invent fault, learning or resolution to complete a response pattern. Prepare from your own experience rather than borrowed answers [[5]](#ref-positions-jensen1999behavioral). Clarify an unexpected question and acknowledge a gap rather than perform certainty. Use the interview to investigate actual work and support, too: chemistry, eye contact or performed enthusiasm do not establish scientific competence or a workable job [[6]](#ref-positions-fiske2000interview).

<a id="positions-interview-example"></a>**Example: discussing a possible postdoc project**

Asked how she would begin in Dr Imani’s group, Nadia says: “I would first learn how the group currently measures repair and which results are reliable enough to build on. Then I would try my tracking workflow on a small experiment and compare what it shows with the existing analysis. If it adds useful information, we could plan a larger imaging project. I can bring the segmentation and tracking workflow; I would need help learning the immune-cell assays.”

After each conversation, write down what you learned, what remains uncertain and the promised next action. Send a brief follow-up when it is appropriate to thank the person, correct a material omission or confirm an agreed step. This record will also help you compare an offer with the criteria you set at the start.

<a id="positions-pipeline"></a>

## Review the search and compare offers

Keep several possibilities active while awaiting decisions. Review where applications stop. If applications to apparently suitable roles rarely reach screening, ask a knowledgeable reader to assess whether your experience meets the requirements and whether the documents make that clear. If interviews repeatedly expose the same gap, distinguish difficulty explaining your experience from experience you still need to gain. The first calls for a clearer example; the second may require training or a different choice of roles. Record promised follow-ups and deadlines [[3]](#ref-positions-jensen2010jobsearchplan).

When an offer arrives, compare the actual work and conditions with your priorities. Resolve unanswered questions about the project, supervision, funding or employment period, location and start date. If other decisions are pending, ask when a response is needed and whether more time is possible. Use the [research-environment comparison](02-choosing-an-advisor-and-research-environment.md#advisor-decision) for a PhD or postdoc, and return to your [career priorities](01-exploring-career-options.md#career-options-work) for other roles. [[1]](#ref-positions-sabbagh2021postdoc).

For a postdoc, discuss how project control, credit, data, materials and future directions will be agreed and revisited as the work changes. Legitimate continuing collaboration is different from blocking development; neither a verbal promise nor an alumni outcome settles what this person can carry forward. Notify other groups when your decision closes an active process. Use agreed or stated timelines for further contact, rather than repeated calls on a fixed schedule; silence alone does not reveal the result [[1]](#ref-positions-sabbagh2021postdoc).

<a id="positions-checklist"></a>**Checks as the search progresses**

- I understand the position, application route and requirements.

- I can explain why the work interests me and which experience prepares me for it.

- I have followed the position’s stated application or contact instructions.

- I have prepared research or work examples and questions for the interview.

- I have recorded what I learned and kept the rest of the search moving.
