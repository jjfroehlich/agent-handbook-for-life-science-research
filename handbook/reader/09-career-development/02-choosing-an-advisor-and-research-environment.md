# Choosing an advisor and research environment

Two laboratories can work on equally interesting questions yet offer very different training. Read their work, talk with each prospective advisor, and ask current and former members how projects and supervision develop in practice. Compare the research, the support you need and the working environment before choosing. [[1]](#ref-advisor-barres2013advisor); [[2]](#ref-advisor-kamens2014mentor).

<a id="advisor-stage"></a>

## Decide what you need from the position

Start with the outcome you need from the training period. A PhD and a postdoc both involve research, but they make different demands of supervision.

Write down your priorities and any conditions you cannot accept. A student learning to design experiments may need frequent early feedback; a postdoc preparing for an independent position may need opportunities to develop and carry forward a distinct research direction. Ask about support for the career you intend to pursue, including work outside academia. [[2]](#ref-advisor-kamens2014mentor).

For a PhD, identify who provides advice beyond the primary supervisor, such as a committee member or programme director. Ask how that person becomes involved if supervision or progress is difficult.

Read recent papers before these conversations. Ask how a possible project would build on that work, which methods you would need to learn and who would teach them. Confirm the funding and facilities available for your work, including what depends on a pending application. [[1]](#ref-advisor-barres2013advisor).

<a id="advisor-conversations"></a>

## Conversations and visits

Talk with people at different points in the laboratory: a newer member, someone approaching a transition, and, where possible, a former member. A visit, rotation or attendance at a lab meeting can reveal how questions are handled and who participates. Conversations away from the immediate selection setting may make it easier for people to explain ordinary practice. Ask the same core questions of several people, but leave room for what they think matters. [[2]](#ref-advisor-kamens2014mentor); [[3]](#ref-advisor-sabbagh2021postdoc).

Ask for concrete examples: “Could you describe the feedback on the last paper or project proposal?” Follow up on how the advisor helped when a trainee was stuck or a project needed to change direction. Ask how decisions about credit were made. Compare the advisor’s account with those of current and former members.

Give people room to decline a question, and keep private accounts confidential. If several people describe the same problem with supervision, treatment or credit, take it seriously. Seek independent advice before committing, especially when a prospective advisor’s explanation conflicts with the accounts of current or former members. [[2]](#ref-advisor-kamens2014mentor).

<a id="advisor-evidence-example"></a>**Example: compare a laboratory through evidence**

In this fictional comparison, Mina is choosing between two PhD laboratories in the same field. She needs structured feedback while learning to frame a project, but she also wants room to develop an independent question.

**Laboratory A:** the advisor and two trainees describe weekly individual meetings. An alumnus recalls detailed feedback on an early proposal. A student explains how a lab discussion helped narrow a stalled project to a related, feasible question. The first-year project would sit within a funded collaboration, so its initial scope is relatively narrow and day-to-day teaching would often come from a senior postdoc.

**Laboratory B:** the research question matches Mina’s interests more closely, and two trainees value the freedom to shape their projects. The advisor schedules individual meetings every two weeks, and a staff scientist offers regular methods guidance. An alumnus describes developing a question in the group and carrying that line of work into the next position. When one project stalled, the trainee moved to a new question and another researcher took over the original work. The trainee says the advisor supported the change, although the first months required more independent planning than Mina expects to manage comfortably.

Mina chooses Laboratory A. She accepts a narrower starting project and more day-to-day dependence on a senior postdoc because weekly feedback meets her immediate training need. Laboratory B offers the closer topic, earlier ownership and a credible route to independence, but expects more planning between meetings. A student who already works confidently with that method might reasonably choose it.

<a id="advisor-decision"></a>

## Compare the opportunities

Compare the opportunities against your priorities. You might accept less freedom in exchange for closer initial supervision, but first establish what that supervision would involve. For a new group with no alumni, ask who will provide day-to-day training while it is being established. Observe scientific discussions where possible and find out what support the department offers. [[1]](#ref-advisor-barres2013advisor).

For a PhD, check whether the programme provides alternatives if the initial project or advisor arrangement does not work. For a postdoc, discuss how you could develop a distinct contribution and what support you would receive for the next position. Agree which projects you could take with you and which collaborations would continue. [[2]](#ref-advisor-kamens2014mentor); [[3]](#ref-advisor-sabbagh2021postdoc).

Resolve the questions that could change your choice before accepting. Record the agreed project, supervision and funding arrangements so you and the advisor can revisit them when work begins.

<a id="advisor-checklist"></a>**Before choosing an advisor or environment**

- I know what training and opportunities I need from this PhD or postdoc.

- I have assessed research fit, supervision, environment, resources and trainee trajectories separately.

- I have asked several people for concrete examples and compared their accounts with direct observation where possible.

- I know which unanswered question would change my decision.

- I have not treated prestige, a familiar topic or one positive interaction as sufficient evidence.
