# Choosing an opportunity and planning the application

Before drafting an application, check whether you can apply and whether the funder supports the work you want to do. Prepare an initial budget to see whether the available funding would cover it [[2, pp. 883–884]](#ref-grant-plan-consalvey2023funding).

<a id="grant-plan-fit"></a>

## Eligibility and fit

Start with a short list of plausible opportunities. Your institution’s research office, colleagues and funders’ previous awards can help you find schemes that support similar work. Read the current call before using a colleague’s successful application as a model: the scheme’s scope or requirements may have changed. Previous awards cannot tell you how likely your application is to succeed [[2, p. 883]](#ref-grant-plan-consalvey2023funding).

Check eligibility. Read the current call’s requirements for applicants, host institutions and partners. If a condition is unclear, ask your research office or the funder’s contact and keep their answer with the application instructions.

Read what the funder wants to support. A fellowship may place substantial weight on your training, while a pilot grant may support testing whether a method works. Explain how your proposed work meets that purpose. Where the funder welcomes enquiries, send a brief project description and ask about the point you are unsure of [[2, p. 883]](#ref-grant-plan-consalvey2023funding); [[1, pp. 161–165]](#ref-grant-plan-rightmoves2006).

<a id="grant-plan-requirements"></a>

## Application requirements

Read the application instructions and assessment criteria together. Check where the application asks you to address each criterion and how it will be weighted [[2, p. 883]](#ref-grant-plan-consalvey2023funding).

List the required documents and portal fields, who will prepare them and when they are needed. Record the applicable length limits and use the supplied templates from the start. Allow time for another person to approve a letter or support statement before its due date. Check whether portal registration or institutional authorization must be arranged before submission; finishing the text may not be the last step.

Clarify questions that could change the proposed work before drafting in detail. For example, if institutional costs count towards the award limit, less money remains for the experiments. If a partner has not agreed to provide samples, you cannot yet rely on that access. Ask the funder or research office about an unclear rule, and the partner about their commitment. Check for official amendments to the instructions during preparation [[2, p. 884]](#ref-grant-plan-consalvey2023funding).

<a id="grant-plan-costs"></a>

## Preparing a budget

Start with the work you propose to do. Estimate the staff time and materials needed, and ask facilities or service providers for quotes. If you plan to use existing equipment, check that time on it is available and include any running charges. Note what the institution or partners have agreed to provide.

Work with your finance or grants office to check which costs the funder allows. They can also establish whether institutional overheads count towards the funding limit. Direct costs pay for the project itself; indirect costs cover shared support, such as administration and building services. Include the applicable charges before deciding whether the project is affordable [[2, p. 884]](#ref-grant-plan-consalvey2023funding); [[1, pp. 168–169]](#ref-grant-plan-rightmoves2006).

Check whether the requested funding and confirmed contributions cover the work. If essential costs exceed that support, reduce the scope, secure an agreed contribution or choose another opportunity. Removing a necessary cost from the budget leaves the work unfunded. Request what the project needs, even if it is below the maximum award. Eisenstadter advises examining a foundation’s usual award scale and explaining each expense; her observations concern smaller foundations and do not establish that cheaper proposals generally win [[3, p. 154]](#ref-grant-plan-eisenstadter2014grants).

The following fictional case concerns a researcher who wants to use spatial RNA profiling in plant tissue. A usable preservation and processing workflow has not yet been established; no pilot measurements or biological findings are available. Both opportunities and all limits below are invented for the example.

<a id="grant-plan-worked-choice"></a>**Opportunity decision: establish the workflow first**

**Chosen opportunity:** A nine-month methods pilot with a maximum total request of €35,000.

**Scientific fit:** The pilot supports establishing a workflow and deciding whether it can support the planned biological study. It does not require us to promise the larger seasonal comparison within nine months.

**Information checked:** The research office confirmed applicant and host eligibility, the institutional submission route and that service charges are permitted. The facility supplied a quote and indicated capacity conditional on an award. Finance estimates €29,000 for staff time, services, consumables and the permitted institutional charge. That total includes €3,000 of institutional costs within the €35,000 cap. Existing space and equipment are institutionally supported; no equipment purchase is requested.

**Alternative:** A two-year biological project could fund a seasonal comparison, with a maximum total request of €180,000. It is also eligible and relevant. However, access to external seasonal material remains unresolved, and the workflow is untested. We cannot yet show how we would obtain and process the samples needed for that comparison.

**Decision:** Prepare the pilot application. Reconsider the larger study once sample access is agreed and we have evidence that the workflow can support the seasonal comparison.

**Condition for reconsideration:** If the facility cannot provide the required service within the proposed period, or revised costs leave essential work unfunded, revisit the pilot’s scope or choose another opportunity before completing the application.

The pilot proposal still needs to specify how the workflow will be assessed and what different results would mean for its use in the biological study.

<a id="grant-plan-contributors"></a>

## Contributors and reviewers

Identify who will lead the proposal, who will supply scientific or technical contributions, who will cost it and who is authorized to submit it. Agree what each person will provide and when. Confirm what help the research office offers; support may include interpretation of the scheme, budget checks, editing or submission, and it differs between institutions. If no such office is available, identify experienced colleagues and the person who can authorize the application rather than assume those tasks will happen [[2, p. 884]](#ref-grant-plan-consalvey2023funding).

Ask each partner what they will contribute and whether they can provide it during the proposed project. If a collaborator will supply samples or run an assay, agree the work and timing before describing that support in the application. Use [collaboration, authorship and credit](../10-mentoring-and-management/04-collaboration-authorship-and-credit.md#collaboration-chapter) to agree scientific responsibilities and credit.

Invite readers before you need the review. A reader familiar with the method can examine feasibility and missing controls; someone outside the immediate field can test whether the question, rationale and contribution are understandable. Give them the call’s criteria and ask for specific criticism, with an agreed return date. Send a complete draft early enough to discuss a major objection and revise the science or scope [[3, pp. 153–154]](#ref-grant-plan-eisenstadter2014grants).

<a id="grant-plan-schedule"></a>

## Scheduling and submission

Begin with the institution’s internal deadline and the funder’s cutoff, including the specified time zone and who must complete each submission step. Work backwards: when must the package be ready for authorization, the costs agreed and reviewers’ comments returned? Set the drafting schedule around those dates. Request quotes and support statements early enough for the people supplying them to respond. Scientific writing and costing can proceed in parallel; send changes in the proposed work to the person preparing the budget.

Choose lead times from the work and people involved. Eisenstadter suggests that two months before a deadline may not be too early to finish a draft for review, and allowing a week or two away from it can expose buried information. The institutional committee example in *Making the Right Moves* also begins at least two months before its NIH deadline. These illustrate room for thinking and revision; they are not a universal timetable [[3, pp. 153–154]](#ref-grant-plan-eisenstadter2014grants); [[1, p. 161]](#ref-grant-plan-rightmoves2006).

If time is short, check whether the necessary access, review and authorization can still be arranged. A smaller proposal may be possible if it answers a worthwhile question with resources already available. But if cutting the work removes the experiment needed to answer that question, consider a later round. Waiting delays the research and may mean missing this opportunity; weigh that cost against submitting a project that no longer answers the question or still lacks essential support.

The fictional pilot applicant has seven weeks until the funder cutoff. The research office requires the final package five working days earlier and has agreed to begin checks before then. The applicant and contributors agree the following plan; its future tasks have not yet been completed.

<a id="grant-plan-worked-plan"></a>**Application plan: review before authorization**

**Seven weeks before cutoff — choose and assign.** The applicant records the eligibility and fit checks above, confirms the current instructions with the research office and invites two readers. The facility and finance contact agree dates for support text and final costing.

**Five weeks before — make the proposal assessable.** The applicant completes a scientific outline and first full account of the workflow work, including how its performance will be assessed. Finance prepares the initial budget; the applicant sends draft access and support statements to their owners.

**Four weeks before — obtain scientific review.** A methods reader examines measurement, feasibility and risks. A colleague outside the immediate field reads for purpose and rationale against the pilot’s criteria. Both return comments within the agreed week; the applicant discusses substantial objections with them.

**Three weeks before — reconcile revisions and support.** The applicant resolves comments, revises scope where needed and sends changes to finance and the facility. Final costing and support text are agreed against the revised work.

**Two weeks before — assemble for institutional checks.** The applicant provides the narrative, budget, applicant information and required support documents to the research office. The office checks the assembled package and requests corrections before its internal deadline.

**At least five working days before — complete authorization.** The applicant delivers the corrected final package. The research office completes the required authorization and confirms who will submit.

**Target three working days before — submit and confirm.** The authorized submitter sends the application through the specified system. The applicant and submitter check validation messages and receipt, resolve correctable problems before cutoff, and retain the submitted version and confirmation.

Before submission, reconcile the assembled application with its commitments. The narrative, budget and support documents should describe the same scope, people and access. Inspect the generated application or entered fields where the system makes them available: an uploaded file does not establish that every required component has been accepted. Confirm receipt through the applicable system rather than treating a saved draft as a submission.

Retain the submitted version, its costing and the instructions used. Consider how you would proceed if funding were declined or delayed.
