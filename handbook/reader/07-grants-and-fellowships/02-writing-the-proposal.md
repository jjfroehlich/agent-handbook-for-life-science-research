# Writing the proposal

A proposal explains why a question is worth pursuing and how you plan to answer it. Give reviewers enough detail to assess the experiments and judge whether your team can carry them out. Explain what you could learn from different outcomes.

Use the current call and application instructions to determine the required sections and supporting material.

<a id="proposal-question"></a>

## Research question and significance

Put the objective near the beginning, before a detailed history of the field or description of techniques. Introduce the unresolved problem, say which part you will address, and explain why the answer matters. If a hypothesis organizes the proposal, describe how you will test it and what would count against it [[5, commandments 1–3 and 6]](#ref-proposal-elowitz2023grants); [[6, tips 2–6]](#ref-proposal-ross2021nih).

Make clear what is already known and what you propose to find out. Support claims about the field with evidence, identify hypotheses, and use future tense for planned experiments. Explain exactly what preliminary results establish. Images might demonstrate that you can measure a response without yet showing which mechanism causes it.

Significance explains why the answer matters. Innovation explains how the question or approach differs from existing work. If you propose a new instrument, explain which comparison it makes possible and why that comparison matters. A familiar method may be sufficient to answer an important unresolved question. Compare the proposal with specific prior work before calling it innovative. If you suggest a practical application, explain what further work would be needed: an experiment in a controlled system rarely establishes a therapy or field application by itself [[6, tips 4–7]](#ref-proposal-ross2021nih); [[3, pp. 883–884]](#ref-proposal-consalvey2023funding).

<a id="proposal-aims"></a>

## Aims and experiments

State what each aim will establish. “Determine whether a response depends on treatment order” gives the experiment a purpose; “perform imaging” leaves the question unanswered. Then describe the comparison and explain how the measurements and analysis will answer it. Include the controls needed to distinguish explanations and identify which samples or experimental runs provide independent replication [[2, pp. 166–167]](#ref-proposal-rightmoves2006).

Explain how the aims depend on one another. If a later experiment tests the cause of a response you have not yet established, say what must be learned before it can begin. Describe what useful work remains if that response does not appear. Aims can depend on earlier results, provided reviewers can judge the risk and the alternatives [[6, tips 9–10]](#ref-proposal-ross2021nih).

For each aim, explain what different results would mean. A precise result inconsistent with the hypothesis may answer the question. Failure to deliver the treatment or measure the response leaves the proposed test incomplete. Plan for both possibilities [[5, commandments 7–8 and 13]](#ref-proposal-elowitz2023grants).

<a id="proposal-feasibility"></a>

## Feasibility and alternatives

Separate scientific uncertainty from technical uncertainty. Whether a mechanism operates is the question; whether you can impose the intended condition, obtain the samples or measure the endpoint is a feasibility issue. Show what you have already checked. Preliminary work may establish that a method works; an access agreement documents who has agreed to provide the samples and on what terms. Where a procedure remains untested, describe how you will assess it, how much time this will take and what happens if it fails. Burchardi’s account of an economics ERC panel emphasizes access to data and a design capable of answering the question. His examples illustrate feasibility concerns, rather than requirements for every funding scheme [[1, point 4]](#ref-proposal-burchardi2022erc).

Explain what an alternative method would let you learn. If automated scoring fails, manual scoring may still allow a comparison at the final observation time. But observations made by hand may be too infrequent to test a hypothesis about response timing. State when you would switch, whether the alternative answers the same question, and whether you can complete it with the time and resources requested. If the method works and the result challenges the hypothesis, reconsider the hypothesis.

Use the applicant and environment sections to show how the work can be done. Connect your experience to the proposed tasks and identify collaborators who provide expertise you lack. Confirm access to essential facilities and distinguish agreed support from requests still awaiting an answer. These commitments should agree with the staffing and costs elsewhere in the application [[3, p. 884]](#ref-proposal-consalvey2023funding); [[2, pp. 166–169]](#ref-proposal-rightmoves2006).

<a id="proposal-excerpt"></a>**A focused proposal excerpt**

In this fictional basic-research proposal, a researcher plans to characterize seed accession S under a defined pulsed-light protocol. The researcher has seed-handling and image-scoring experience and confirmed access to programmable light chambers, but no preliminary biological result. Red/far-red order effects are already known; the proposed question concerns this accession and protocol. A real application would need literature showing why this particular characterization is still needed, calibrated treatment parameters and the sections required by the scheme.

**Objective and significance.** We will characterize accession S’s germination response to a defined red/far-red light protocol. We will determine whether exposure order changes germination timing when the dose of each exposure is held constant, and how the interval between exposures affects that comparison. We hypothesize that reversing the order produces different germination trajectories within this protocol. If changing the order or interval changes germination, that detail will need to be specified when the protocol is used for this accession. The project will not identify a receptor mechanism, establish a treatment for agricultural use or determine how other accessions respond.

**Aim 1: Determine whether exposure order changes germination.** Seeds will receive one calibrated one-hour red exposure (R) and one calibrated one-hour far-red exposure (F), in sequences R→F or F→R with a fixed dark interval. Each sequence will deliver the same dose of R and the same dose of F; temperature, hydration and seed age will be matched. All final pulses will end at the same seed age, followed by the same observation period. A dark-only group will establish germination without the pulses. We will record time to visible radicle emergence and retain the fraction that has not germinated by the observation limit. This comparison tests order under the specified doses, rather than attributing differences to total illumination.

**Aim 2: Determine how the interval between exposures affects the order comparison.** For both sequences, we will compare predefined short and long dark intervals. Compensating darkness before the first pulse will keep final-pulse timing and the subsequent observation period aligned. Doses will remain unchanged. We will estimate the order difference at each interval and compare those differences directly. A smaller difference after a longer interval would be consistent with a response that depends on the temporal separation of the pulses; it would not, by itself, identify molecular memory. This aim uses the predefined sequences and can proceed without selecting a favorable result from Aim 1. An imprecise order estimate would still limit what either aim can conclude.

**Design and feasibility.** Each chamber run will receive one complete light schedule. Schedule allocation will be randomized and balanced across chambers and run blocks; containers will be randomized across positions. Several independently sourced lots of accession S will be represented across schedules. The analysis will retain chamber and run blocks and the nesting of seeds within containers and chamber runs. Containers sharing a chamber run will not be counted as independent treatment replicates, nor will repeated runs from the same lot count as new independent lots. Before biological testing, we will verify delivered spectral doses and temperature stability and compare image scoring with manual scoring. Calibration variation and the precision needed for an informative order contrast will determine the replication plan. Our existing seed-handling and scoring experience supports this stage; reliability of this particular pulsed assay remains to be established.

**Risks and interpretation.** Poor seed viability could make an apparent lack of response uninterpretable, so each lot will have a separate viability check. If image scoring is unreliable, we will use manual scoring at planned observation times only if it can retain the timing resolution required by the aims; otherwise we will narrow the timing question or revise the design before scaling up. If dose or temperature control fails, we will repair and requalify the treatment system before testing the hypothesis. If the checks pass and the estimates exclude an order difference large enough to matter for the stated question, we will report that constraint at the tested doses and intervals. We will not call an uncertain estimate evidence that order never matters.

Treatment calibration, a justified precision target and the resulting replication plan remain unfinished. Those details would need completion before this became a submission-ready proposal.

<a id="proposal-readability"></a>

## Structure and readability

Use the required headings. Open each section with its main point, then develop the explanation. A reviewer skimming the objective and aim statements should understand the question and how you plan to answer it. Explain terms that a scientifically trained reader from another field may not know. Give the reason for an experimental comparison where you describe it, even if the background section has introduced the problem [[5, commandments 3–4 and 15]](#ref-proposal-elowitz2023grants); [[4, pp. 153–154]](#ref-proposal-eisenstadter2014grants).

A figure can help explain a complicated design. For a schedule with several interventions, show when each treatment occurs and when measurements will be taken. If one aim depends on another’s result, a diagram can show the dependency and the alternative you would pursue. Explain the comparison in the caption so readers can interpret the figure without searching the main text. Follow the scheme’s format and accessibility requirements [[5, commandments 5 and 9–11]](#ref-proposal-elowitz2023grants); [[4, p. 154]](#ref-proposal-eisenstadter2014grants).

Ask a specialist to challenge the scientific logic and a reader outside the immediate field to explain the proposal back to you. Can they say what the aims will establish and why the work is feasible? What would you learn if the expected result did not appear? Where they struggle, check whether an explanation is missing, a comparison is ambiguous or a claim lacks support. After revising, read the whole application. A change to an aim may also require changes to the figures, staffing or budget [[5, commandment 16]](#ref-proposal-elowitz2023grants); [[6, tips 16–18]](#ref-proposal-ross2021nih).
