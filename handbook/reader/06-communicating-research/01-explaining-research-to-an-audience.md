# Explaining research to an audience

The same study can call for very different explanations. A colleague may want to know whether a control distinguishes two mechanisms; someone outside the field may first need to understand why the result is surprising. Before preparing slides or writing a summary, decide who needs the explanation and what they should understand when it ends.

<a id="audience-purpose"></a>

## Start with audience and purpose

Find out who will attend and why. For an invited talk, ask the host which departments were invited and what the audience knows about your topic. A room of biologists is not necessarily one audience: some may know the biological problem but not your methods, while others know the methods but not the system. [[1]](#ref-audience-meredith2010); [[3]](#ref-audience-rubenson2018).

Purpose changes the explanation, too. A departmental seminar might introduce an unfamiliar discovery; a meeting with potential collaborators might need to show where their contribution could help solve an open problem. In the second setting, spend more time on the unresolved work and the help you are seeking. Write the intended outcome in one sentence before selecting material. [[1]](#ref-audience-meredith2010).

With a mixed audience, everyone needs access to the question and main finding, even if only some listeners need the technical detail that follows. Introduce an unfamiliar concept when it becomes necessary to understand the next step. A long preliminary glossary asks listeners to remember terms before they know what the terms are for. [[2]](#ref-audience-lefever2012); [[3]](#ref-audience-rubenson2018).

<a id="audience-message"></a>

## Choose the message and the evidence

Before choosing results, try stating the main finding in a sentence. “Bacterial antiviral defence” names a subject; “Brake uses an RNA template to produce a gene encoding an antiviral protein” makes a claim the explanation can develop. A longer talk may need supporting messages for different parts of that finding, but they should still lead toward the central claim. [[3]](#ref-audience-rubenson2018).

That sentence gives you a test for each result: does it support the claim, or rule out a serious alternative? A colleague may need the control that separates two mechanisms, though not every preliminary experiment that led to it. Explain the comparison, measurement and distinguishing test whenever they determine what can be inferred. Procedural detail can wait for discussion or the accompanying paper. [[1]](#ref-audience-meredith2010).

The harder decision may be what to leave out. An additional result that needs several new concepts yet adds little to the conclusion can wait for another occasion. A result that changes the conclusion belongs in the explanation, even if something else must be shortened. For an unfinished study, explain what the current result shows and what question it leaves open. [[3]](#ref-audience-rubenson2018); [[5]](#ref-audience-yanai2020).

<a id="audience-explanation"></a>

## Organize the explanation

Begin with a question or observation listeners can understand, then give enough context to show what needs explaining. For unfamiliar work, that may mean introducing the biological process or experimental comparison before stating the finding. [[2]](#ref-audience-lefever2012).

When the finding emerges through several tests, let each result answer a question and raise the next one. In Brake, identifying a coding sequence in the DNA does not by itself show that the encoded protein causes defence. The next question is whether defence survives when translation is blocked but DNA production continues. Framing that comparison before its result lets listeners see why the experiment matters. [[4]](#ref-audience-kirchoff2021).

For a short update or an audience already familiar with the question, give the conclusion first and work through its support. Successive questions are more helpful when listeners need to see how a mechanism was resolved. In either order, the reasoning should determine the sequence; the dates on which experiments were performed need not. [[1]](#ref-audience-meredith2010); [[4]](#ref-audience-kirchoff2021).

Some relationships are easier to follow when they can be seen. If listeners must keep track of several intermediates or transformations, a simple schematic can show how one leads to another. Introduce its objects and arrows while explaining them, using the same names in speech and in the diagram. [Specialized displays and explanatory diagrams](../03-data-visualization/05-specialized-displays-and-explanatory-diagrams.md#specialist-chapter) covers its design. [[6]](#ref-audience-wong2011); [[7]](#ref-audience-jamborabstracts2024).

Return to the opening question at the end. Give the answer and explain what remains unresolved. If the audience is interested in an application, say what would still need to be tested before the result could be used that way. [[1]](#ref-audience-meredith2010).

<a id="audience-language"></a>

## Adapt detail and language

Explain an unfamiliar technical term when you introduce it: “the enzyme that copies RNA into DNA, a reverse transcriptase.” Specialists can usually start with “reverse transcriptase.” Then use the same name throughout, so listeners do not have to work out whether different labels refer to the same thing. [[1]](#ref-audience-meredith2010); [[2]](#ref-audience-lefever2012).

Vocabulary is only one part of the adjustment. Researchers studying bacterial defence may need the construct comparison and the timing of DNA production. Biologists outside that field may first need to know why producing a gene from RNA is the finding. Both still need the experiment that supports the conclusion. The two explanations below use the fictional Brake study, inspired by Tang and colleagues (2024) [[8]](#ref-audience-tang2024).

<a id="audience-brake-versions"></a>**Example: explaining the finding to two audiences**

**For researchers studying bacterial defence**

Brake posed a puzzle. Its locus has a reverse transcriptase and a conserved RNA template, but no conventional effector gene. The DNA product contains repeated units, with a coding sequence spanning their junctions. We detect single-stranded DNA before infection; during infection, we also detect the double-stranded product and the Brake protein.

But is the protein responsible for defence? In a translation-disrupted construct, DNA production continues, yet growth arrest and restriction of phage A disappear. Expressing the protein separately restores both. That supports an RNA-templated route to a protein effector; it does not identify the protein’s molecular target. Brake also restricts phage B, while phage C remains productive.

**For biologists outside the field**

Some bacteria use proteins to restrict viruses called phages. Brake was puzzling because we could not find a conventional gene for its defence protein. Instead, it copies an RNA template into DNA that carries the instructions for that protein.

Was making the DNA enough for defence, or did the system also need the protein? We blocked production of the protein, but the DNA was still made. The bacteria no longer stopped growing, and phage A multiplied again. Supplying the protein separately restored growth arrest and restricted phage multiplication. So Brake builds a functional defence gene from RNA. It restricted two of the three phages we tested; the third still multiplied. We do not yet know what the protein acts on.

The specialist version can name the DNA intermediates, coding junctions and construct comparison because its listeners can use those details. The broader version asks whether DNA production alone is enough, then gives the same decisive comparison in ordinary language.

An analogy can help listeners grasp an unfamiliar relationship, provided the correspondence is clear: which part of the familiar example stands for which object or process? Keep it brief, then return to the actual system for the evidence. If the analogy implies intention or a mechanism the study has not established, use another explanation. [[2, pp. 83–92]](#ref-audience-lefever2012); [[5]](#ref-audience-yanai2020).

<a id="audience-test"></a>

## Test and refine the explanation

Try the explanation with someone whose background resembles the intended audience’s. Ask them to summarize the finding and explain how the main experiment supports it. Where did they need more background, or lose the connection between results? [[1]](#ref-audience-meredith2010); [[2]](#ref-audience-lefever2012).

Their response tells you what to revise. Someone who knows the terms but cannot explain why the result supports the conclusion needs a clearer comparison or transition. Someone who cannot follow the comparison may need a concept introduced earlier. If the main point disappears among details, remove a secondary result and try again.

Use a short outline to plan the explanation or review its order. The outline below connects the broader Brake version’s question, evidence and answer before any slides are designed.

<a id="audience-outline"></a>**Example: an explanation outline for a biology seminar**

**Purpose:** explain how an RNA template can supply an antiviral gene to biologists unfamiliar with this defence pathway.

1. **The puzzle:** Brake restricts phage propagation, yet its annotated region has no conventional effector gene. Introduce the reverse transcriptase and adjacent RNA template as the clues to where a gene might come from.

2. **Where the gene comes from:** show how repeated DNA contains a coding sequence. Connect the template and junction analysis to the observations of DNA and protein production.

3. **What causes defence:** compare intact Brake with the translation-disrupted construct. The continued production of DNA makes the loss of defence informative; separate protein expression then restores growth arrest and restriction.

4. **What this establishes:** the RNA template supplies a gene encoding the defence protein. Close with the tested phage range and the protein’s unresolved molecular target.

Keep more detailed results available for questions about the experiments in the outline.
