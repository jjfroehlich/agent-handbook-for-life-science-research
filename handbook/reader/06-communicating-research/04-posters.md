# Posters

A poster is read in fragments. Someone may notice the title while walking past, approach the central result, ask a question, then leave with a contact or a link.

<a id="poster-content"></a>

## Choose the content

Decide what you want to discuss: a finding, an unresolved problem or a possible collaboration. Choose content for the people you expect to meet. A poster that will also circulate afterward needs enough explanation to be read without you. [[1]](#ref-poster-broadposter).

Write the main finding as a sentence, then choose the evidence readers need to assess it. Give the decisive comparison the largest area. Include enough background to explain the question and enough methods to interpret the result. Put secondary analyses and procedural detail in a handout or linked material unless they are needed to evaluate the central finding. [[2, pp. 2–3]](#ref-poster-doumont2017); [[3]](#ref-poster-moralesgarcia2022).

Identify the presenter, affiliation and contact details. Place them where they are easy to find but do not displace the claim or evidence. [[2, pp. 4, 6]](#ref-poster-doumont2017).

Adapt manuscript figures for the poster: select the relevant panels, enlarge labels and annotate the key comparison. Retain the controls and uncertainty needed to interpret the result. [[3]](#ref-poster-moralesgarcia2022).

<a id="poster-arrange"></a>

## Arrange the poster

Sketch the reading order before refining fonts or colors. Make the main finding prominent, place related text and figures together, and separate sections with whitespace. Align blocks along a few consistent edges. Use headings or numbers where the sequence would otherwise be unclear. [[2, pp. 3–4]](#ref-poster-doumont2017); [[3]](#ref-poster-moralesgarcia2022).

The fictional Brake study below is inspired by Tang and colleagues (2024) [[5]](#ref-poster-tang2024).

Figure 1: This focused poster portion gives most of its space to the quantitative restriction result, then uses a compact test strip to separate the DNA intermediate from the encoded protein. Original teaching figure.

The quantitative result occupies the largest region. The smaller translation test answers the next mechanistic question without competing with the main plot.

When several results need to be compared, place their evidence blocks beside one another and give each a heading that states its local finding.

Use headings that state the finding where possible: “Translation is required for phage restriction” tells a visitor more than “Results.” Use consistent type sizes for headings and body text, and align related blocks. Color can reinforce a grouping or emphasis, but readers should also be able to identify conditions from labels or symbols. [[3]](#ref-poster-moralesgarcia2022); [[2, p. 5]](#ref-poster-doumont2017).

<a id="poster-readability"></a>

## Print and digital readability

Check the conference specification before committing to orientation, dimensions, logos, file format or display method. For a conventional A0 print poster, make an early A4 reduction and read it at normal page distance; then inspect the full-size poster in the expected light from roughly 0.5 to 1.5 metres. In both checks, the title, headings, axes, annotations and contact details must be legible. Use the production and export checks in [Preparing figures for publication](../03-data-visualization/06-preparing-figures-for-publication.md#layout-chapter). [[2, pp. 2, 6]](#ref-poster-doumont2017); [[3]](#ref-poster-moralesgarcia2022).

For a digital poster, keep an overview visible so visitors can identify the topic and main finding even if they arrive halfway through an animation or explanation. Check which interactive features the conference platform supports and whether visitors can operate them. An animation can explain a process; a zoomable image can reveal detail. Neither helps if visitors cannot find the comparison or return to the overview. [[4]](#ref-poster-nsedigitalposter).

<a id="poster-conversation"></a>

## Use the poster in conversation

Prepare a short orientation: the question, the finding and the decisive evidence. Give it when someone asks what the poster is about, then pause. For the model above: “Brake restricts phage propagation, but its locus has no conventional effector gene. The large plot compares intact Brake with an empty vector and an inactive reverse transcriptase. Infectious phage yield is much lower with intact Brake than with either control. Is the protein encoded by Brake’s DNA needed for defence? We blocked protein production but still detected the DNA. Growth arrest and phage restriction disappeared, and both returned when we supplied the protein separately.” Their next question tells you whether to clarify the method, discuss a limitation or offer deeper material. [[1]](#ref-poster-broadposter); [[2, p. 3]](#ref-poster-doumont2017).

Rehearse with the poster in front of you. For a visitor unfamiliar with the study, start at the question; for a specialist, start at the finding. Point to the relevant evidence as you discuss it. If the conversation becomes lengthy, invite others to join or arrange to continue later. [[2, p. 3]](#ref-poster-doumont2017). See [Delivering a talk and answering questions](03-delivering-a-talk-and-answering-questions.md#delivery-chapter) for rehearsal and difficult questions.
